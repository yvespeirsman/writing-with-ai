The Lighthouse Keeper's Last Letter

Every morning for forty-one years, Edwin Marsh had climbed the one hundred and twelve iron steps of the Carrow Point lighthouse, trimmed the wick, polished the lens, and wound the great clockwork that turned the light. He had done it through gales that shook the glass in its frame, through fog so thick he could not see the rail beneath his hands, and through the long, silent winter after his wife, Clara, died.

The island had only four houses left now, and the ferry came twice a week when the weather allowed. The mainland had been calling the lighthouse obsolete for a decade. Ships had satellites and radar and computers that could tell a captain where he was down to the width of a hair. Edwin knew this. He read the notices the ferryman brought. But he kept the light burning anyway, because the light was not really for the ships. Not anymore. It was for the handful of fishermen still out past the shoals, and for himself, because a man needs something to climb toward each night.

In October, a letter arrived that was not a notice.

The envelope was cream-colored and smelled faintly of lavender. The handwriting was looping and familiar, and for a long moment Edwin simply held it, unable to open it. It was addressed to him in Clara's hand.

He sat down on the kitchen step to read it.

*My dearest Edwin,*

*If you are reading this, then the ferryman has done his job, and I have finally done mine, which was to be the one who made the arrangements. Forgive me for the small deception. You always said I could never keep a secret, and I suppose I proved you wrong at the very end.*

*I know how hard the winters have been for you. I know you still climb those stairs every night and tell yourself it's for the fishermen. It isn't, my love. It's for me. You have always kept a light burning for the people you cannot reach, and I have loved you for it. But I need you to stop now.*

*Go down to the cove on the east side, where we used to swim when the children were small. There is a rowboat there, and in it a tin box. Inside you will find the logbook from our first year on the point, when we were young and foolish and certain we would change the world. Read it. Then row out to the shoals, and let the light go dark for one night. Just one. The sea will not notice, and neither will the ships. But you may find that you can finally sleep.*

*I will be waiting, Edwin, but not in the lighthouse. Let yourself come down from the tower.*

*All my love, always,*
*Clara*

Edwin read the letter three times. Then he laughed, a short surprised sound that echoed off the stone walls, and then he was crying in a way he had not allowed himself since the funeral.

The cove was exactly as she had described it. The rowboat sat above the tide line, its paint faded to the color of old bone. Beneath the forward seat, wrapped in an oilcloth gone stiff with age, he found the tin box. Inside was the logbook, its pages swollen and brown at the edges. The first entry was in his own hand, dated the spring they had come to the island.

*Light lit at dusk. Clara says the first ship she ever saw come into harbor will be the one that remembers us. I told her that was sentimental nonsense. She laughed and said I'd see.*

He turned the pages slowly. Forty-one years of weather, of tides, of small domestic notes. *Clara brought soup to the Hanlon boys, the fever is breaking. Storm from the northwest, lens cracked on the east pane, fixed it. Clara sang while she mended the nets. Child's first step on the cove sand, Maeve, she's four. Clara sick today, I'll climb alone. Clara better.* The entries grew sparser in the later years, and then the last page was in her handwriting, faint and shaky: *Edwin, keep the light for the ones still out there, but remember the ones who are home.*

Edwin closed the logbook. The sun was lowering over the water, turning the sea to hammered copper.

He sat in the rowboat for a long time. Then he pushed off from the shore, took up the oars, and rowed out toward the shoals, where the fishermen would be coming home soon, and where for one night, for the first time in forty-one years, the light at Carrow Point would stay dark.

When he rowed back to the cove under a sky full of stars, he looked up at the tower and saw nothing but black stone against the dark. It was strange, and it was quiet, and for the first time since October, he felt like he could breathe.

He walked up the cove path to the cottage, lit the lamp in the kitchen window, and for the first time in years, went to bed without climbing the stairs at all.
