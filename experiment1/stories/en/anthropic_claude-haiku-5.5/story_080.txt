The Lighthouse Keeper's Last Letter

Every morning for forty-one years, Elias Brandt had climbed the one hundred and twelve iron steps of the Carrow Point lighthouse, wiped the salt from the great lens, and checked the wick. He had never once missed a night. Not during the storm of '82, when the lantern room shook like a struck bell, and not the winter his wife, Marta, was buried in the frozen churchyard two miles inland.

Now the Coast Authority wanted to automate the light. A solar panel and a box of circuitry would do his job, they said, and do it cheaper. The final night of his service was a Thursday in late October, and the wind had already begun to sing through the rigging of the fishing boats below.

Elias didn't mind the machine so much. What troubled him was the boy.

Teodor Vance, nineteen, had been sent from the mainland office to "learn the systems" before the handover. He was thin and eager, with a notebook full of questions and a habit of checking his phone for signal that didn't exist out here. He had asked Elias, on the first day, why he didn't just let the light run itself.

"Because a light that runs itself," Elias had said, "is only ever half watched."

Teodor hadn't understood. Elias hadn't expected him to.

That evening, as the sun dropped red behind the headland, Elias sat at the small iron desk in the keeper's quarters and wrote a letter. He wrote it slowly, the way he did everything now that his hands had begun to ache in the cold.

*To whoever reads the logbook next,*

*I have kept this light for forty-one years. Some nights it was the only thing I was sure of. I have watched ships come home because of it, and I have watched a few not come home, and I have never been able to tell the difference from the shore, only from the sea's silence afterward.*

*Do not think the lamp only guides ships. It also tells the land that someone is still awake. My wife used to say that from her kitchen window she could see the sweep of the beam crossing her ceiling, and that it made her feel the world had a watchman. I never told her how much I needed to be that watchman for her, even from two miles away.*

*So when you are tempted to call the machine enough, remember that a light is a promise that someone will show up. Machines keep promises too, I suppose. They just have no one to miss.*

He paused, then added one more line.

*Teodor, if you read this, the logbook's back page has a pencil under the spine. Check the glass on the northwest panel before dark. It cracks in the cold, and nobody will notice until the beam goes crooked.*

He sealed the letter, tucked it into the logbook, and went up the stairs for the last time as keeper.

Teodor was already in the lantern room, fiddling with a tablet. Elias set down his thermos, took the cloth from its hook, and began to polish the lens with the slow, careful circles he had repeated ten thousand times.

"You don't have to do that," Teodor said. "The system does it."

"I know," said Elias. "Watch the northwest panel. It cracks."

The boy looked, saw the hairline fracture running like a silver thread through the glass, and his mouth fell open. He reached for the pencil under the logbook's spine, and found it.

Elias lit the lamp at dusk, the way he always had. The beam swept out over the black water, across the fishing boats and the rocks and the long dark miles of coast, and somewhere inland a kitchen window, long since sold, went briefly gold.

Then, for the first time in forty-one years, he let someone else keep watch.
