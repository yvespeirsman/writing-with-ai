The Lighthouse Keeper's Last Letter

For forty-one years, Margaret Tull climbed the one hundred and twelve iron steps of the Carrow Point lighthouse every evening at dusk, and she never once got used to the cold of the lamp room.

It was a small, round room of glass and brass, and in winter the wind found every seam in the panes. But it was hers. She knew the rhythm of the great lens the way some women knew the rhythm of their own breathing: the slow turn, the brief dark, the bright sweep across the water that told the fishing boats where the rocks were and where home was.

The island had lost most of its people by the time she took the post. The young ones went to the cities, to factories and offices and lives with electric light on every hour of the night. Margaret stayed. She had married a fisherman named Tom Tull, who had drowned the second winter of their marriage, and there had seemed no reason to leave a place that still held him.

So she kept the light.

In the spring of her forty-second year, the mainland sent word that the lighthouse would be automated. A machine would do her work. Her post would end at the close of the season.

She received the letter on a grey morning and read it twice at the kitchen table, then folded it into quarters and put it in the drawer with the birth certificates and the recipes.

That evening she climbed the steps as always. The lens turned. The sea below went from silver to violet to black.

A knock came at the lamp room door, though no one had ever knocked there before. Margaret opened it to find a boy of about ten, soaked to the skin, holding a dripping lantern.

"My father's boat," he said, breathless. "It's out past the shoals. The engine quit. I saw your light, but I couldn't tell if it was... I didn't know if it was still working."

Margaret looked past him at the sea. Far out, a tiny speck of yellow was bobbing where the light could barely reach.

"It's working," she said. "Come in. Stand here by me and watch the glass. When the light passes over the boat, I'll flash the signal, and you wave your lantern. Can you do that?"

He nodded, wide-eyed.

They stood together in the cold room for most of the night. Each time the beam swept round, Margaret held it an extra moment on the water's edge, and the boy waved his lantern like a flag, and somewhere out in the dark a second light answered.

At first light, the coast guard cutter found them: a fisherman, cold and furious and grateful, and his son asleep against the lighthouse keeper's coat.

The mainland's automation crew arrived three weeks later. They inspected the lens, nodded at the wiring, and said the new system would be flawless.

Margaret didn't argue. She only asked to be the one to switch it on.

On her last evening, she climbed the one hundred and twelve steps for the final time. She set her hand on the cold brass rail and looked out at the sea, which had taken her husband and given back so many others.

Then she turned the lamp to life, and watched its light go out across the water, steady and bright, for the last time under her care.
