The Lighthouse Keeper's Last Letter

For forty-one years, Ada Morrow climbed the one hundred and twelve iron steps of the Pellham Point lighthouse every evening at dusk, lit the great lamp, and turned its brass crank until the beam swept out across the water. Ships knew her light. Fishermen steered by it. Her husband, Thomas, used to say the lighthouse was the only thing on the coast that never once asked for anything in return.

Thomas had been gone six winters now. The sea took him on a grey morning in February, when his skiff drifted off its moorings in a squall and was found a week later, upturned on the rocks at Gallow's Cove. Ada had not cried at the funeral. She had cried, instead, at odd moments since: over a burnt pan, over a song on the wireless, over the sight of his boots still standing by the back door as if he might step into them any minute.

The island's post came once a fortnight, by a rowboat driven by young Eli Crane, who was seventeen and freckled and talked too much. On the first Tuesday of October, he handed her a thin envelope with no return address and a stamp from a city she had never visited.

"Didn't know if I should bring it up," Eli said, shuffling his feet on the landing. "Looked important."

Ada took it inside, set the kettle on, and sat at the kitchen table for a long time before she opened it.

The handwriting was Thomas's. She would have known it anywhere: the looping T, the way he crossed his sevens.

*Ada,*

*If you're reading this, then the post office in Harrow Bay did their job, which means I did mine, which means I've finally gotten around to the thing I should have said thirty years ago.*

*I was never out on the water the morning I died. I'd left the boat in May, after the fight with the harbor board, and you never knew because I didn't want you to worry. I took the job in Portsmouth. I worked at a shipping yard. I sent money every month through Dale Hawley, who I trusted to keep my secret. I have been living a quieter life than I let you believe, and I am sorry for the lie.*

*I am not writing to explain myself fully. There isn't room, and some of it is shameful. I am writing because I found out last spring I was sick, and I did not want to go the way I was going, by pretending.*

*The thing I want you to know is this: the years I spent on that island with you were the only years I was ever entirely myself. Every night you climbed those stairs, I was proud to be the man who made the light possible. I never once wanted to be anywhere else.*

*The key to the boathouse loft is under the third stone by the north wall. There's a box there. Open it when you're ready. It is not money, and it is not a confession. It is something I should have given you a long time ago.*

*I love you. I always did. I am sorry it took me so long to be brave enough to say it plainly.*

*T.*

Ada read it three times. Then she folded it, set it on the table, and sat with her hands flat on either side of it while the kettle began to scream and then fell silent.

She did not sleep that night. At first light she walked down to the north wall in her wool coat and found the third stone loose in the grass, as if someone had been waiting for it to be moved. Beneath it lay a tin box, rusted at the corners, wrapped in oilcloth.

Inside were three things: a brass key she didn't recognize, a bundle of letters tied with twine, and a photograph of a young woman with a bright, stubborn face standing beside a little girl in a yellow dress. On the back, in Thomas's looping hand, was written: *Mary and Lily, 1961.*

Ada stared at it for a long time. Then she untied the twine.

The letters were dated across three decades. Some were from a woman named Mary Lacey. Some were from Thomas. They spoke of a daughter, of money, of a love that had begun long before Ada had ever seen the light at Pellham Point, and of a choice that had been made, and remade, and made again.

The last letter was dated the previous spring. It was short.

*Tom, Lily is asking for you. She's twenty-nine now and she has your stubbornness. Come if you can. She deserves to know her father before it's too late for both of you.*

Ada folded the letter back along its creases. Out past the rocks, the morning tide was coming in, steady and indifferent, the way it always had. She thought of the one hundred and twelve steps, the lamp she had kept burning for forty-one years, the light that had guided so many people safely home.

Then she stood, brushed the grass from her coat, and walked back up the path toward the house, where she sat down at the kitchen table and began, slowly, to write a reply.
