The Lighthouse Keeper's Last Letter

For forty-one years, Eleanor Vance climbed the one hundred and twelve iron steps of the Carrow Point lighthouse every evening at dusk. She trimmed the wick, polished the great lens, and turned the crank that set the mechanism in motion. Then she watched the beam sweep across the black water, a slow, steady promise to every ship out there that someone was still awake.

Her husband, Thomas, had taken the post first. He had loved the light the way some men love the sea itself, and when his heart gave out one winter, the Board of Lights offered Eleanor the position out of pity more than policy. She accepted before they could change their minds. The light had been his, and she was not ready to let it go dark.

The island had a population of nineteen when she arrived and six when she finally left. Children grew up and went to the mainland for school and did not come back. The general store closed. The ferry came twice a week, then once. Eleanor kept to her routines: the logbook entries, the weather notes, the careful recording of every passing vessel in a neat, slanting hand.

It was in the logbook that she found the letter.

It had been tucked between the pages of an entry from 1987, a sheet of thin blue paper folded into a small square, the ink faded to the color of weak tea. She almost didn't open it. Lighthouse keepers found all sorts of things over the years: love notes, grocery lists, once a pressed flower. But the handwriting on the outside made her pause. It was Thomas's.

*For whoever keeps the light after me.*

She sat down on the cold stone floor of the lamp room and read.

*I don't know who you are, but I know the work. Some nights the beam will feel like the only honest thing in the world, and some nights it will feel like shouting into a storm nobody hears. Both are true. Keep going anyway. Ships are out there, and they are counting on a light they will never once think about. That is the whole job, and it is enough.*

*If you ever find yourself wondering whether anyone notices, look at the logbook. Every ship you log is a life you helped bring home. You won't get a parade. You'll get the quiet satisfaction of a morning where nothing went wrong, which is the best kind of morning there is.*

*Eat something hot before the watch. Talk to the gulls if you must. And don't let the Board tell you the light is obsolete until you've seen a fog bank roll in with a freighter somewhere past the shoals.*

*—T.*

Eleanor laughed, and then, to her surprise, she cried, the way you cry when a stranger says the exact thing you needed and you didn't know you needed it.

That night a fog rolled in thick and silver. At ten past eleven, she heard the low, mournful horn of a freighter somewhere to the east, searching. She climbed the one hundred and twelve steps, lit the lamp, and turned the lens. The beam swept out across the water, steady and patient, and after a long while the horn answered, closer now, and then closer still, until a dark shape emerged from the fog, moving carefully along the channel toward the harbor.

She wrote it in the logbook: *11:10 p.m. Freighter, northbound, identified by horn. Passed safely at 11:52. Visibility poor. Light held.*

Then, after a moment, she added one more line beneath it, in her own careful hand:

*Still here. Still counting.*

She folded Thomas's letter back into its square, tucked it in the logbook beside the entry, and went down to make herself something hot to eat.
