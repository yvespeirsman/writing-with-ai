The Lighthouse Keeper's Last Letter

For forty-one years, Eleanor Vance climbed the one hundred and twelve iron steps of the Carrow Point lighthouse every evening at dusk. She trimmed the wick, wound the clockwork that turned the great lens, and watched the beam sweep across the black water like a patient hand searching for something lost.

The island had only nineteen residents when she arrived as a young woman, and by the year she turned seventy, there were four. The ferry came on Tuesdays now, when it came at all. The schoolhouse had closed. The church bell hung silent, its rope frayed and gray. Still, the light burned, because the charts still showed Carrow Point, and ships still needed to know where the rocks were.

Eleanor did not mind the solitude as much as people assumed. She had her routines: tea at five, the crossword at six, the logbook at nine, in which she recorded weather, tides, and the occasional passing vessel in a looping, careful hand. Her husband, Thomas, had kept the light with her for twenty years before the sea took him during a November storm. She still set two cups out some mornings before remembering.

One gray afternoon in October, a letter arrived with the supply boat. The envelope was addressed in handwriting she did not recognize, and the postmark was from a city she had left decades ago. Inside was a single sheet.

*Dear Mrs. Vance,*

*You won't remember me. I was the boy who rowed out in the fog the winter of 1979, the one your light pulled through the rocks when my father's engine failed. You climbed down those steps, wrapped me in your husband's coat, and told me to keep my eyes on the beam and never on the water. I have done that ever since. I became a pilot. I have flown over that island more times than I can count, and every time I see the light, I think of you.*

*I wanted you to know that the light was never just for ships. Thank you for keeping it.*

*With gratitude,*
*Daniel Ross*

Eleanor read the letter three times. Then she folded it carefully and set it on the windowsill beside the logbook.

That evening, she climbed the one hundred and twelve steps, slower than she used to, stopping twice to rest her knees. At the top, the lens glowed amber in the dusk. She wound the clockwork, trimmed the wick, and watched the first beam sweep out across the water.

Somewhere far off, she imagined, a plane was passing through the dark, and a man was looking down at a small point of light and remembering a night when someone had stayed awake for him.

She opened the logbook and wrote, in her steady hand: *Light lit. Weather clear. Someone is still watching.*

Then she sat down in the lamp room, wrapped her shawl tighter, and kept watch until morning.
