**The Clockmaker's Last Repair**

In the town of Haverly, where the streets curved like dropped ribbon, there lived an old clockmaker named Elias Thorne. His shop sat at the bottom of Mill Lane, wedged between a bakery and a shop that sold only umbrellas. Inside, a hundred clocks ticked in a hundred different rhythms, and together they made a sound like rain on a tin roof.

One gray October morning, a girl of about nine pushed open the door. The bell above it jingled, and she stood there dripping, holding a small brass pocket watch in both hands as if it might escape.

"It stopped," she said. "My grandmother's. She died in the spring, and I've been winding it every night, but this morning it just... stopped."

Elias took his glasses off and wiped them on his apron. "What's your name?"

"Nell."

"Well, Nell, let's have a look."

He set the watch on his workbench beneath the lamp and opened its case with a tiny silver key. Inside, the gears lay still, dusty and intricate. He bent close, his breath slow, and began to work.

Nell watched him. "Can you fix it?"

"Probably," Elias said. "Clocks are mostly patience. Most things that stop can be started again, if someone knows where the trouble is."

"What's the trouble?"

He tilted the movement toward the light. "A hair-thin spring, worn out. It's been running down for a long time, I'd guess. Your grandmother must have loved it very much to keep it going for so many years."

Nell was quiet. Then: "She said time was a thing you could hold if you were careful."

Elias smiled faintly. "She sounds like she was a wise woman."

"She was a baker." Nell's voice wobbled. "She used to say the best bread needed waiting. I didn't understand that until she was gone and I had to wait for everything."

Elias paused, his tweezers hovering over the mechanism. For a while the only sound was the shop's hundred clocks, ticking their many separate times.

"Do you know what I think?" he said at last. "I think your grandmother didn't just wind this watch. She wound you up, too. Every time she showed you how to knead dough or tell a good story, she was putting a little spring in your days. That kind of winding doesn't wear out."

Nell sniffed. "Will it work again?"

"Give me an hour."

He worked for most of it, replacing the spring with a new one he cut and shaped by hand, polishing each tooth of the gears until they gleamed. Nell sat on a stool with her chin in her hands and watched the rain streak the window. At one point she fell asleep, and Elias draped his old wool cardigan over her shoulders without a word.

When she woke, the lamp had been turned low, and the watch lay on a folded cloth in front of her. Its hands were moving. Tick. Tick. Tick.

"It's running," she whispered.

"It is." Elias pressed the watch gently into her palm. "Now, the trick is to wind it a little each day. Not too tight, not too loose. Enough."

Nell closed her fingers around it, feeling the faint, steady pulse through the brass. "Thank you, Mr. Thorne."

"Come back if it ever gets tired. I'll always have a spring or two."

She pulled his cardigan off, folded it neatly, and handed it back. At the door she turned. "Do you have a grandchild?"

Elias looked at the hundred clocks around him, then back at her.

"Not yet," he said. "But I'm hoping someone will come in here one day and need me to show them how."

The bell jingled as she stepped out into the rain, the watch ticking softly in her pocket, keeping time for both of them.
