The Lighthouse Keeper's Last Letter

The storm came in on a Tuesday, the way storms always seemed to in the autumn, with no warning but a change in the smell of the air. Marguerite Vale felt it in her knees before she noticed it in the sky. She had kept the Ardent Point light for thirty-one years, and her body had become a second barometer she trusted more than the government forecasts.

She climbed the one hundred and twelve iron steps to the lantern room, counting them out of habit, and found the great lens already turning on its own clockwork. It needed winding every four hours. Her late husband, Thomas, had taught her the rhythm of it the summer they arrived, when he still believed the light would be a grand adventure and not the slow sentence it became.

Below, the Atlantic was gathering itself into gray mountains.

At the base of the tower, the supply boat had not come in three weeks. The mainland had grown quiet, the radio spitting only static and the occasional fragment of a voice she could not make out. She had stopped trying to call anyone after the second week. There was enough tinned food for two more months, and enough oil for the lamp for longer than that. The light would keep burning. That had always been the point.

She sat at the small wooden table beside the stove and pulled a sheet of paper from the drawer. For years she had kept a logbook, recording wind and weather, the passage of ships, the births and deaths of gulls on the rocks below. But tonight she wanted to write to someone.

*To whoever finds this,*

*My name is Marguerite Vale. I am the keeper of the Ardent Point light. If you are reading this, the tower is still standing, and perhaps I am not.*

*I want you to know that this light was never only about ships. Ships are what the government wrote in its reports, and what the insurance men cared about. But on the long nights I came to understand that the light was a promise, a small and stubborn sentence spoken into the dark: someone is still here. Someone is still awake. Come toward this and you will not be alone with the water.*

*Thomas used to say that every lighthouse is a letter written by people who will never meet the ones they are writing to. The fishermen who rounded the point in fog. The ferry captains. The child who stood on the cliffs at dusk and wondered where the boats went. We never knew you. We only hoped you were getting home.*

The wind hit the glass like a thrown stone. Marguerite waited until the tower stopped trembling, then continued.

*I have made mistakes. I left my sister unanswered for eleven years over a quarrel about a house we never should have argued about. I did not visit Thomas's grave for the first two years after he died because I could not bear the walk. I was afraid of the quiet, so I filled my days with the sound of the lens and the tick of the clock, and I called that duty when it was partly cowardice.*

*But I also watched a hundred and six sunrises come up red over the water from this window. I saw a whale breach so close the spray reached the glass. I delivered a baby once, in the winter of the great freeze, when the supply boat could not reach us and the fishing family's cottage was a mile down the rocks. I learned that a person can be more capable than they believed, and that the sea, for all its violence, is also honest. It does not pretend.*

She paused, listening. Somewhere below, a shutter banged. The lens turned, throwing its long white arm across the churning water, and she thought of the ships she had never seen again, the ones that had passed safely into harbor and forgotten her entirely. That had never bothered her. It was the nature of the work. You kept the light so that others could arrive, and you did not need to be thanked.

*If the storm has taken this station,* she wrote, *then let this letter be the last thing the light says. Not warning. Not fear. Only this: you were worth guiding. Whoever you are, wherever you were going, I hoped you reached it.*

*With respect and with love,*
*Marguerite Vale*
*Keeper, Ardent Point*

She folded the letter carefully, slid it into an oilcloth envelope, and tucked it into the brass box beside the stove, the one meant for logs and the occasional lost message. Then she stood, stretched her aching back, and climbed the one hundred and twelve steps once more.

At the top, she wound the clockwork. The great lens began to turn faster, its beam sweeping out over the storm, bright and steady against the dark.

Marguerite pulled her coat tight, sat down beside the glass, and kept watch until morning.
