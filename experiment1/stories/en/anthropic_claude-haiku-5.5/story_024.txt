The Lighthouse Keeper's Last Letter

Every morning for forty-one years, Eleanor Vance had climbed the one hundred and twelve iron steps of the Marrow Point lighthouse, trimmed the wick, polished the great lens, and watched the gray Atlantic swallow the sun. She knew the sound the lamp made when it turned over at dusk. She knew which gulls would nest on the north rail and which would fight for the south. She knew the sea the way some people know a difficult relative: thoroughly, warily, and with a grudging affection.

The mainland had stopped sending supply boats in October. The new automated light at Cragmoor Bay had made Marrow Point obsolete, and the Coast Guard had been polite about it, sending a letter that used the word "transition" four times. Eleanor had read it once, folded it into quarters, and tucked it behind the clock in the kitchen. The clock had stopped in 1987. She never fixed it. She liked having one thing in the house that agreed with her about time.

On the last night of her post, a storm rolled in from the southwest, the kind that sounded like a freight train circling the tower. Eleanor climbed the steps anyway. The lamp was still hers to keep lit until midnight, by the terms of the decommissioning, and she had never once in forty-one years left a light dark before its hour.

At the top, the lens hummed. Rain hammered the glass. And through the spray, far out on the black water, she saw a small light bobbing—a fishing boat, too far out, too careless, heading straight for the reef called the Teeth.

Eleanor did not hesitate. She ran the sequence she had memorized decades ago: the manual shutter, the signal lamp, the three long flashes and two short that meant *turn back, danger ahead*. Her hands were steady. Her heart was not, but that had never mattered much.

The boat turned. Slowly, then all at once, it swung north and began to fight its way toward the harbor channel.

When the last flash faded, Eleanor sat down on the cold iron floor of the gallery, her back against the railing, and laughed until she cried.

In the morning, the Coast Guard cutter came to take her off the point. A young officer helped her down the one hundred and twelve steps and asked if she wanted to take anything with her.

She thought about the clock, the gulls, the lens, the letter.

"Just the lamp's logbook," she said. "And the storm glass. The rest can stay. Somebody will need to know the light was kept."

As the cutter pulled away, she looked back once. The tower stood white against the gray sky, its lamp dark for the first time in forty-one years.

Then, faintly, it flickered—once, twice—as the fisherman's boat, safe in the harbor, flashed a grateful answer back across the water.

Eleanor raised her hand, and didn't lower it until the point disappeared into the morning.
