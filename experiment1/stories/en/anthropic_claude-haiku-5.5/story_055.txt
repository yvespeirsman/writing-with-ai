The Lighthouse Keeper's Last Letter

For forty-one years, Eleanor Vance climbed the one hundred and twelve iron steps of the Marrow Point lighthouse every evening at dusk, and every evening she lit the lamp. She had done it through blizzards that rattled the glass, through summers when the fog sat so thick she could not see the rail, and through the long winter after her husband drowned off the eastern shoals. The light had never once failed. It was the one promise she had kept without fail.

Now her knees ached, and the doctor in town had used the word "retirement" in a tone that suggested it was a kindness.

The lighthouse would be automated by spring. A panel of solar cells and a little gray box would do what she had done by hand. The Coast Guard had sent a polite man in a pressed jacket to explain it, and he had been kind about it, which somehow made it worse.

She did not argue. She was not a woman who argued with the sea or with the government. But on her last night, she climbed the steps slowly, one hand on the cold iron, and when she reached the top she did not light the lamp right away.

Instead she sat down on the wooden stool by the lens, the same stool she had sat on while nursing her infant son through fevers, and she took out a letter she had written three days earlier and never sent.

It was addressed to Thomas, her son, who had moved to Denver at nineteen and called on holidays. They had not spoken in almost two years, not since the argument about selling the house, not since she had said things about his father that she had not meant and he had not forgotten.

*Tom,*

*They're turning the light off on me. Or rather, they're turning it on by itself, which I suppose is the same thing. I'm told it's efficient.*

*I have been thinking about the first night I ever lit this lamp. Your father had just been stationed here, and I was twenty-six and terrified of the stairs. I counted every one. I still know the number. He used to say I was the only person he knew who could count in her sleep.*

*I have been thinking that I spent so long keeping this light burning for other people that I never asked whether anyone was looking for me. You were looking, I think, for a while. I was too stubborn to see it.*

*I was wrong about the house. I was wrong about what I said about your father. He was not perfect, but he loved you more than the sea, and I think I was angry at the sea for a long time and took it out on you.*

*I'm not asking you to come. I'm only asking you to know that the last light I ever lit, I lit thinking of you.*

*Your mother,*
*Eleanor*

She read it twice. Her hands were steady, which surprised her. Then she folded it, placed it in the envelope, and wrote the address in careful script. She would post it in town in the morning.

Then she stood, her knees complaining, and turned the brass key that started the mechanism. The great lens began to turn, slowly at first, then with the familiar smooth hum she had felt in her bones for four decades. Light swept out across the black water in its long, patient arc, touching the rocks, the distant buoys, the fishing boats coming home late.

She stayed until the light had made its first full rotation. Then she descended the one hundred and twelve steps, counting each one, and did not look back.

---

In Denver, a letter arrived nine days later. Thomas read it standing at his kitchen counter, then sat down very slowly, and then, for the first time in two years, he picked up the phone.
