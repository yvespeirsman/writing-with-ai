The Lighthouse Keeper's Last Letter

For forty-one years, Eleanor Vance climbed the one hundred and twelve iron steps of the Cape Merrow lighthouse every evening at dusk. She lit the lamp, turned the great brass lens, and watched the beam sweep across the black water like a patient hand searching for something lost.

She had come to the island as a young widow with a suitcase, a cat named Pilot, and a letter from the Lighthouse Board offering her the position her late husband, Thomas, had been promised before the storm took him. The board had assumed she would refuse. She didn't. The sea was the only thing that had ever made sense to her since the funeral, and the lighthouse gave her a reason to face it every night.

The island had two hundred residents then, mostly fishing families who regarded her with polite suspicion. A woman keeper was unusual, and a widow keeper was, to some of them, a bad omen. But Eleanor kept the light burning through three hurricanes, one fire in the oil store, and a winter so cold the harbor froze solid. Slowly, the suspicion softened into something like trust. Children began leaving shells on the lighthouse step. Old Mr. Abernathy started bringing her the first catch of every season. The fishermen's wives asked her to read their letters aloud when their eyes grew too weak.

She read them all. Love letters, bills, letters from sons in distant cities who never wrote back. She learned more about the island through its correspondence than through any conversation.

In her forty-second year on the rock, the Board sent a notice: the lighthouse would be automated. A machine would do what she had done, faster and cheaper. Her post would end at the close of the month.

Eleanor read the notice twice, folded it neatly, and put it in her apron pocket. Then she climbed the one hundred and twelve steps and lit the lamp as she always did.

That night, the island's residents gathered at the harbor without anyone quite deciding to. They had heard. Mr. Abernathy spoke first, his voice rough: "Forty-one years. You've kept us from the rocks every single night."

Others spoke too. The fishermen. The children. The woman whose husband had come home in the fog because the beam had shown him the way. They talked until the stars came out, and by morning they had written a petition, three pages long, signed by every household on Cape Merrow.

The Board received it in a sealed envelope, along with a letter that Eleanor wrote herself. She did not plead. She simply described the winter the harbor froze, the night the oil store burned, the storm that had nearly torn the lens from its mounting, and the fishing boats that had come home because she had never once let the light go dark.

Three months later, a reply arrived. The automation was postponed. Then, after a second petition and a visit from an inspector who spent a week on the island listening more than he spoke, the Board made it permanent: Eleanor Vance would remain keeper of the Cape Merrow light for as long as she wished.

She kept it another nineteen years.

When at last her hands grew too unsteady for the iron steps, the island carried her up them, one by one, in a chair lashed to poles. She sat beside the lens while the sun went down, and she let the young keeper, a boy from the mainland named Daniel, turn the brass lamp for her.

"Watch the water," she told him. "It's always telling you something."

Her last letter, written the night before she died, was addressed to no one in particular. It was pinned to the lighthouse door where anyone could read it:

*To whoever climbs these stairs next,*

*The light doesn't keep the ships safe. It tells them where home is. Remember that on the nights when no one seems to be watching.*

*Keep it burning.*

*—E.V.*

The beam still sweeps the water at Cape Merrow every night, and the fishermen still say that when the fog rolls in thick and blind, if you look hard enough toward the rocks, you can see a small figure at the top of the tower, lifting a lamp to the sky.
