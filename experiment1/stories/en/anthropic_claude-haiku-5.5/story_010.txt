The Lighthouse Keeper's Last Letter

For forty-one years, Eleanor Vance climbed the one hundred and twelve iron steps of the Marrow Point lighthouse every evening at dusk. She trimmed the wick, polished the great lens, and turned the crank that set the mechanism turning, so that its beam could sweep across the black water and tell the ships where the rocks were.

No ship had ever thanked her. That was the nature of the work. A lighthouse was successful precisely when no one noticed it.

The autumn she turned sixty-five, the mainland sent a young man named Teodor to replace the automated system. The council had decided the old lamp was obsolete. A satellite could do her job, they said, and a computer would never need its supper.

Eleanor did not argue. She had long ago stopped arguing with things she could not change. Instead she showed Teodor the logbooks, forty-one years of them, each page recording wind and weather, the color of the sky at sunset, the names of the boats that passed. He read them late into the night by lamplight while she sat in the cramped kitchen below, drinking tea that had gone cold.

"You wrote about everything," he said finally, coming down the iron stairs with a stack of pages in his hands.

"I wrote what I saw."

"There's a note here from 1987. 'Fog so thick I could not see the rail. Heard a child singing on a boat somewhere out there. Hoped they were safe.'" He looked up. "Did you ever find out?"

Eleanor set down her cup. "No. The fog lifted at dawn, and the boat was gone. But I kept the light on anyway. Every night, I hoped that whoever was out there could see it."

Teodor was quiet for a long moment. Then he asked, "Why do you think they built this place so far out? There's nothing here but rock and gulls."

"Because the sea doesn't care about convenience," she said. "Ships still need to find their way home, even if the land is far away."

The next morning, the storm came in from the northwest, fast and furious, the kind of gale that rattles the windows of the mainland and makes old fishermen cross themselves. Teodor's ferry had already departed, and the mainland radio crackled with news that the coastal road had flooded.

Eleanor climbed the one hundred and twelve steps for the last time that night, her knees aching, her breath short. Teodor followed slowly behind her, carrying the spare lamp oil.

At the top, she stood before the lens and watched the darkness churn beyond the glass. Somewhere out there, she knew, a small fishing boat was struggling home, its crew exhausted and half-blind with spray.

"Turn it up," Teodor said, peering over her shoulder. "Full brightness."

"It's already full."

"Then we keep it going until morning."

So they did. They worked through the night, Eleanor trimming the wick, Teodor hauling oil up the stairs two cans at a time, until the storm finally broke and the first gray light crept over the water.

Below them, a fishing boat rounded the point, riding low in the swell, its engine coughing but steady. A hand rose from the deck, and then another, waving at the tower.

Eleanor did not wave back. She simply stood beside the great lens, watching the boat find its way home, and felt something settle quietly in her chest.

The council reversed its decision the following spring. The automated system was never installed.

Eleanor Vance kept the light for two more years, until her knees finally refused the stairs. When they laid her to rest on the headland, the fishermen of Marrow Point carried her coffin, and the lighthouse beam swept across the water above them, as it always had, guiding the way home.
