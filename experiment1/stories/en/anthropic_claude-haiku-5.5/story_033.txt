The Lighthouse Keeper's Last Letter

The storm came in off the Atlantic on a Tuesday, the way storms always did on that stretch of coast, with no warning but a change in the gulls. Marta Ilyin noticed it first from the kitchen window of the keeper's cottage, where she had been slicing bread for her father's supper. The gulls had gone silent and were huddled along the rocks, facing inland, as if they already knew something she didn't.

Her father, Anton, was seventy-three and had kept the Carrow Point light for forty-one years. The automated systems had come in a decade ago, and the government had offered to decommission the tower, but he had refused to leave. "A light that isn't watched is just a fire," he liked to say. So Marta had moved back from the city to keep him company, and though she had told herself it was temporary, she had stopped counting the months somewhere around the second winter.

By dusk the wind was pounding the shutters. Anton climbed the spiral stairs with the same steady rhythm he had used for four decades, one hand on the rail, pausing only at the landing where the old brass plaque read *Trust the Light*. Marta followed with the flashlight.

"The lamp's working fine," she said. "The automation's running."

"The automation runs on a timer and a sensor," he said. "It doesn't know when a boat is in trouble."

At the top, the glass was streaked with salt and rain. Beyond it, the sea was a churning dark mass, white crests flaring up and vanishing. Anton stood at the rail, his breath fogging the cold glass, and watched.

The radio crackled. A voice, thin with static, came through the speaker mounted by the door. "Carrow Point, this is the *Harriet Lane*, we've lost our engine, taking on water, approximately two miles northeast. Anyone receiving, please respond."

Anton was already at the console. He keyed the mic with a hand that did not shake. "*Harriet Lane*, this is Carrow Point. We hear you. Stay on this channel. Tell me your number of persons aboard."

"Four. Three crew and my nephew. Oh God, we're going under."

"Keep your heads up and your life vests on. I'm going to bring the light around to you. Look for the beam."

Marta stared at him. "Dad, the coast guard—"

"Is forty minutes out by helicopter in this weather, if they fly at all. Get the manual switch, Marta. Second drawer."

She found it, a heavy brass lever, cold as iron. Together they disengaged the automated rotation and set the lens by hand, Anton's knuckles white around the crank, the great Fresnel lens groaning as it turned. The beam swept out over the water, slicing through rain, and for a moment it found nothing but waves.

"Again," Anton said. "Slow. Count with me."

They counted. They swept. Marta's arms ached. Her father's breathing was ragged but even, and she realized he was murmuring the count under his breath, the way he must have done for decades on long nights when he was young and the light was the only thing between the rocks and the ships.

On the sixth sweep, the beam caught a flash of orange.

"There," Marta cried. "A life ring, a flare—"

"I see it." Anton grabbed the radio. "*Harriet Lane*, we have your position. The beam is on you now. Hold steady. Do not drift toward the Point. Turn your bow to the light. Do you understand?"

Static, then a man's voice, shaking. "Copy. Turning. Oh God, I see it. I see you."

For forty minutes they kept the beam on the water, guiding the battered boat through the channel between the reefs, calling out bearings, adjusting the lens as the tide shifted. Marta lost feeling in her hands. Her father never stopped talking, low and steady, to the crew on the radio and to himself, reminding them of the rocks, the depth, the turn to starboard.

At last a searchlight broke through the rain from the south, and the coast guard helicopter thundered overhead. Anton exhaled and sat down heavily on the stool by the console.

"They're in," Marta said, her voice cracking. "Dad, they're in."

He nodded, but he didn't look at her. He was staring out at the dark water, where the rescue lights were bobbing toward the shore.

"Forty-one years," he said quietly. "I thought I'd never need to do that by hand again."

Marta sat beside him and rested her head on his shoulder. Below them, the lamp kept turning, steady and bright, as it always had.

Later, when the storm had blown itself out and the sea lay quiet and gray, the crew of the *Harriet Lane* sent a letter to the lighthouse. It was short, written in a shaky hand by the boat's captain:

*Keeper, we don't know your name, but you brought us home. Whatever the light is for, tonight it was for us. Thank you.*

Anton pinned it beside the brass plaque on the landing, where it stayed for the rest of his years on the Point.
