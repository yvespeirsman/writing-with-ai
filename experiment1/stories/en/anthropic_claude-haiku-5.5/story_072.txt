The Lighthouse Keeper's Last Letter

The storm came in off the Atlantic on a Tuesday, the way storms do when no one is watching for them. Marguerite Vale was seventy-three years old, and she had kept the light at Harrow Point for forty-one of those years. She knew the sea's moods the way other women knew their husbands' moods, by the small signs: the way the gulls stopped crying, the color of the water turning from green to the dull pewter of old knives.

That evening, the pewter had gone black.

She climbed the iron stairs one last time before the automation crew arrived in the morning. The county had decided the light no longer needed a human hand. A sensor and a satellite link could do the job now, they said, and Marguerite had nodded politely at the man with the clipboard and not told him that the sea did not respect sensors.

At the top, the great lens turned in its brass cradle, throwing its white pulse across the water. She put her palm against the cool glass and felt the old hum through her bones.

Her grandson, Theo, had called from the city that afternoon. "Gran, just come live with us. You don't have to be out there alone."

"I'm never alone out here," she had told him. "The light keeps me company."

It was not entirely a joke. For four decades she had sent her thoughts out with that beam, one long sweep after another, to every ship that might be lost. She had watched fishing boats limp home, freighters slide past in the fog, and once, a small sailboat with a dead man at the tiller and a living girl curled beneath the sail, whom Marguerite had guided in by radio through three hours of dark water.

The wind shrieked against the windows. Somewhere below, the foghorn began its mournful call.

Then, through the spray and the dark, she saw a light that was not hers. Small. Flickering. Low on the water, to the southeast, where the rocks called the Teeth lay hidden at high tide.

Marguerite grabbed the radio.

"Vessel southeast of Harrow Point, you are drifting toward the Teeth. Turn to port. Repeat, turn to port."

Static. Then a voice, thin and terrified. "We've lost the engine. We can't—"

"I can see you. Keep your bow to the light. Keep it on your bow."

She could not leave the lamp. The mechanism needed her hands to override the automatic cycle and hold the beam steady on the channel. So she stood at the controls and talked, her voice calm and unhurried, the way she had talked to every frightened soul for forty-one years.

"You're doing well. Hold that line. There's a gap in the rocks to your left. Can you see the white water?"

"I see it."

"Good. Now steer for the gap. Slowly."

The minutes stretched. The boat, a small fishing trawler by the look of its running lights, rose and fell on the swells. Twice it nearly struck the rocks. Twice Marguerite's voice pulled it back.

At last the trawler slid through the gap and into the calm water of the harbor mouth. A cheer crackled over the radio, ragged and wet with relief.

"Thank you," the captain said. "Ma'am, thank you. Who are you?"

She smiled, though no one could see it. "Just the light keeper."

When the crew arrived the next morning, they found her asleep in the chair beside the lens, one hand still resting on the brass. They tried to wake her gently. Only then did they notice the envelope on the console, addressed in careful handwriting to Theo.

It was short. It said that the light was yours now, that she hoped he would find something to keep his own nights company, and that the sea was always listening, so he should always speak to it kindly.

They buried her in the spring, in the little churchyard above the harbor, where the wind comes in clean and the lighthouse can be seen turning from every window in town.

The beam still sweeps the water each night. Fishermen say that on the worst nights, if you listen closely, you can hear a woman's voice in the static, calm and steady, telling you to keep your bow to the light.
