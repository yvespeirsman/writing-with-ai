The Lighthouse Keeper's Last Letter

For forty-one years, Eleanor Vance climbed the spiral stairs of the Gull Point lighthouse every evening at dusk, wound the great clockwork mechanism, and lit the lamp that guided ships past the jagged rocks of the northern coast. She had come to the island as a young bride, and when her husband Thomas drowned in a November storm, the Lighthouse Board offered her the position out of pity. She accepted it out of stubbornness, and she stayed out of something closer to love.

Now her hands were knotted like driftwood, and the stairs took her a full quarter hour to climb. The Board had sent a letter in March announcing that the lighthouse would be automated by autumn. A machine would do her work. A panel of switches in a office in the city would light the lamp, and no one would ever again need to live on the rock.

Eleanor read the letter twice, folded it, and put it in the drawer beside Thomas's pocket watch. Then she made tea.

In May, a boy arrived on the supply boat. He was perhaps twelve, sent by his aunt on the mainland to spend the summer with the island's only remaining family, which was Eleanor. His name was Caleb, and he carried a battered sketchbook and asked more questions in an hour than the gulls had asked her in a decade.

"Why do you keep the clock going if the lamp runs on electricity now?" he asked on his first evening, watching her wind the brass drum at the base of the tower.

"Because the electricity is coming in a few months," she said. "And until then, I'm still the keeper."

He thought about this seriously, the way children do when they sense that an adult is telling the truth in a roundabout way. "Do you want them to take it away?"

Eleanor did not answer right away. She had not expected the question, and she found she did not have a ready answer.

Over the summer, Caleb followed her everywhere. He learned the names of the lenses, the ratio of oil to wick, the signals the fog bell made in different weather. He sketched the lamp room, the gears, the view from the gallery at dawn. Eleanor taught him the old charts and the names of the wrecks scattered beneath the waves: the *Margaret Ann*, the *Stellan*, the schooner whose name no one remembered but whose bell still sometimes rang in the surf.

One evening in August, a storm rolled in fast from the west. The radio crackled with warnings, and Eleanor's knees ached with the sudden drop in pressure. She climbed the stairs slowly, Caleb close behind with a lantern.

"Can the machine do this?" he asked, pausing on a landing to catch his breath. "When the wind gets bad?"

"The machine can do many things," Eleanor said. "It doesn't know when to be afraid."

"Are you afraid?"

"Every storm," she said. "That's how you know it matters."

They reached the lamp room as the first real gusts hit the glass. Eleanor checked the wick, adjusted the lens, and watched the beam sweep out across the black water. Somewhere to the north, a small fishing trawler was struggling home, its crew watching for the light. Eleanor had no way of knowing who they were, but she knew they were out there, and that was enough.

The trawler came in safely just before midnight. Caleb watched from the gallery as its lantern bobbed through the harbor mouth, and he let out a whoop that the wind swallowed whole.

In September, the Board's technicians arrived with crates of equipment. They were polite and efficient, and they measured the lamp room with tape measures while Eleanor watched from a kitchen chair. On the last day, the lead technician came to say goodbye.

"You've kept an extraordinary record, Mrs. Vance," he said, holding up a thick ledger. "Forty-one years of logs. Every storm, every ship. The Board would like to archive it."

"Archive it," Eleanor repeated.

"In the mainland museum. People will be able to read it."

After he left, Eleanor sat alone in the lamp room for a long time. Then she took out a fresh sheet of paper and began to write.

She did not write a log entry. She wrote a letter, addressed to no one in particular, to whoever might read the ledger someday and wonder who had been on the rock.

*To whoever finds this,*

*I kept the light here for forty-one years. I was not brave, only stubborn, and then only faithful. On the nights I could not see the water, I trusted there were ships out there, and I lit the lamp anyway. I think that is most of what any of us do.*

*A boy named Caleb helped me in my last summer. He asked whether I wanted them to take it away. I told him the truth, which is that I did not know. I still do not. But I know the light was never really for me. It was for the people on the water, and for the boy who will someday stand on a rock of his own.*

*If the machine is working when you read this, I hope it is keeping someone safe.*

*—E. Vance, Keeper*

She folded the letter, tucked it into the ledger's last page, and closed the book.

That night, for the last time as keeper, Eleanor climbed the stairs at dusk. Caleb followed with his lantern, and together they wound the clock and lit the lamp. The beam swept out over the water, steady and bright, the way it had every night for forty-one years.

Below them, far out on the dark sea, a ship's captain looked up from his charts and saw the light, and adjusted his course toward home.
