The Lighthouse Keeper's Last Letter

For forty-one years, Eleanor Vance climbed the one hundred and twelve iron steps of the Carrow Point lighthouse every evening at dusk, and every evening she lit the lamp. She had done it through blizzards that rattled the glass, through summers when the fog sat so thick on the water that she could not see her own hands, and through the long winter after her husband, Thomas, drowned off the eastern rocks.

The lamp was her proof that the world still had edges, that ships could find their way home.

The year she turned sixty-eight, the town council sent a letter announcing that the lighthouse would be automated. A satellite system and a blinking buoy would do the work now. Her services were no longer required. They thanked her for her dedication and offered a small pension and a plaque to be mounted near the door.

Eleanor read the letter twice at the kitchen table, folded it into thirds, and set it beside the teapot. Then she climbed the steps as usual that evening, lit the lamp, and watched the beam sweep across the dark water.

A knock came at the door the next morning. It was Owen Pike, the young harbor clerk, holding a clipboard and looking as though he would rather be anywhere else.

"Mrs. Vance, I'm sorry. I'm here to collect the keys."

"Of course you are." She handed them over. Then, because he seemed to need something to do with his hands, she said, "Would you help me carry something down from the tower first?"

He followed her up the spiraling stairs, breathing hard by the top. In the lamp room, she opened a wooden cabinet and pulled out a bundle of envelopes tied with twine. They were addressed in a dozen different handwritings, all to the same place: *Carrow Point Light, Keeper.*

"Letters," Owen said.

"Every year since my first winter, the fishing families write when someone goes out in a storm, and sometimes they write to say thank you when someone comes home. Sailors' wives, children, a few men who just wanted to say they were grateful the light was there." She untied the twine gently. "I never answered any of them. I thought the lamp was answer enough."

Owen read one aloud, slowly. It was from a girl named Ruth, age nine, asking whether the keeper ever got lonely up so high.

Eleanor laughed softly. "I wrote back to her once, years ago. I told her the sea is never truly empty, and neither is the dark, if you know where to look."

"Did you mean it?"

"I meant it about the lighthouse. I was never sure about the rest."

They sat together on the cold iron floor while the morning light came through the salt-streaked windows. Owen read more letters aloud, and Eleanor told him stories about the people who had written them: the fisherman who had carved a small wooden lamp for her windowsill, the couple who had named their first daughter after the light, the storm of 1987 when she had stayed up for thirty-six hours straight and the whole harbor had come up the hill to thank her.

By noon, the clipboard lay forgotten on the floor.

That evening, Eleanor climbed the one hundred and twelve steps one last time. She lit the lamp, adjusted the wick, and sat in the chair by the window with the letters in her lap. The beam turned over the water, steady and patient, as it always had.

Below, a single boat was heading toward the harbor, its running light small and yellow against the dark. Eleanor watched it until it reached the breakwater, safe.

Then she turned the lamp down for the last time, not out, but low enough to rest, and went home.

In the morning, the town found a new letter pinned to the lighthouse door. It was written in her careful hand, addressed to the children of Carrow Point, and it read:

*The light will always be here for you. Only the keeper changes. Look for it when you are far from home, and you will know the way back.*
