**The Lighthouse Keeper's Last Letter**

For forty-one years, Eleanor Vance climbed the spiral stairs of the Gull Point lighthouse every evening at dusk, wound the great brass mechanism, and lit the lamp that guided ships past the jagged rocks of the northern coast. When automation came, the town council sent her a polite letter thanking her for her service and a small gold watch as a parting gift. She set the watch on the windowsill and never wound it.

On her last night as keeper, she climbed the stairs one final time. The lamp was already electric, humming quietly to itself, and the room felt strangely empty without the ticking of the old clockwork. She sat down on the iron bench where she had eaten thousands of dinners and pulled a worn notebook from her coat pocket.

Her whole life, she had kept a log: wind speeds, tide heights, the names of ships that passed. But tonight she wanted to write something different.

*To whoever keeps the light next,* she wrote. *The glass on the east window sticks in cold weather. Tap it twice, not once. The third step from the bottom creaks, but it's sound. And if the fog comes in thick around midnight, don't trust your eyes. Trust the bell.*

She paused, then kept writing.

*I was afraid, the first winter, that the light didn't matter, that no one was out there. Then a fishing boat came around the point in a storm, and the captain later wrote to tell me his son was aboard, and that he had seen the beam and known where he was. I never met either of them. I didn't need to. That was the job: being the thing someone could trust in the dark without ever having to know them.*

Below her window, the sea breathed against the rocks. She finished the letter, folded it, and tucked it into the drawer of the desk beside the logbooks.

Then she picked up the gold watch from the windowsill, held it for a moment, and finally wound it. The second hand began its small, steady tick.

She walked down the stairs for the last time, counting them out of habit. Nineteen. Twenty. The third step from the bottom creaked, just as she'd said it would.
