The Lighthouse Keeper's Last Letter

For forty-one years, Marguerite Oakes climbed the one hundred and twelve iron steps of the Carrow Point lighthouse every evening at dusk, and she never once forgot to light the lamp.

The town below had grown smaller in her eyes over the decades, its slate roofs huddled against the wind like sheep. Fishing boats came and went. Ferries stopped running in the winter. Her husband, Henry, had drowned the year their daughter turned six, and the sea had never returned him, only his wool cap, which washed up on the shingle a week later like an apology nobody wanted.

After that, the lamp became the only thing Marguerite was certain of.

She wound the clockwork each night, trimmed the wick, polished the great lens with a chamois until it threw light like a cathedral of glass. Ships passed in the dark and never knew her name, and she preferred it that way. A lighthouse keeper's virtue was to be unseen. You were only noticed when you failed.

In the spring of her forty-second year, a letter arrived by the supply boat. The envelope was thick, cream-colored, addressed in a looping hand she did not recognize.

*Mrs. Oakes,*

*You don't know me. My name is Thomas Vale, and I am the son of Eleanor Vale, who was a schoolteacher in Harrow's Cove and who, I believe, once rowed out to your light during a storm in 1979 because she had seen a lamp go dark.*

Marguerite sat down on the cot slowly.

*My mother kept a diary. I found it after her funeral. On the night of the storm, she wrote that the Carrow lamp had gone out for nearly four minutes, and that she had feared a ship would wreck on the Teeth. She wrote that she rowed across the channel alone, found the keeper's door unlocked, and discovered you asleep at the lamp with a fever of 104 degrees. She sat with you until the fever broke and then relit the lamp herself, because she knew how.*

*She wrote that you didn't remember it in the morning, and that she never told anyone. She wrote, "I have seen her face at the window of the lamp every night since, and I think she has no idea what she is holding up."*

Marguerite read it twice. Then a third time, her eyes blurring.

She remembered the fever. She remembered waking with a strange woman's coat over her and the lamp already burning, a warm glow she'd assumed was her own memory playing tricks. She had thought, for thirty years, that she had simply dreamed a kindness.

The letter continued.

*I'm writing because my mother died believing that no one ever thanked you for what you do. She was wrong about the part where no one knows. Ships know. Fishermen know. The Harbor Board has a plaque in the town hall for the lighthouse, though I suspect you've never looked at it. I am sending you her diary, with the page marked. I thought you should have it.*

*With gratitude, and with the lamp still burning,*
*Thomas Vale*

Marguerite folded the letter and held it against her chest. Outside, the sun was sinking into the gray water, and the first gull of evening cried over the rocks.

She stood. Her knees ached as they always did. She climbed the one hundred and twelve iron steps, one hand on the cold rail, and when she reached the top she did not hurry. She wound the clockwork. She trimmed the wick. She polished the lens with the chamois until the light gathered and spilled out over the dark water, reaching toward every boat that was still out there, searching for home.

"There you are," she whispered to the ships, to the sea, to the stranger in Harrow's Cove who had once kept her alive. "I see you too."

Then she lit the lamp.
