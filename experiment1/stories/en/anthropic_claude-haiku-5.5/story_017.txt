The Lighthouse Keeper's Last Letter

For forty-one years, Eleanor Vance had climbed the one hundred and twelve iron steps of the Marrow Point lighthouse every evening at dusk, and every evening she had lit the great lamp that guided ships past the jagged teeth of the reef. She knew the rhythm of the place the way some people know a song: the groan of the stairwell in wet weather, the particular smell of paraffin and salt, the way the wind found the gap beneath the lantern room door and whistled a thin, mournful note.

The automation notice had come in March. A letter with a government seal, polite and final. The light would be replaced by a satellite-linked beacon on a steel post, maintained from a office two hundred miles inland. Her position would be discontinued at the end of the season.

She had read the letter twice, folded it along its creases, and put it in the drawer with the tide tables and her late husband's pocketknife. Then she had gone up the stairs and lit the lamp, because that was what the light was for, whether or not anyone needed her to light it.

In October, the storms came early.

On the eleventh night, the wind rose to a howl by nine o'clock, and the sea threw itself against the rocks with a violence that shook the tower to its foundations. Eleanor was at the lamp when the radio crackled to life in the corner of the room.

"Marrow Point, Marrow Point, this is the *Helen Greaves*, do you copy? We've lost our engine. We're drifting toward the reef."

She grabbed the handset. "Helen Greaves, this is Marrow Point. I see you. Hold your heading. The channel is to your starboard, just past the second marker. Keep the light on your bow."

"We can't see the light, Marrow Point. It's too dark. Rain's too heavy."

Eleanor looked out the salt-streaked glass. Beyond the beam she could see nothing but black water and white spume. The lamp turned, sweeping its arc across the waves, and she thought of the notice in the drawer, the satellite beacon that would blink patiently on its steel post, unable to answer a single voice.

"Then listen to me," she said. "Count the flashes. Every twelve seconds, the light comes around. When it's bright on your bow, you're clear. When it passes your stern, the channel is open. Do you understand?"

A long pause. Then: "Understood. Counting."

She stayed with them for three hours. She talked them through the channel one marker at a time, her voice steady in the cold room, while the lamp turned and turned above her. When the *Helen Greaves* finally cleared the reef and its running lights appeared, small and gold in the dark, a man's voice came over the radio, rough with relief.

"Marrow Point, we're through. Thank you. Thank you. Who are we talking to?"

Eleanor pressed her hand against the cold glass. "Just the keeper," she said. "Just the keeper, on watch."

In the morning, the storm had blown itself out. She climbed down the one hundred and twelve steps slowly, the way she always did, and went to fetch the mail. There was a second letter waiting in the box, from the same office, the same seal. She opened it expecting more of the same.

It was a commendation. The coast guard had recorded the night's transmissions. Her name was on the order. A review had been opened, and the automation program, the letter said, would be suspended pending assessment of the station's continued necessity.

Eleanor read it twice, then folded it carefully and put it in the drawer with the tide tables and the pocketknife.

That evening, as the sun slid toward the water, she climbed the stairs, one step at a time, and lit the lamp.
