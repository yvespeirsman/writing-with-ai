The Lighthouse Keeper's Last Letter

For forty-one years, Eleanor Vance climbed the spiral staircase of the Gray Point lighthouse every evening at dusk. She wound the clockwork, trimmed the wick, and turned the great lamp toward the sea so that ships could find their way past the rocks that had claimed so many hulls.

The work had been her father's, and his father's before him. When the automated beacon was installed on the mainland ridge, the government sent a letter explaining that Gray Point would be decommissioned by spring. Eleanor read it twice, folded it into quarters, and put it in the drawer with her mother's sewing scissors.

She did not tell anyone. Who would she tell? The island had emptied years ago. The last fishing boat had left in autumn, and the ferry came only when the weather allowed, which was rarely now.

On the last night of October, a storm came up from the southwest faster than the radio had predicted. Eleanor was halfway through her evening round when the wind hit the tower like a fist. Rain blew sideways through the gallery railings. She climbed the final steps with her coat soaked through and her hands numb, and she lit the lamp just as the first wave broke white against the outer reef.

She watched the sea through the glass for hours. Sometime after midnight, she saw it: a small green running light, bobbing where no light should be. A fishing trawler, maybe, or a pleasure craft caught out too far. It was drifting toward the reef, slowly, the way a sleepwalker walks toward an edge.

Eleanor grabbed the signal horn and blew it in long, desperate bursts. She swung the lamp in the pattern she had learned as a girl, the warning sequence her father had drilled into her: three long, three short, three long. Over and over, until her arms ached and her voice, which she had also been using to shout into the wind, went hoarse.

The green light stopped drifting. Then, slowly, it turned.

By dawn the storm had passed. Eleanor sat on the cold floor of the lamp room, her back against the iron housing, and watched a small boat tie up at the rough landing below. Three young people climbed out, soaked and shaking, and one of them looked up at the tower and raised a hand.

She raised her own in return.

The letter in the drawer still said spring. But that morning, for the first time in a long while, Eleanor Vance did not feel like the lighthouse was finished with her yet.
