The Lighthouse Keeper's Last Letter

For forty-one years, Edwin Marsh climbed the one hundred and twelve iron steps of the Carrow Point lighthouse every evening at dusk. He lit the lamp, turned the great brass lens until it hummed, and watched the beam sweep across the black water like a patient hand searching for something it had lost.

Nobody ever told him the light was needed. The ships had radar now, and the coast guard had their satellites and their automated buoys. But Edwin kept the lamp burning anyway, because his wife Clara had once said that a light left dark was a promise broken, and he had never been a man to break a promise.

Clara had died in the winter, quietly, in the small house at the bottom of the hill. Edwin had sat with her through the last night, holding her hand while the wind pressed against the windows. In the morning, he went up the hill to the lighthouse, because it was time, and he did not know what else to do with his hands.

Now, in the spring of his seventy-third year, he found himself with a problem. The Coast Authority had announced that Carrow Point would be decommissioned by autumn. The lamp would go dark for good. A solar-powered marker would replace it, a squat gray thing that blinked on a timer and needed no one at all.

Edwin read the notice three times at the kitchen table. Then he folded it carefully and put it in the drawer where Clara used to keep the birthday cards.

That evening, he climbed the steps as always. Halfway up, his knees ached and his breath came short, and he stopped to rest on the landing where the window looked out over the bay. A girl was standing on the rocks below, perhaps twelve years old, with a sketchbook under one arm and a scowl of concentration on her face.

He lifted a hand. She looked up, startled, then waved back.

When he reached the top, he found the lamp room exactly as he had left it, and he lit the wick with trembling fingers. The beam leapt out across the water. Below, he heard the girl call up, "Is that you? The man with the light?"

"That's me," Edwin called down.

"My name's Pia. I'm drawing the lighthouse. Can you come down so I can ask you something?"

He hesitated, then made the long descent, one careful step at a time. When he reached the rocks, she was waiting, sketchbook open. The drawing showed the tower against a bruised purple sky, the beam fanning out like the spokes of a wheel.

"It's good," Edwin said, and meant it.

"Mum says they're turning it off." Pia's voice was matter-of-fact, but she was watching his face. "Is that sad?"

Edwin looked up at the tower, its white paint gone soft with salt, its lamp pulsing steadily against the coming dark.

"Yes," he said. "It is."

Pia nodded as though this was the correct answer. "My gran used to say lighthouses were for people who got lost. She said getting lost is only scary when there's nothing to find your way back to."

They stood together on the rocks as the beam swept over them, again and again, faithful and unhurried.

The next morning, Edwin wrote a letter. Not to the Coast Authority, though he composed several angry versions in his head. He wrote to the local paper, and then to the school, and then to the families in the village whose children had grown up waving at the light from the harbor wall.

He asked them to come. Not to save the lighthouse, not yet, but to climb it with him once before the end. To see the view from the lamp room. To write their names in the logbook, the way lightkeepers had done for a century and a half.

By autumn, nearly two hundred people had signed the book. Some had written only their names. Some had written messages. Pia had drawn a small picture of the beam, and beneath it she'd written: *So nobody gets lost.*

On the last night, Edwin climbed the one hundred and twelve steps with the logbook tucked under his arm. He lit the lamp one final time and turned the great lens until it hummed.

Far out on the water, a fishing boat's running lights bobbed toward the harbor. The beam found it, touched it gently, and passed on, leaving the little boat steady and sure of its way home.

Edwin watched until his eyes blurred. Then he went down the steps, one at a time, and did not look back, because he had kept his promise, and the light had done what lights are for.
