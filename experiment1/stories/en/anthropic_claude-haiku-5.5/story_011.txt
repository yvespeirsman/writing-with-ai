The Lighthouse Keeper's Last Letter

For forty-one years, Eleanor Vance climbed the one hundred and twelve iron steps of the Harrow Point lighthouse every evening at dusk. She trimmed the wick, polished the great lens, and turned the crank that set the mechanism turning, so that its beam could sweep across the black water and warn the ships away from the rocks below.

No ship had ever struck the rocks while she was keeper. She took a quiet pride in that, though there was no one to tell.

The automated system arrived in the spring of her sixty-third year. A man from the coastal authority came in a gray truck, shook her hand, and said the work was "a formality." He spoke kindly, the way people speak to someone they've already decided to pity. Within a month, a steel box bolted to the gallery rail would do what her hands had done for four decades.

"You'll have a pension," he told her. "And a plaque, maybe."

Eleanor thanked him. She didn't mention that she had no one to share a pension with, and that the plaque would be read by gulls.

On her last night, she climbed the steps slowly. Her knees complained at every turn. At the top she stood for a long moment in the lamp room, her palm resting on the cool brass of the railing, and looked out at the sea going violet, then silver, then black.

Then she sat down at the small wooden table beneath the window and began to write.

*To whoever keeps the light next,*

*The lens needs cleaning every Sunday, not Saturday. The salt gets into the brass by Saturday night, and the brass remembers it.*

*When the fog comes in from the northeast, the horn will sound on its own for about three minutes before the wind shifts. Don't panic. It's only the sea clearing its throat.*

*There is a loose stair at the ninth landing. Step to the left.*

*Some nights the light will feel heavy. You'll wonder whether anyone sees it. I used to wonder that. Then one spring a fishing boat came round the point at four in the morning, and its captain flashed his lamp twice at the tower, the way you'd wave at a neighbor. I never knew his name. I think about him often.*

*The light doesn't need to be seen by everyone to be worth lighting. It only needs to be there for the one boat that's lost.*

*I hope whoever receives this knows that. I hope the machine knows it too, though I suspect it doesn't need to.*

*With care,*
*E. Vance*

She folded the letter, tucked it into the small drawer beneath the logbook, and then, because it was the thing she had always done, she climbed back down to the lamp room one last time and turned the crank.

The beam swept out across the water, steady and patient, the way it had every night for forty-one years.

Out past the rocks, a single running light appeared on the horizon. It was moving toward the point.

Eleanor watched it for a long time from the foot of the tower, her coat pulled tight against the wind, until it passed safely into the harbor and the light behind it began, at last, to dim.
