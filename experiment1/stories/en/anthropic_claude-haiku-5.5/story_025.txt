The Lighthouse Keeper's Last Letter

For forty-one years, Elias Moran climbed the one hundred and twelve iron steps of the Carrow Point lighthouse every evening at dusk, and every evening he lit the lamp. He had done it through gales that rattled the glass, through fog so thick he could not see the rail, and through the winter his wife died, when he lit the lamp with hands so cold he could barely strike the match.

The lamp was never his to decide about. The Board in the city sent the paperwork, the automation men came in their white vans to talk about "modernizing," and by the spring of his forty-second year, the decision had been made. The light would be switched to a solar beacon on a steel pole at the end of the new breakwater. Carrow Point would go dark.

Elias did not argue. He had never been a man for arguing. But on his last night, he climbed the steps one final time, and he brought with him a stack of paper and a pencil stub.

He wrote a letter to the sea.

*Dear Water,* he began, and then felt foolish, and kept going anyway.

*I have kept your ships safe for forty-one years. I have watched the fishing boats go out in the grey mornings and come home in the gold evenings. I have seen the Hallorans' boy, who was afraid of you, grow up to captain the Marigold. I have seen storms take three boats and one man I knew well. I am sorry for the ones I could not warn in time. I was always watching, but the light only reaches so far.*

He paused, pencil hovering, and looked out at the darkening water. A single gull drifted past the gallery window.

*Tonight I light the lamp for the last time. Tomorrow the light will still exist, a little way off, on its steel pole. But it will not be here. It will not know the rocks the way I did. I am not sure anything can know them the way a person does, after long enough.*

He lit the lamp. The great lens turned, and its beam swept across the bay in its familiar slow rhythm: one long, two short. He had timed it a thousand times. He had never once grown tired of it.

*You were always the one who decided what I got to keep,* he wrote, *and you were never cruel about it. You took my wife in the end, and I have not forgiven you, but I have not stopped loving you either. Those are two honest things, and I suppose a man can carry both.*

Dawn came slowly, the way it does on the coast, a thin grey line before any color. Elias folded the letter in three and tucked it into the lamp room's brass logbook, under the final entry he had written that morning: *Light extinguished at 6:14 a.m. Sea calm. Wind southwest, light. All ships accounted for.*

Then he climbed down the one hundred and twelve steps, one at a time, the way he always had, and walked out past the gate without looking back.

Behind him, the lamp stayed dark, and the water, which had never once asked anyone for anything, moved gently against the rocks as it always had, and as it always would.

---

The logbook was found eleven years later by a volunteer restoring the lighthouse as a museum. Inside, in careful pencil, were the words that began: *Dear Water.* The volunteer read it twice, then sat for a long time on the iron steps, listening to the sea.
