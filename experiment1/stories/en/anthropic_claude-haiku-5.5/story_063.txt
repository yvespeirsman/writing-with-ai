**The Umbrella Nobody Claimed**

Every autumn, Walter Penrose emptied the lost-and-found at Harrowgate Station, and every autumn he found one umbrella that nobody came back for.

This year it was a green one with a bent spoke and a yellow ribbon knotted around the handle. He hung it on the hook behind the counter, where the other forgotten things waited: a violin case with no violin, a child's mitten, three identical brown gloves, a wedding ring in a matchbox that he still hadn't been able to return.

Walter had worked the counter for thirty-one years. He knew that most lost things were lost on purpose, in a way. People left their umbrellas when they were in a hurry to leave something else behind.

On the third Tuesday of October, a woman in a gray coat came to the window, rain beading on her shoulders.

"I think I left an umbrella," she said. "Green. The handle's tied with ribbon."

Walter didn't say anything for a moment. Then he reached for the hook.

"Yellow ribbon," he said.

"Yes." She let out a breath that was almost a laugh. "I tied it the summer my father taught me to tie a bowline. I never could get the knot right, so I just made it look decorative."

Walter handed it across the counter. "It's been here since September. Nobody claimed it."

"I didn't know where else to look." She turned the umbrella over in her hands. "I was here that day. I was supposed to be leaving for the coast, to start over somewhere. I got as far as the platform and turned around. I think I left it on the bench so I'd have an excuse to come back."

"Did you?"

"No." She smiled, a little ruefully. "I went to my sister's instead. We didn't speak for two years after that. Then she called me last spring and asked if I wanted to come to Thanksgiving, and I said yes, and I didn't think about the umbrella until just now."

Walter nodded slowly. He had heard a hundred stories like this across the counter, though never one so neatly tied up.

"Well," he said, "it's yours now. I'd hate to see it go to the box for unclaimed things."

She opened it, and a few drops of old rain fell onto the floor. "It still works."

"Most things do, if you let them."

She laughed then, really laughed, and for a moment the station felt less gray. She thanked him and turned toward the doors, the green umbrella raised above her head.

Walter watched her go. Then he looked at the hook behind the counter, empty now, and thought that next autumn he might start a new tradition: leaving something small and forgotten where someone could find it, just in case they needed an excuse to come back.
