The Lighthouse Keeper's Last Letter

Every morning for forty-one years, Elias Warrick had climbed the ninety-two iron steps of the Saltmarrow Light, trimmed the wick, polished the great lens, and wound the clockwork that turned it. No ship had ever run aground on his watch. He took a quiet pride in that, the way some men take pride in a well-kept garden nobody visits.

The autumn he turned sixty-eight, the letters began.

They arrived in the supply boat every second Thursday, tucked between the flour sacks and the lamp oil, addressed in a looping hand to "The Keeper, Saltmarrow Light." There was never a return address. Inside each envelope was a single sheet of paper, and on each sheet, a single sentence.

*The tide is coming in faster than the charts say.*

*Your brother still keeps the old bell.*

*Check the third stone from the landing.*

Elias read them at the kitchen table with his tea going cold, and he told himself they were a prank, the sort of thing bored fishermen's children did. He had no brother. He had buried his wife, Marguerite, nine years earlier, and had not spoken to anyone about the old bell since the night the storm took the *Cormorant* and he had stood on the gallery shouting into a wind that swallowed every word.

But on the fourth Thursday, the letter read: *You were right not to ring it that night. They were already gone.*

He sat down very slowly.

That night he climbed the tower with a lantern, though the lamp above him needed no help. On the landing, he counted stones from the bottom of the third step, and the third stone, when he pressed it, shifted. Beneath it was a small iron box, rusted but intact.

Inside lay a brass key, a folded chart of the coast he had never seen, and a letter in Marguerite's handwriting.

*Elias,*

*If you are reading this, the letters have started, and that means I was right to worry. I did not want to frighten you while I lived. There is a truth about the night of the Cormorant that I could never say aloud. Those men did not drown because you failed to sound the bell. They drowned because someone in Harlow Point had been signaling false channels to the wreckers for years, and that someone wanted the light dark that night. You rang the bell anyway, and it was the bell that brought the rescue boat from the north cove. Some of them lived. The records say none did, because I burned the true logs and wrote the false ones to protect you from the men who would have blamed you.*

*The key opens the signal house at the point. The chart shows where the false lamps were hidden. I am sorry, my love. I thought I was protecting you. Perhaps someone else has decided it is time.*

*Keep the light.*

*M.*

Elias read it three times. Then he looked out through the tower window toward Harlow Point, where a single lamp, which had never been lit in his memory, was burning steady and yellow against the dark.

He pulled on his coat, took the key, and started down the ninety-two steps.

Behind him, the great lens kept turning, sending its sweep across the water, as it always had, as it always would.
