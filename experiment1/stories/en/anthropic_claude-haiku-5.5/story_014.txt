The Lighthouse Keeper's Last Letter

Every morning for forty-one years, Edwin Marsh had climbed the one hundred and twelve iron steps of the Carrow Point lighthouse, trimmed the wick, polished the great lens, and watched the sea. He had never once thought of it as lonely. The sea had its own conversation, and a man who listened carefully never lacked for company.

That changed the autumn the automated beacon arrived.

It came on a supply boat in a crate marked FRAGILE in red letters, and the young engineer who installed it was polite, efficient, and gone by sundown. "You'll hardly need to climb anymore, Mr. Marsh," he had said cheerfully, and Edwin had nodded, because there was nothing to be gained by arguing with progress.

The new light blinked on that first night with a steady, indifferent precision. It did not need trimming. It did not need him.

Edwin sat on the bottom step of the tower with his cold tea and felt something he didn't have a name for.

In the morning, he found a letter tucked beneath the door of the keeper's cottage. It had not been there the night before. The envelope was cream-colored, sealed with blue wax, and addressed in neat, looping handwriting: *To the Keeper of Carrow Light.*

Inside was a single sheet.

*Dear Keeper,*

*I don't know if anyone reads these. I've been sending them out with the bottles from my boat for nearly a year, and yours is the first I've heard of anyone finding. My name is Nell. I fish the northern shoals in a boat called the Thimble. My husband drowned off the Gannet Rocks eleven years ago, and I've been trying, in my clumsy way, to find a lighthouse that might tell me whether that light was on that night. I know that's a strange thing to ask. I'm asking anyway.*

*If you were keeping the light that night, I'd be grateful to know. If you weren't, I'd be grateful to know that too.*

*Yours across the water,*
*Nell Harrow*

Edwin read it three times. Then he sat down heavily at the kitchen table.

He remembered that night. The fog had rolled in thick as wool, and the foghorn had moaned for six hours straight. He had stood in the gallery until his eyes stung, watching for any sign of a boat. Near midnight, he had seen a faint glow to the north, a flare, perhaps, that vanished almost at once. He had reported it. Nobody had come.

He had never known whose boat it was.

That afternoon he rowed out to the Gannet Rocks with the old logbook under his arm, the one with the ink still faded and the entries in his own cramped hand. He found the page. *11th November. Fog. Foghorn running. Sighted red flare bearing north-northeast approx. 11:40 PM. Reported to Coast Office. No further sighting.*

He wrote his reply that evening, at the same table, by the light of the new beacon that blinked on without him.

*Dear Nell,*

*I was the keeper that night. I saw your flare, or what I believe was one, and I reported it. I'm sorry no one came. I'm sorrier that I never learned what became of it, and I'm glad, after all these years, to be able to tell you I was watching.*

*I'm keeping the old logbook. I'll send you the page if you'd like it.*

*Also, the light has been automated. I think I'll be climbing the steps anyway, if you don't mind the company of a man who misses the sea.*

*Yours across the water,*
*Edwin Marsh, Keeper*

He sealed it in cream-colored paper, found a bit of red wax in the drawer, and set it on the windowsill, where the wind off the water would carry it out to the bottles and the boats and whoever might be reading.

The next morning he climbed the one hundred and twelve steps, not because the light needed him, but because he did.
