The Lighthouse Keeper's Last Letter

For forty-one years, Eleanor Vance climbed the one hundred and twelve iron steps of the Cutter's Point lighthouse every evening at dusk. She lit the lamp, wound the great clockwork that turned the lens, and watched the beam sweep across the black water like a patient hand searching for something lost.

The village below had long ago stopped asking why she never left. Her husband, Thomas, had been a fisherman, and the sea had taken him one February night when the wind came out of the northeast without warning. They had never found his boat. Eleanor took the lighthouse post the following spring, partly because it paid, and partly because from the top of the tower she could see the whole bay at once, and somehow that made the loss feel smaller.

On the night of her forty-second year, a storm rolled in faster than the weather reports had promised. Rain struck the glass like thrown gravel. The waves rose higher than she had seen in decades, white crowns tearing off the rocks below. She lit the lamp, as she always did, and the beam cut through the gloom.

Then, out past the reef, she saw a small light bobbing in the dark.

Eleanor pressed her face against the cold glass. It was a lantern, swinging on the mast of a small boat that was clearly in trouble, turning helplessly in the swell. She grabbed the radio, but the static swallowed her calls. She tried the signal horn. The wind tore the sound away before it could travel a hundred yards.

There was only one thing left to do. She opened the lamp housing and began turning the mechanism by hand, adjusting the lens to flash a pattern she had not used since her training: three long, three short, three long. The old distress code. Thomas had taught it to her as a joke, years ago, on a calm summer evening. *In case you ever need to shout across the water,* he'd said, *and nobody's listening.*

She flashed the signal until her arms burned and her hands bled from the brass gears. Minutes stretched into an hour. Then the bobbing lantern steadied, turned, and began to move toward the channel markers, guided by the light.

At dawn, the storm broke. Eleanor sat on the cold floor of the lamp room, exhausted, watching the sun rise over a calm gray bay. Down at the harbor, a small boat was tying up at the dock. A figure stepped onto the stones and raised one arm toward the tower.

She could not see his face from that distance. But she knew the way he stood, the set of his shoulders, the wave that was half salute and half apology.

Eleanor laughed, and the sound broke into something else, and she wept at last for the first time in twenty years.

Some letters, she thought, take a very long time to arrive. But they always find their way home.
