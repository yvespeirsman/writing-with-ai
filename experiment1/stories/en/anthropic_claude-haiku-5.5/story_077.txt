The Lighthouse Keeper's Last Letter

Every morning for forty-one years, Elias Brandt had climbed the one hundred and twelve iron steps of the Carrow Point lighthouse, wiped the salt from the great lens, and checked the wick. Some mornings the sea was glass. Some mornings it tried to tear the rock from the cliffs. He had never once missed a night of light.

Then his hands began to shake, and the doctor in town used words like "shortly" and "not much longer," and Elias understood that the light would soon need a new keeper.

The town sent Mara.

She arrived on the supply boat in late October, a slim woman of about thirty with a canvas bag and a notebook already half-full of questions. She had grown up inland, she said, and had never seen the sea up close until she was twelve. She wanted to learn the lamp. Elias, who had expected a grizzled sailor or a sullen young man looking for a quiet post, found himself oddly charmed.

For three weeks he taught her. The mechanism of the clockwork that turned the lens. How to trim the wick without burning your fingers. How to read the barometer's moods, and how to log the weather in the ledger in a hand steady enough to be read by a stranger. "Someone will read this one day," he told her. "Maybe no one. But write it as if they will."

On the twenty-second night, a storm came up from the southwest faster than any forecast had predicted. Elias sat in the lamp room, wrapped in a blanket, too weak to climb. Mara ran the stairs, three times, carrying oil and rags and a flask of tea. Her palms blistered. Her voice went hoarse calling the weather to him through the stairwell. Near dawn, when the sea had finally settled, she sat beside him on the floor of the gallery, both of them soaked and grey-faced, and watched the sky lighten over water that no longer looked like it wanted to kill them.

"You didn't have to do all that," he said.

"You taught me that the light doesn't go out," she said. "I didn't think you meant it only when it was easy."

He laughed, and it turned into a cough, and she held his shoulder until it passed.

In December, Elias took the supply boat back to the mainland. He did not look back at the tower; he had never been one for sentiment. But that evening, in a small rented room with a window facing the harbor, he received a letter in Mara's careful hand.

*Elias,*

*The light was lit at dusk. The sea was calm, then not, then calm again. I logged everything the way you taught me. Tonight I wrote the first entry in my own name.*

*The ledger is in good order. The wick needs replacing by spring. The gull that nests on the north rail has returned.*

*I thought you would want to know the light is still here.*

*— Mara*

Elias read the letter twice, then folded it carefully and placed it in his breast pocket, over his heart, where he could feel its small weight every time he breathed.

Out past the harbor wall, far beyond sight, the beam turned steadily across the dark water, faithful and unhurried, as it had for forty-one years, and would, he now believed, for many more.
