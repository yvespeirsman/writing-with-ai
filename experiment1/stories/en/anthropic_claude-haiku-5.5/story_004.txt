The Lighthouse Keeper's Last Letter

For forty-one years, Eleanor Vance climbed the one hundred and twelve iron steps of the Greywater Point lighthouse every evening at dusk, and every evening she lit the lamp.

The town below had long forgotten why it mattered. Modern ships carried their own satellites and radar, and the great Fresnel lens had become something tourists photographed rather than something sailors depended on. But Eleanor kept the lamp burning anyway. Her father had kept it, and his father before him, and somehow the habit had settled into her bones the way tides settle into a shoreline.

The autumn she turned sixty-eight, the letters began.

They arrived in a plain brown envelope tucked beneath the lighthouse door, with no stamp and no return address. The first one was only a single line in careful, slanting handwriting: *The light was still on the night I came home. Thank you.*

She read it three times, then folded it and put it in the drawer with the tide tables.

The second letter came a week later. *I was twelve the summer my brother drowned off the rocks. I watched your light all night, waiting for the boat to come back. It never did, but the light kept going, and I didn't feel so alone.*

The third: *I am leaving the city tomorrow. I don't know if I'm coming back. I wanted someone to know I was leaving.*

Eleanor began to wait for them the way she waited for weather. She started leaving the lamp a little brighter, polishing the glass until it caught the last of the sun like a held breath. She did not know who was writing. She suspected it did not matter.

In winter, the letters grew more frequent and more personal. A man who had once been a fisherman wrote about the rhythm of nets, and how he missed the sound of waves against a hull. A young woman wrote about a mother in hospice and the long drives home along the coast road, where the lighthouse was the only thing still awake in the dark. A child, in pencil, drew a crooked picture of a tower with a yellow star at the top and wrote, *Is it lonely up there?*

Eleanor sat for a long time over that one. Then she wrote back, the first reply she had ever sent, and tucked it beneath the stone by the gate where the letters appeared:

*Sometimes. But I have never once been alone up here, because somebody down there is always looking up.*

In spring, the storm came.

It rolled in from the northwest with no warning the forecasters had bothered to take seriously, and by midnight the sea was climbing the cliffs in white fury. Eleanor climbed the steps with her heart hammering, her hands raw on the cold iron, and found the lamp flickering. The generator had failed. The backup was not catching.

She worked by hand, cranking the old mechanism with her shoulders screaming, while rain lashed the windows and the lens threw wild shadows across the walls. Somewhere out in the dark, she knew, a fishing boat was running for harbor blind.

The lamp steadied. The beam swept out across the water, pale and sure.

Hours later, as the storm finally broke and the sky went gray at the edges, a single small boat came limping into the bay, its running lights barely visible, guided home by a light that had never once gone out.

Eleanor did not learn until much later that the man at the helm was the same fisherman who had written about the sound of waves. He had seen the lamp through the storm and known, without knowing how, that someone was keeping it lit for him.

The next letter was waiting beneath the door that morning. It was only a few words.

*You were there. I knew you would be.*

Eleanor sat on the top step with the envelope in her lap and watched the sun come up over the water, her hands still trembling, her heart full.

She decided, then, that she would keep the lamp burning for as long as there were people out there looking for it.
