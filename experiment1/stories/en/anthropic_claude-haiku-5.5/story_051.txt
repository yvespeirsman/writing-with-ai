The Lighthouse Keeper's Last Letter

For forty-one years, Eleanor Vance climbed the one hundred and twelve iron steps of the Carrow Point lighthouse every evening at dusk, and every evening she lit the lamp. She had done it through gales that rattled the glass, through fog so thick she could not see the railing from the gallery, and through the winter her husband died, when the wind howled so fiercely she wondered if the whole tower might simply lift off the rock and sail away.

The lamp was her job, and she was good at it. The Admiralty had sent her a letter of commendation once, and she kept it in the drawer beneath the logbook, folded in thirds, unread for years because she knew what it said.

The automated beacon arrived in the spring of her forty-second year.

It came by supply boat, a squat gray box with blinking panels and a cheerful technician named Dev who spoke too quickly and apologized for everything. "It's very reliable," he kept saying. "Never needs sleep. Never needs anyone."

Eleanor watched him install it in the lamp room, where her brass fittings gleamed and her polished lenses caught the light. She said nothing. She made him tea. She signed the forms.

On his last evening on the island, Dev lingered by the door. "You know they're decommissioning the tower," he said gently. "Once the new system's running, there won't be a keeper."

"I know."

"Where will you go?"

She looked out the window at the water, dark and restless, and at the far-off mainland lights she had watched for four decades without ever once wishing to be among them.

"I'll stay until the last night," she said. "Then I'll go."

---

The new beacon worked perfectly. Ships passed the point on schedule, their captains never knowing whose hands had once kept the flame alive.

On her final evening, Eleanor climbed the one hundred and twelve steps slowly, one hand on the cold rail. At the top, she stood in the lamp room and looked at the machine, humming and indifferent.

She did not touch it. Instead she sat down on the wooden stool beside the old logbook and opened it to the first page, written in her own young handwriting: *Lamp lit. Wind northwest, light. Visibility good.*

She turned the pages slowly. Storms. Rescues. Birthdays she had marked in the margins. The day Dev's predecessor had brought her a cake. The night the fishing boat *Margaret Ann* had come through the fog, and the captain had waved his cap at the light in gratitude.

At the back of the book she wrote her final entry:

*Lamp lit. Wind calm. Visibility excellent. Keeper signing off.*

Then she took the folded letter from the drawer, read it at last, and smiled.

When the boat came for her in the morning, the sun was rising gold over the water, and the great light behind her was still turning, steady and bright, as it had for a hundred and forty years.

She did not look back. She didn't need to. She knew it would keep shining.
