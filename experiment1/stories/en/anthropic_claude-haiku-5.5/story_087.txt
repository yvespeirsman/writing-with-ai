The Lighthouse Keeper's Last Letter

For forty-one years, Eleanor Vance climbed the one hundred and twelve iron steps of the Harrowmouth Light every evening at dusk, and every evening she lit the great lamp that guided ships past the jagged teeth of the reef.

She had come to the island as a young widow with a suitcase, a cat, and no particular plan except to be somewhere the noise of the world could not follow her. The job had been advertised in a faded newspaper: *Keeper wanted. Solitude guaranteed. Apply to the Board.* She had applied, been accepted, and never once regretted it.

Now she was seventy-three, and the Board had sent word that the light would be automated by spring. A machine would do what her hands had done for four decades. Her cottage would be sold. The island would empty.

She took the news the way she took weather: without complaint, but with a long look at the horizon.

On her last night, she did not climb the steps right away. Instead she sat on the rocks below the tower with a tin of tea and watched the water turn from pewter to ink. The gulls had gone quiet. Somewhere out past the reef, a single running light blinked, moving slowly north.

She thought about the ships she had guided over the years. Fishing boats returning heavy with catch. Freighters carrying grain and steel and, once, a circus elephant that had bellowed the whole night long. She had never seen most of their crews, never learned their names. That had been the arrangement. The light asked nothing and gave only passage.

Yet sometimes, in the early years, a sailor would wave from a deck as he passed, and she would wave back into the dark, not knowing if he could see her at all.

When the stars came out, she climbed the steps. One hundred and twelve. Her knees complained at every one, as they had for years, but she did not hurry.

At the top, the lamp room smelled of brass polish and paraffin, as it always had. She trimmed the wick, checked the lens, and turned the mechanism that set the great beam turning. Light swept across the black water in its slow, patient arc.

Then she sat down at the small wooden desk beside the window, took out a sheet of paper, and began to write.

*To whoever is on the water tonight,*

*I have kept this light for forty-one years. I do not know your name, but I have known your passage. Every time you came safely through, I counted it a good night's work.*

*Tomorrow someone else will take my place, and the light will keep burning without a hand to tend it. But you will still need it. So I hope you will keep watching for it, the way I kept watching for you.*

*Steer true. Come home.*

*—E. Vance, Keeper*

She folded the letter, tucked it into the brass drawer of the desk where the logbooks lay, and went back to the window.

Out past the reef, the running light had stopped moving. Someone was waiting for the beam to find them.

Eleanor lifted her hand, and the lamp swept on across the dark.
