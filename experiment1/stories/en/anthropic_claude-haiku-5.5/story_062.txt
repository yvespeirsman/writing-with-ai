The Lighthouse Keeper's Last Letter

For forty-one years, Eleanor Vance climbed the same hundred and twelve iron steps every evening at dusk to light the lamp at Gull Point. She knew the number without counting. She knew the way the stairwell smelled of salt and old paint, and which step groaned under her left foot, and how the wind pressed against the glass at the top like a patient animal wanting in.

The lighthouse was automated now. The Coast Guard had said so in a letter three winters ago, polite and final, thanking her for her service and enclosing a check that was smaller than she expected. But Eleanor had never quite accepted the machine that had replaced her. So she still climbed each evening, polished the lens, and sat in the small chair by the window until the beam swept out across the black water, reliable as breath.

Her son wanted her in Portland. Her doctor wanted her to stop climbing stairs. The town wanted her to have a plaque. Eleanor wanted none of it.

On a gray October evening, she found an envelope tucked beneath the door of the lamp room. It had no stamp. The handwriting was careful, slanted, and familiar in a way that made her hands go still.

*Eleanor,*

*I know you won't believe me, and I'm not sure I'd believe me either. But the light is still the thing that brings me home. I have been trying to reach the point for thirty-eight years. The tide has been unkind. Tonight I will try once more. If you see a lamp on the water that isn't yours, don't come down. Just keep it burning.*

*—Thomas*

Thomas Reed had been her husband. He had gone out in a storm in 1986 to bring in a fishing boat that was not his to save, and the sea had taken both him and the boat. The Coast Guard had found nothing but a single oar.

Eleanor read the letter three times. Then she folded it carefully, set it on the sill, and turned to the lens.

Outside, the sky had gone the color of slate. Far out, past the rocks where the current ran hard, a small light flickered on the water. It was low and yellow, the color of an old oil lamp, and it was moving.

Her hands trembled as she checked the wick, the oil, the mechanism that had not turned in years. Then she did the thing she had done every night for forty-one years. She lit the lamp and let it sweep across the dark.

She stayed at the window until dawn. The small light never reached the shore. But twice, just before the sun rose pale over the water, she thought she saw a figure in a yellow slicker lift one hand against the glow, and then the fog closed over and there was nothing but the sea.

Eleanor kept the lamp burning all that winter. Every evening she climbed the hundred and twelve steps, and every evening she left the light on for someone who might still be finding his way home.
