The Last Lighthouse Keeper's Letter

Every evening for thirty-one years, Walter Finch climbed the one hundred and twelve iron steps of the Marrow Point lighthouse and lit the lamp. He did it in gales that rattled the glass, in fog so thick he could not see the railing, and on the still nights when the sea lay flat as a dinner plate and the gulls slept on the rocks.

The automatic system arrived in a crate in March. Two men from the city came over on the supply boat, unpacked a grey metal box covered in dials, and bolted it to the wall beside his old brass mechanism. They were polite. One of them shook his hand and said, "You've done a fine job, Mr. Finch. The Authority is grateful."

Walter nodded and said nothing. He had never been good with gratitude. It always seemed to arrive after the thing it was thanking you for, like a bill.

After they left, he sat on the bottom step for a long time.

His wife, Margaret, had died six winters ago. She had been the one who talked. She talked to the gulls, to the fishermen who rowed out with their catches, to the seals that hauled themselves onto the eastern skerry. Walter had listened and grunted and occasionally laughed, and that had been enough. When she was gone, the lighthouse grew quieter than any place should be, and he had kept climbing the steps because the climbing was the only thing that still made sense.

Now the box on the wall would do the climbing for him.

On his last evening, he did not sleep. At dusk he polished the lens one final time, though it did not need it. He wiped the brass and checked the wick and the oil, as though the machine might be offended by a careless keeper. Then he climbed the steps slowly, pausing at the landings to catch his breath, and at the top he stood in the gallery with the wind pulling at his coat.

Below him the village was lighting its windows one by one. Somewhere a dog barked. The fishing boats were coming in, small and steady, following the same path they had followed since before he was born, guided by the beam he had tended for three decades.

He lit the lamp by hand, the old way, though the machine would have done it at the touch of a switch. The flame caught, and the great lens gathered it and threw it out across the water in a slow, sweeping arm of gold.

"Well," Walter said aloud, to no one. "There you go."

The next morning, the supply boat came, and the two men from the city found the keeper's cottage empty, the kettle cold on the stove. On the table was a single envelope addressed to the Authority. Inside was a note in careful handwriting:

*The light still works. I checked it one more time. You'll find the oil is full and the wick is trimmed. Whoever comes after, tell them the trick is to keep your eyes on the horizon, not the water. And talk to the seals. They're good listeners.*

*— W. Finch, Keeper, Marrow Point, 31 years*

The men read it twice. Then one of them walked out to the rocks and looked up at the tower, where the machine on the wall was turning the lamp on and off exactly as it should, and the beam swept out over the water, steady and bright, as if someone were still there to watch it go.
