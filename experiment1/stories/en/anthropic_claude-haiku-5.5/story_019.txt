The Lighthouse Keeper's Last Letter

For forty-one years, Eleanor Vance climbed the spiral stairs of the Gannet Rock lighthouse every evening at dusk. She wound the clockwork, trimmed the wick, and turned the great lens so its beam could sweep across the black Atlantic. Ships passed in the night and never knew her name. That was the job. You kept the light burning, and the light kept the sea from taking people.

The island was two miles of granite and wind, a single supply boat each month, and a radio that crackled more than it spoke. Eleanor didn't mind the solitude. She had learned, somewhere in her thirties, that people were easier to love from a distance, and the sea was honest about its intentions in a way people rarely were.

Her husband, Thomas, had been a fisherman. The sea had taken him the winter their daughter turned four, and Eleanor had taken the lighthouse post the following spring, partly to be near the water that had swallowed him and partly because she could not bear the sight of the house on the mainland where every room held his shape.

Her daughter, Clara, had grown up on the mainland with Eleanor's sister and had visited the island only in summers. The visits had grown shorter, then less frequent, until they were mostly letters. Clara wrote every month, neat handwriting on thin blue paper, and Eleanor wrote back with the careful brevity of someone who did not know how to say what she meant.

This autumn, the letters stopped.

The supply boat came on the first of October, and the man who delivered the flour and paraffin handed Eleanor a envelope with her sister's handwriting on it. Inside was a short note: *Clara is in the hospital in Portland. It's her heart. She asked me to write to you. She didn't want to worry you, but she wanted you to know she is not angry about the summers you couldn't come. She says she understands now what the light means to you.*

Eleanor read the note three times. Then she set it on the iron table beside the lamp and stared at it until the sun went down.

That night, for the first time in four decades, she considered leaving the light.

The mechanism would run on its own for a day or two. She could be on the mainland by morning if the weather held. She could be at the hospital by afternoon. But the regulations were clear, and the lighthouse board had no replacement for weeks. And the weather was turning. And Thomas had always said that a keeper who abandoned the light was no keeper at all.

She sat with the question until the wind rose and the glass began to hum. Then she climbed the stairs, wound the clockwork, and watched the beam sweep across the water, one slow turn after another.

Somewhere out there, a trawler was coming home. Somewhere, a child was asleep in a harbor town, trusting that the rocks would be seen in time. Eleanor thought of her own child, sick in a bed three hundred miles of coastline away, and understood for the first time that the light had never been only for the ships.

At dawn she wrote a letter, sealed it, and gave it to the supply boat captain on his next pass.

*Clara,* it read. *I am staying at the light because someone must, and because I have learned that the people we love are often the ones who understand why we stay. Come to the island when you are well. I will keep the lamp lit. I will keep the door open. I have always been coming back to you, even when I didn't know how to show it.*

*Your mother.*

The boat carried the letter away across the grey water. Eleanor watched it until it was a speck, then climbed the stairs again, because the light was waiting, and so was she.
