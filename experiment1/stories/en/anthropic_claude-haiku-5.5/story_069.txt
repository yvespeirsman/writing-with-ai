The Lighthouse Keeper's Last Letter

For forty-one years, Elias Brandt climbed the one hundred and twelve iron steps of the Carrow Point lighthouse every evening at dusk, lit the great lamp, and turned the brass crank that sent its beam sweeping across the black water. He had done it through gales that tore the railings loose, through a winter so cold the sea froze at the shoreline, and through the long, quiet years after his wife, Marta, died of a fever that had nothing to do with the sea and everything to do with bad luck.

The island was small, and the mainland was a grey smudge on the horizon that he could see on clear days and forget on foggy ones. A supply boat came every six weeks. The man who ran it, a talkative fellow named Oswin, always brought newspapers, tinned peaches, and gossip, and always left before the tide turned.

In the spring of his forty-second year, Oswin brought something else.

"Came in with the post," he said, handing over a bundle of letters bound with twine. "This one's addressed to the lighthouse, but it's got your name on the back. Old hand, by the look of it."

Elias turned the envelope over. The handwriting was Marta's.

He sat down on the bottom step of the tower, his knees suddenly unreliable. Oswin, to his credit, said nothing and busied himself with the tinned peaches.

The envelope was thin and yellowed. The postmark was eleven years old. Marta had written it the autumn before she died, when she had been strong enough to walk to the village but not strong enough, she'd said, to climb the lighthouse steps one more time to watch him light the lamp. She had asked the postmaster to hold it, to send it only if Elias ever stopped coming to the mainland, or if he ever asked. The postmaster had been dead for three years. His daughter had found the letter in a drawer and, having no idea what to do, had sent it along with the supplies.

Elias read it sitting on the cold stone step, the sea wind pulling at the pages.

*My dearest Elias,*

*If you are reading this, then either I am gone, which I expect you already know, or you have finally decided to let the mainland back into your life, which I hope you have.*

*I know you think the light needs you more than you need anything else. I know you believe that if you leave the tower, a ship will break on the rocks because you were not there to turn the crank. You are a good man, and a stubborn one, and I have loved both of those things about you for thirty years.*

*But I want you to know something I was too proud to say when I was alive. I was never waiting for you to keep the light burning. I was waiting for you to come home. The lamp was always just a lamp. You were the one I wanted to see at the end of every day.*

*Please don't spend the rest of your life on that rock out of duty to a machine. Go and walk in the orchard in the spring. Eat something warm. Let someone else tend the flame for a while, and see what the world looks like without you standing guard over it.*

*I'll be waiting, my love, whichever way the tide goes.*

*Yours, always,*
*Marta*

When he finished, Elias folded the letter carefully and sat for a long time while the sun slid into the water and the sky went from gold to violet to black.

Then he stood, climbed the one hundred and twelve steps, and lit the lamp.

But the next morning, when Oswin's boat returned for the return trip, Elias was waiting on the dock with a canvas bag over his shoulder.

"How long will you be gone?" Oswin asked.

Elias considered the question, looking back at the tower he had kept for so long, its white paint gleaming in the early light.

"I don't know," he said. "That's rather the point."

He stepped onto the boat, and for the first time in forty-one years, he did not look back at the light.
