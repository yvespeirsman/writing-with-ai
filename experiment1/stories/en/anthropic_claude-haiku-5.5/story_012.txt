The Lighthouse Keeper's Last Letter

For forty-one years, Eleanor Vance climbed the one hundred and twelve iron steps of the Greywater Point lighthouse every evening at dusk, and every evening she lit the great lamp that turned its slow gold eye across the restless sea.

She had come to the island as a bride of nineteen, following her husband Thomas, who had taken the post because it paid well and because he liked the quiet. The quiet had not agreed with Eleanor at first. She missed the noise of the market in town, the clatter of wagons, the sound of other women laughing over fences. But the sea has a way of teaching patience to those who stay near it long enough, and by her third winter on the rock, she had learned to love the way the wind hummed through the lantern room and how the gulls wheeled in the gray mornings like scraps of paper caught in a current.

Thomas died in the spring of her thirty-second year at the lighthouse, a clean and sudden death from a fever that swept through the coast. Eleanor buried him in the small cemetery behind the cottage, beneath a stone he had carved himself the autumn before, when he had joked that he wanted something to keep him company. After that, the Board of Lights offered to send a replacement keeper, a young man with a sunburned face and a nervous laugh. Eleanor sent him back with a polite note and a basket of bread. She told the Board she could manage. And she did.

The years went on. Ships passed. Some waved, some did not. The lamp needed polishing, the wicks needed trimming, the brass needed oil, and Eleanor did all of it, even when her hands ached in the cold and her knees complained on the stairs.

In her sixty-third year, a letter arrived by supply boat. It was addressed in an unfamiliar, careful hand to "The Keeper of Greywater Light," and it had been sealed with plain wax and carried through three ports before it reached her.

She sat at the kitchen table, turned the envelope over twice, and finally opened it.

*Dear Keeper,*

*I do not know if you will remember me. Forty-four years ago, I was a boy of nine, traveling with my mother on the packet steamer Corinna. We were caught in the storm that came off the western shoals. The engine failed. The captain did not know where he was. My mother held me on the deck and told me that everything would be all right, because she had seen a light. She said it was a golden light that turned and turned, and that as long as we could see it, we were not lost.*

*We were not lost. The Corinna limped into harbor the next morning. My mother lived until last winter. Before she died, she asked me to find the light and tell its keeper what it had meant to us. I have spent much of my savings and much of my courage getting here, and I am sorry it has taken me so long. I wanted you to know that someone was watching the light that night, and that the light was watching back.*

*With gratitude,*
*Daniel Harrow*

Eleanor read the letter three times. Then she folded it carefully and set it beside the lamp oil on the shelf.

That evening, as the sun slipped into the sea and bruised the sky with rose and violet, she climbed the one hundred and twelve steps as she always had. Her breath came hard near the top, but she did not stop. She lit the wick, adjusted the lens, and watched the golden beam rise and spill out across the water.

Somewhere out there, she thought, a ship was turning toward home, guided by a light it could not yet see from the deck but would someday remember.

She stayed in the lantern room long after the work was done, one hand resting on the cool brass rail, and let the light turn on without her.
