The Lighthouse Keeper's Last Letter

For forty-one years, Eleanor Vance climbed the spiral stairs of the Gannet Point lighthouse every evening at dusk. She wound the clockwork, trimmed the wick, polished the great lens until it caught the last of the sunlight and threw it back at the sea like a handful of scattered coins. Then she lit the lamp, and the beam swept out across the water, telling ships where the rocks were and where the safe channel lay.

She had come to the point as a young widow with a suitcase and a letter of appointment, and the island had taken her in the way cold water takes a swimmer: all at once, and then gradually, until she could no longer remember what it felt like to be anywhere else.

The lighthouse was automated now. The Coast Guard had said so in a letter three winters ago, polite and final, with a date printed in bold. Her last night would be the thirty-first of October. A young technician would arrive by boat the following week to install a sensor array that would do the job without her.

"You'll have a pension," the letter said, as though that settled everything.

Eleanor folded it into her apron pocket and went back to scrubbing the kitchen floor.

On the last evening she climbed the stairs slowly, one hand on the cold iron railing, counting the steps out of habit. One hundred and ninety-seven. She had counted them so many times that the number lived in her body the way a melody does.

At the top, the lens was waiting. She opened the brass door, breathed in the smell of salt and oil and warm dust, and sat down on the small wooden stool she had carried up every day for four decades.

Below her, the sea was turning from gold to pewter. Somewhere out past the reef, a fishing boat was running for home, its engine a faint, steady heartbeat.

She had always imagined that when this day came, she would feel relief. Instead she felt something closer to grief, though she couldn't have said for what. For the nights she'd sat awake listening to the foghorn? For the ships she had guided through the dark, most of which she would never see again? For the version of herself who had climbed these stairs at thirty-four, believing that the work would be simple and that her life would be whatever she made of it?

Eleanor reached into her apron and took out a folded sheet of paper. It was not the Coast Guard's letter. It was one she had written herself, in the spring, and never sent.

*To whoever comes after me,*

*The light needs tending, but mostly it needs someone who looks at it. Check the wick at dusk and again at midnight. Keep the glass clean with vinegar, not soap, or it hazes. When the fog comes in thick, don't trust your eyes. Trust the horn and the count.*

*The storms will test you. The silence will test you more. You will think no one is watching. Someone always is, out there, in a wheelhouse at three in the morning, looking for a shape they can steer by.*

*I was never brave. I was only here, every night, and that turned out to be enough.*

*Be here. That's all it asks.*

*—E.V.*

She read it twice. Then she stood, crossed to the great lens, and rested her palm against its cool, faceted surface.

At nine o'clock precisely, she lit the lamp for the last time. The beam unspooled across the water, steady and white, reaching past the reef, past the fishing boat now rounding the headland, past the dark bulk of the island, out toward the places she would never go.

The boat's running lights flickered, adjusted, and held steady on the channel.

Eleanor watched until her eyes ached. Then she turned off the lamp, slid the note into the brass drawer beside the stool where the next keeper would find it, and went down the stairs one at a time, counting.

One hundred and ninety-seven.

At the bottom, she paused, and out of habit, she looked back up.

The light was gone, but she knew it would be there again tomorrow. Somebody would make sure of it.
