The Lighthouse Keeper's Last Letter

For forty-one years, Elias Warrow climbed the one hundred and twelve steps of the Saltmarsh Point lighthouse every evening at dusk. He carried a brass lantern in one hand and a wool blanket over his shoulder, and he never once forgot to wind the great clockwork that turned the lens.

The village below thought him a dull man. He spoke little, traded fish for flour at the general store, and kept to himself in the cottage at the base of the cliff. But the fishermen who sailed past the point at night knew the steady sweep of his light, and they knew that as long as it turned, they could find their way home.

In the autumn of his sixty-third year, Elias began to feel the cold in his bones in a way that had nothing to do with the sea wind. He noticed his hands shook when he wound the clock. He noticed he had to stop halfway up the stairs. And one gray morning, he noticed a letter waiting in the post box at the foot of the cliff, addressed in handwriting he had not seen in thirty-two years.

He opened it sitting on the rocks, the wind tugging at the pages.

*Elias,*

*I don't know if this will reach you. I don't know if you still live in that lighthouse, or if you still remember the girl who once rowed out to you in a borrowed boat and begged you to let her see the lens up close. I was foolish then. I left because I was frightened of how much I wanted to stay. I have regretted it every year since.*

*I am writing because my daughter is about to be married, and she asked me last week what the sea looked like the night I left the village. I could not answer her. I realized I had spent my whole life running from a light that was never meant to chase me.*

*If you have a moment, I would like to hear how you are. No need to answer if you would rather not.*

*—Marian*

Elias read it three times. Then he folded it carefully and tucked it into his coat.

That evening, he climbed the one hundred and twelve steps slowly, pausing often, the letter pressing against his chest. At the top, he wound the clock. His hands trembled, but the mechanism turned, and the lens began its patient circle over the dark water.

He sat down on the iron bench by the glass and, for the first time in many years, he picked up a pen.

*Marian,*

*The light still turns. I still keep it. I have thought of you on many nights, though I never told you so, and I am sorry for that silence too. Tell your daughter that the sea looks like nothing at all when you are alone with it, and like everything when someone is out there waiting for you to come home.*

*I am well enough. Older than I'd like. But the lamp is lit.*

*—Elias*

He mailed it the next morning. Then he climbed the stairs again that evening, and the evening after that, and the light swept across the water as it always had, steady and faithful, guiding whoever was still out there in the dark toward shore.
