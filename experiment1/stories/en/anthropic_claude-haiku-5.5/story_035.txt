The Lighthouse Keeper's Last Letter

Every morning for forty-one years, Edwin Marsh had climbed the one hundred and twelve iron steps of the Gull Point lighthouse, trimmed the wick, polished the lens, and watched the sea. The town below called him stubborn. His daughter called him lonely. Edwin called it his job, and he had never once missed a night.

Then the storm came in October, the kind the old fishermen spoke of in hushed voices, and the sea rose against the rocks like something alive and angry. Edwin lit the lamp at dusk as always. The beam swept across the black water, and somewhere beyond it, a small boat was fighting its way home.

He saw it only because the lightning flashed. A single fishing skiff, its sail torn, its lantern swinging wildly. Edwin's hands moved before his mind caught up. He cranked the foghorn, fired the emergency flare, and kept the beam steady, though the wind howled so fiercely through the gallery that it nearly knocked him off his feet.

For three hours he did not sit down. He called out coordinates into the radio, though no one answered, and he kept the light burning, though his arms ached and his eyes stung from salt and strain.

Near midnight, the skiff slipped through the channel between the rocks. Edwin watched the small lantern bob closer, closer, until it bumped against the stone jetty below. A figure climbed out, soaked and shaking, and raised one hand toward the tower in a wave.

Edwin raised his hand back.

In the morning, the storm had passed, and the sea lay flat and gray and quiet, as though it had never tried to swallow anything at all. A young fisherman named Tomas came up the iron steps with a basket of bread and a bottle of cider, and a letter folded in his coat.

"My mother wrote this," he said, a little embarrassed. "She wanted you to have it. She says you kept her son alive."

Edwin unfolded the paper with careful fingers. It was short, written in a looping hand, and it thanked him in words so simple they made his throat tighten.

He read it twice. Then he folded it, tucked it into his pocket, and looked out at the water.

"Tell her," he said quietly, "that the light was only doing what it's for."

But that evening, when the sun dropped and the lamp came alive once more, Edwin stood a little taller on the gallery. For the first time in many years, he did not feel alone in the dark.
