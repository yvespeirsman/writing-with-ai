**The Mapmaker of Harrow Lane**

Every morning, old Ines Quell unrolled a fresh sheet of vellum on her kitchen table and drew the town as it had been the day before.

She drew the bakery on the corner, the crooked bridge, the bench where the Halloran twins had carved their initials. She drew the chestnut tree that had been struck by lightning in '98 and the red door that had been painted blue in the spring. Nobody had asked her to. She simply believed that a town forgot itself a little more each day, and someone ought to keep count.

Her grandson Tomas thought it was a strange hobby. "Nobody's going to frame a map of yesterday, Nonna," he said once, sliding a mug of tea across the table.

"Not for the walls," she agreed. "For the record."

One gray Tuesday, while she was inking the outline of the old railway station, a knock came at the door. A girl stood on the step, perhaps nine years old, clutching a shoebox with both hands.

"Are you the mapmaker?" she asked.

"I suppose I am."

The girl opened the box. Inside, wrapped in newspaper, was a small glass jar. Something pale and folded sat inside it, like a moth pressed flat.

"I found it under the floorboards at my house," the girl said. "The man who lived there before us left it. There's a note. It says it's for whoever draws the town."

Ines took the jar carefully and unscrewed the lid. The paper inside was a map. Not of Harrow Lane as it was, but as it had been drawn in 1961, in pencil so faint it was nearly silver. Every street was there, and so were a dozen things she had long since lost track of: a footbridge over the creek that the council had demolished, a tiny theater above the hardware store, a row of linden trees along Mill Road.

And in the margin, in careful handwriting, someone had written: *The things we lose are only lost if no one remembers to draw them.*

Ines sat down slowly.

"Do you know what it means?" the girl asked.

"I think so," Ines said. "I think it means you have to keep going." She looked at the girl's dusty sneakers and her serious eyes. "What's your name?"

"Bea."

"Well, Bea, would you like to help me finish today's map?"

Bea nodded so hard her hair flew.

They spread the old 1961 map beside the new sheet, and together they began to draw. Ines sketched the bridge that was gone. Bea, who had a good eye and a steady hand, drew the little theater with its painted curtains. By the time the streetlights flickered on, the margins were crowded with names and small pictures: the bakery's cat, the fountain with the broken spout, the linden trees along Mill Road, now restored in pencil.

When Tomas came home that evening, he found his grandmother and a stranger's child bent over the table, laughing about whether the hardware store had ever really sold bicycles.

"Nonna," he said, "who's this?"

Ines didn't look up from the vellum. "This," she said, "is my new apprentice."

Outside, the town went on forgetting itself, one small thing at a time. But on Harrow Lane, at least, two people had started writing it down.
