The Lighthouse Keeper's Last Letter

Every morning for forty-one years, Edwin Marsh had climbed the one hundred and twelve iron steps of the Carrow Point lighthouse, trimmed the wick, polished the great lens, and watched the sea. The sea had never once asked anything of him. That was what he liked about it.

The town below had changed around him. The fishing boats had grown fewer, then larger, then fewer still. The cannery became a gift shop. The schoolhouse became apartments for people who came in summer and left before the leaves turned. Edwin remained, a fixed point, the way a lighthouse is meant to be.

In October of his forty-second year, the automated system arrived. A government man in a pressed gray coat explained it over coffee in Edwin's small kitchen. "The light will run itself," he said. "Satellite-synced. Very reliable. You'll be retired, Mr. Marsh, with full pension."

"Retired," Edwin repeated, as though the word were in a foreign language.

"You've earned it."

After the man left, Edwin sat at the table for a long time. The kitchen clock ticked. Outside, the gulls argued over something on the rocks.

That night, for the first time in decades, he climbed the steps without a purpose. At the top, he stood in the gallery with the wind pulling at his coat and looked at the water. The beam swept across the waves exactly as it always had, turning, turning.

He thought about the ships he had guided, though he had never seen most of them. He thought about the fishermen who had called up to him from their boats, the children who had waved from the cliff path, and the one stormy night in 1989 when a freighter had come so close to the rocks that he could see the faces of the crew through the spray. They had passed safely. They had never known his name. That had been enough.

Edwin took a folded sheet of paper from his coat pocket. It was a letter he had started three weeks earlier and not finished. He had been trying to write to his daughter in Portland, who had asked him again and again to move closer to her. He had never sent the letters before. He had never known what to say.

He read the unfinished line by the light of the lens: *I have spent my life making sure others could find their way home. I think I have been waiting my whole life for someone to need me to find mine.*

He folded the letter again, slowly.

Then he went back down the one hundred and twelve steps, put the kettle on, and picked up a pen.

*Dear Ruth,* he wrote. *I'm coming to Portland. Tell me about the house with the yellow door. I want to hear everything.*

Behind him, the light kept turning, steady and bright, guiding the ships that would never know he had once been there. For the first time, he did not mind.
