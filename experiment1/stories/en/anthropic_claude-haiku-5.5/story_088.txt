The Lighthouse Keeper's Last Letter

For forty-one years, Eleanor Vance climbed the one hundred and twelve iron steps of the Marrow Point lighthouse every evening at dusk. She trimmed the wick, polished the great lens, and wound the clockwork that turned the light so that ships out in the black Atlantic could find their way home.

The island had two hundred residents, a single pub, and a ferry that came twice a week, weather permitting. Eleanor had come to it as a young bride, following a fisherman named Thomas Vance who had promised her the sea was gentle and the view was worth any hardship. The sea was never gentle, and Thomas drowned in her third winter there, his boat found upturned on the rocks below the point. After that, the Northern Lights Authority offered her the keeper's post, a sad kindness, and she accepted it because it gave her a reason to climb the stairs each day and look outward.

She kept a logbook. Every night she wrote the weather, the visibility, the condition of the lamp. Sometimes she wrote other things. *Fog thick as wool tonight. Saw a seal sleeping on the north shelf.* *The Harrow girl's baby has her father's chin.* *Thomas would have hated this wind.*

In her forty-second year, the Authority announced the lighthouse would be automated. A satellite-linked beacon would replace the lamp by spring. There would be no more keeper.

Eleanor read the letter twice at the kitchen table, then folded it into her apron pocket and went up the stairs as usual. That night she trimmed the wick with extra care. She polished the lens until it threw light like a cathedral window. And she wrote in the logbook, as she always did, though she was not sure who would read it.

*Final entry, as keeper. The light is steady. The sea is calm, for once. I have seen every kind of night from this window, and I am grateful for each of them.*

In the morning, the island's children climbed the hill to see what the commotion was. A fishing crew had come in through the channel in the dark, guided home by a light they had known all their lives. The captain, a young man named Daniel Harrow, stood on the dock and asked the first person he met who had kept the lamp burning all those years.

They pointed up the hill. Eleanor was sitting on the stone wall outside the lighthouse, watching the boats come in, her coat buttoned against the cold, a steaming cup of tea beside her.

Daniel walked up to her and took off his cap. "Thank you," he said. "We'd have been on the rocks without you."

Eleanor smiled and looked out at the water, where the morning sun was turning the waves to gold.

"No," she said. "You'd have found your way. You always do. I just made sure there was something to find."
