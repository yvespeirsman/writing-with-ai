The Lighthouse Keeper's Last Letter

For forty-one years, Margaret Hale climbed the one hundred and twelve iron steps of the Carrow Point lighthouse every evening at dusk, and every evening she lit the great lamp that guided ships past the jagged rocks the locals called the Teeth.

She was not sentimental about it. The work was simple: trim the wick, polish the lens, check the oil, wind the clockwork that turned the light. Her father had done it before her, and his father before him. The Hale family had kept the light since the lighthouse was built, and Margaret had always assumed her own daughter would take it up one day, though Eleanor had moved to the city at eighteen and rarely wrote.

The autumn the automated beacon was installed on the far headland, Margaret received the notice in a thick cream envelope. The Board regretted to inform her that Carrow Point would be decommissioned at the end of the year. Her position was terminated with thanks for her decades of service. A small pension, a commemorative plaque, a handshake from a man in a grey suit who had never once seen the sea at night.

She read the letter twice at the kitchen table, then folded it and put it in the drawer with the spare keys and the dead batteries.

That evening she climbed the steps anyway.

The light came on at dusk as it always had, its beam sweeping across the black water. Below her, the Teeth glinted white with foam. She stood at the gallery rail with the wind pulling at her coat and thought about the ships. Thousands of them over the years. Fishing boats coming home heavy with catch. Cargo vessels. A ferry that had once carried a wedding party, the bride waving from the deck. Whalers, smugglers, a naval cutter that had come in during a storm with three men lashed to the mast.

Every one of them had found the channel because someone had been awake to show them the way.

In the morning, Margaret did something she had never done in forty-one years. She did not climb the steps to check the oil. She sat in her kitchen and wrote a letter of her own.

It was addressed to her daughter. It was long, and it was not about the lighthouse at all, at least not at first. It was about the winter Eleanor was six and had insisted on staying up to see the light turn on. It was about the night her husband had died and she had climbed the steps in the rain because it was the only place she could breathe. It was about the strange, quiet pride of being needed by people who would never know your name.

At the end, she wrote: *The light is going dark on the first of January. I wanted you to know that someone kept it burning for a long time, and that it was good work, and that I was glad to do it. If you ever find yourself on this coast, look for the lamp. It will be the last one out.*

She sealed the envelope, walked it down to the post office herself, and came back up the cliff road as the sun went down over the water.

That night she climbed the one hundred and twelve steps for the last time that winter, wound the clockwork, and watched the beam sweep across the Teeth, steady and bright, the way it had always been.
