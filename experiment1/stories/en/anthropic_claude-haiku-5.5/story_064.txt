The Lighthouse Keeper's Last Letter

Every morning for forty-one years, Silas Thorne had climbed the one hundred and twelve iron steps of the Carrow Point lighthouse, wiped the salt from the great lens, and trimmed the wick that kept ships off the rocks. He had never once missed a night.

Now his hands shook too badly to hold the cloth, and the doctor in town had used words like "months" in a voice that tried to be gentle.

So on a grey October afternoon, Silas sat at the small oak table in the keeper's cottage and began to write to his daughter, Ada, whom he had not spoken to in nine years.

*Ada,*

*I am told I have not long, and I find I cannot die with the thing unsaid. You will remember the quarrel. You will remember the words I threw at you, and that you threw back, and that I was too proud to take mine back when you left on the morning boat.*

*I was wrong. Not about everything. I still think the sea is no place for a woman who wants to be a painter. But I was wrong to make you choose between me and your own life.*

He paused, the pen hovering. Outside, the wind worried at the shutters. Somewhere below, the sea breathed in and out against the rocks, the way it had every night of his life.

*There is something I have never told you. Your mother did not drown in the storm of '89, as you were told. She left us. She took the mainland train with a fisherman named Owen Hale, and she wrote to me only once, asking me to let her go. I told everyone she was lost at sea, because I could not bear the town knowing I had not been enough to keep her. I think I passed that shame to you, and I am sorry for it.*

He set the pen down and rubbed his eyes. The lamp was not yet lit; that would be his task at dusk, if he had the strength. Tonight, for the first time in forty-one years, the light might not be lit, and he found he did not know what that would mean.

*I am enclosing the logbook from the year you were born. Read the entry for March 14th. I wrote it in the middle of a gale, and I think it is the truest thing I ever put down on paper.*

*If you come, the lamp will be burning. If you do not, I will understand.*

*Your father,*
*Silas*

He sealed the envelope and sat for a long time with his hand on it.

At dusk, the wind was dying down. Silas climbed the one hundred and twelve steps one at a time, resting at every landing, his breath rasping like the sea on stone. At the top, he struck the match, and the great lens woke slowly, throwing its gold across the black water.

He was still at the window when, far off on the road from the station, he saw a single lantern bobbing toward the point.

He did not go down to meet it. He stayed where he was, keeping the light, the way he always had.
