The Lighthouse Keeper's Last Letter

For forty-one years, Eleanor Vance had climbed the one hundred and twelve iron steps of the Marrow Point lighthouse every evening at dusk. She climbed them when her knees ached, when the winter wind tried to pull her off the rail, and once, the night her husband died, when she could barely see through her tears. The light had never once failed on her watch.

Now the Coast Guard was automating the station, and her last night was Thursday.

She spent Tuesday cleaning the lens, though it did not need cleaning. Every prism had been polished so many times that she could have found her reflection in any of them. She wiped each brass fitting and oiled each hinge, moving slowly, as if speed would bring the end closer.

On Wednesday, a young man arrived from the mainland with a clipboard and a sunburned neck. His name was Danny Ruiz, and he was there to learn the new system: sensors, timers, a little gray box that would do what Eleanor's hands had done for four decades.

"It's very efficient," Danny said, nervously. "It doesn't get tired."

"No," Eleanor agreed. "I suppose it doesn't."

He followed her up the stairs, counting the steps under his breath, and at the top he stopped to catch his breath and stared out at the water. "I can see why people stay," he said quietly.

Eleanor didn't answer right away. She was looking at the old logbook on the shelf, its spine cracked from use. Forty-one years of entries, each one in her neat, slanted handwriting: *Light lit at 6:52 p.m. Fog thick. Two fishing boats returned safely. Gull trapped in the storeroom, released.* Almost every page held some small, ordinary miracle that no one but her had ever witnessed.

That night, after Danny had gone to the mainland, she sat at the kitchen table and wrote a letter. She addressed it to no one in particular, then decided it was meant for whoever might one day climb these steps and wonder what the light had meant.

*To whoever keeps the light next,*

*The steps are one hundred and twelve. Count them. It helps on the nights you feel you cannot go on. The lens sticks in cold weather, so warm it gently with your hands before you turn it. The fog comes in fast from the northeast, and when it does, you will not see the boats, only hear them. Keep the foghorn ready. Ring it every thirty seconds. Some people will call it a nuisance. Ring it anyway.*

*Most nights, nothing happens. That is the secret of this job. You climb, you light, you check, and the sea goes quietly on its way because of your quiet work. No one writes songs about a light that works. But somewhere a fisherman is steering home by it, and a child is asleep in a harbor town because a stranger kept a promise to the dark.*

*I was afraid, my first winter here, that my life would be small. I was wrong. Small things, done faithfully, add up to something very large.*

*Take care of her. She has taken care of us.*

*—E. Vance*

On Thursday evening, Eleanor climbed the one hundred and twelve steps one final time. At the top, she rested her hand on the cool brass rail and watched the sun slip beneath the horizon.

Then she turned the key, and the great light swept out across the darkening water, steady and bright, as it had for forty-one years.

Below her, the new system hummed to life, a machine ready to take up the watch.

Eleanor descended slowly, counting the steps, and did not look back until she reached the bottom. When she did, the light was still shining, and somewhere out on the water, a small boat turned toward home.
