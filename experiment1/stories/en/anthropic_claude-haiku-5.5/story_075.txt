The Lighthouse Keeper's Last Letter

For forty-one years, Eleanor Vance climbed the spiral stairs of the Gull Point lighthouse every evening at dusk, wound the great brass mechanism, and lit the lamp that guided ships past the jagged teeth of the coast. When the automated system arrived in the spring of 1987, the fishing town of Harrow's Reach held a small ceremony to thank her. Then they handed her a pension, a gold watch, and a date for her retirement party, and everyone agreed she would be happier now.

Eleanor was not sure she would be happier. But she was sure of one thing: the sea did not forget its debts, and neither did the people who lived beside it.

On her final evening, she climbed the stairs slowly, one hand on the cold iron rail. At the top, the lamp room smelled of paraffin and salt. She touched the lens, polished so many times it had worn faintly thin at the edges, and thought about the letters.

There had been hundreds of them over the years, tucked into the lighthouse logbook by people who had nowhere else to put their feelings. A fisherman's widow wrote about the boat her husband had never returned to. A boy wrote, in careful pencil, that he wanted to be a sailor like his grandfather but was afraid of the water. A woman wrote three times, each letter shorter than the last, about a man who had promised to come back from the city and never did. Eleanor had read every one. She had answered none of them, because the logbook was not meant for replies. But she had kept them all in a biscuit tin beneath her bed, and some nights she took them out and read them aloud to the empty kitchen, the way some people pray.

That night, as the sun sank into the water, she lit the lamp one final time. The beam swept across the bay, pale and steady, reaching the horizon where a single trawler was heading home through the failing light.

Eleanor watched it until the boat rounded the point and slipped into the harbor. Then she took a folded sheet of paper from her coat pocket and began to write.

*To whoever reads the logbook next,*

*I have kept this light for forty-one years. Some nights it felt like the most important work in the world. Other nights it felt like I was only shouting into the dark. I want you to know that both were true.*

*The people who wrote to me were not asking for answers. They were asking to be seen. So if you ever find yourself in this room, alone, with the wind pulling at the glass, remember that the light is not only for the ships. It is for everyone who is still out there, looking for home.*

*Keep it burning.*

*—E.V.*

She tucked the letter into the logbook, closed it gently, and descended the stairs for the last time.

The next morning, a young woman from the county office arrived with a clipboard and a set of keys. She climbed the tower without being asked to, and when she reached the lamp room, she found the logbook open to the final page. She read the letter twice. Then she sat down on the iron floor, put her face in her hands, and stayed there until the sun came up over the water.

Below her, the trawler was already out again, heading for the open sea.
