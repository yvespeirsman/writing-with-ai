The Lighthouse Keeper's Last Letter

For forty-one years, Eleanor Vance climbed the one hundred and twelve iron steps of the Greywater Point lighthouse every evening at dusk. She lit the lamp, wound the great clockwork that turned the lens, and watched the beam sweep across the black water like a slow, patient hand searching for something lost.

The island had only nine residents when she arrived as a young widow in 1961. By the time the automated system was installed in 2002, there were three, and one of them was Eleanor. The mainland had sent a letter then, politely informing her that her position would be "decommissioned." She had read it twice, folded it into quarters, and put it in the drawer where she kept her spectacles and her husband's pocketwatch.

She did not leave. The automated light was installed anyway, a squat gray box bolted to the gallery rail, and it blinked on and off with no one to tend it. Eleanor kept climbing the steps every evening all the same. She told the gulls it was for the ships. She told the wind it was for her knees, which needed the exercise. She never told anyone the truth, which was that the stairs were the only place on the island where she could still hear Thomas's voice, counting them under his breath the way he used to when they climbed together, newlyweds laughing at how many steps there were.

In October of that last year, a storm came up from the southwest with little warning. The radio crackled that the supply boat would not be coming, and that she should stay indoors. Eleanor made tea, sat by the window, and watched the sea go white at the edges.

Near midnight, a light appeared on the water. Small, unsteady, moving the wrong way for the channel. A fishing boat, she realized, one she did not recognize, its engine coughing against the swell. The automated beam swept over it and away, over it and away, and the boat kept drifting toward the reef.

Eleanor did not think. She put on her oilskin coat, took the lantern from its hook, and climbed. Her knees screamed at the ninetieth step. She counted aloud anyway, her voice thin in the roar of the wind. At the top, her hands shook so badly she could barely open the old manual shutter that had not been used in two decades. But the mechanism was still sound, because Thomas had built it to last, and she had oiled it every month of her life.

She turned the lens by hand. Her arms ached. The beam, steady and deliberate, cut through the rain and found the drifting boat.

Then the fishing boat swung wide, away from the reef, and its engine roared to life. It limped toward the safe channel, guided by a light that someone had decided, after all these years, to tend.

Near dawn, when the storm had blown itself out, a coast guard cutter came around the point and found a woman asleep in a chair at the top of the lighthouse, her hand still resting on the brass handle of the lamp.

In the spring, the mainland sent another letter. It said her position had been reinstated, with a small pension and a note of thanks. Eleanor read it twice, folded it into quarters, and placed it in the drawer beside the pocketwatch.

Then she went up the stairs, counting, to light the lamp.
