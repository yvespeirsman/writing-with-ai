The Lighthouse Keeper's Last Letter

For forty-one years, Eleanor Marsh climbed the spiral stairs of the Gannet Point lighthouse every evening at dusk. She wound the clockwork that turned the great lens, trimmed the wick, checked the oil, and watched the beam sweep across the black water like a patient hand. Ships called it the most reliable light on the northern coast. Eleanor called it her companion.

The autumn she turned sixty-eight, her knees began to protest the stairs, and the letter from the Board of Lights arrived. Automation was coming. A new electric beam, self-regulating, would replace the old lamp by spring. Her services were no longer required.

She read the letter twice at the kitchen table in the keeper's cottage, then folded it into a neat square and set it beside the teapot.

"Well," she said to the empty room. "That's that."

The next morning, she climbed the stairs anyway. The lens caught the pale sunlight and threw small rainbows across the whitewashed walls. She ran her palm along the brass railing, worn smooth by decades of hands, and thought about the names in the logbooks: her father, who had kept the light before her, and his father before him. Four generations of Marshes had watched over this rock.

That evening, something unexpected happened. A fishing boat, the *Constance May*, came limping around the point in a rising fog, its engine coughing. Eleanor saw its running lights flicker and vanish in the murk. Without thinking, she lit the lamp early, wound the clockwork faster than she had in years, and swept the beam back and forth across the water in the old signal pattern her father had taught her: two long, one short, pause, repeat.

Ten minutes later, the boat's horn answered, a low, grateful bellow, and the *Constance May* found the channel and slipped safely into harbor.

The next day, the young harbormaster, Tom Reyes, came up the lighthouse path with a box of pastries and an apologetic expression. "Heard you saved the *May* last night," he said. "Her skipper says he'd have hit the reef without you."

"I only did what the light is for," Eleanor replied.

Tom hesitated, then said, "The Board's reviewing the decommissioning. There's a petition. Half the town signed it. They're saying the new system can't handle fog like that. It's got sensors, sure, but it doesn't know these waters the way you do."

Eleanor looked out at the sea, gray and restless under the clearing sky. "The machines will do their job well enough," she said. "But the sea doesn't care about efficiency. It cares about whether someone is paying attention."

The Board reconsidered. Not entirely, and not forever, but enough. Eleanor was granted a final year, then another, and then, to everyone's surprise, a formal appointment as "Senior Keeper and Fog Warden," a title no one had ever bothered to invent before.

She kept climbing the stairs, slower each year, until one winter morning when Tom found her at the kitchen table, the teapot cold beside her, and a half-finished letter addressed to whoever would keep the light after her.

Its last line read: *Don't trust the light to shine itself. Keep watching.*

Tom kept it framed beside the lens, and every dusk, he climbed the stairs, wound the clockwork, and watched the water.
