The Lighthouse Keeper's Last Letter

For forty-one years, Eleanor Wade climbed the one hundred and twelve iron steps of the Gannet Point lighthouse every evening at dusk. She trimmed the wick, polished the lens, and turned the great brass crank that set the light spinning. Ships on the gray Atlantic knew Gannet Point by its pulse: one flash, a pause, two flashes, a pause. Eleanor liked to think she was the reason they found their way home.

The island had two hundred residents when she arrived as a bride of twenty-three, and perhaps forty when her husband, Thomas, drowned hauling nets in a storm the year after their daughter was born. The mainland council offered to automate the light. Eleanor refused. She said the sea had taken enough from the island, and she would not hand it the lighthouse too.

So she stayed. She raised her daughter, Margaret, in the squat stone cottage at the base of the tower. She taught her to read by lamplight, to splice rope, to read weather in the color of the clouds. When Margaret grew restless and talked of the city, Eleanor only nodded and said the light would still need tending whether anyone wanted to tend it or not.

Margaret left anyway, at eighteen, with a suitcase and a promise to write. The letters came every month for the first year, then every season, then at Christmas. Eleanor kept them all in a biscuit tin beneath her bed, and she read each one twice: once for the words, and once for the things Margaret didn't say.

In the autumn of her sixty-fourth year, Eleanor's knees began to fail. The climb that had once taken four minutes now took twelve, and she had to stop at the landing where the window looked out over the water. Her doctor on the mainland told her to rest. The council sent a letter about automation again, more politely this time.

One October evening, a fog rolled in so thick that Eleanor could not see the rocks from the cottage door. She dragged herself up the stairs, one hand on the cold wall, and reached the lamp room gasping. The lens turned. The light swept out into the white nothing, and somewhere beyond it, she heard the low, uncertain horn of a freighter searching for the channel.

She cranked the mechanism, watched the beam sweep, and kept her eyes on the sea until the horn grew fainter and then, slowly, moved away toward the harbor. Her hands were shaking when she finally sat down on the iron floor.

Two days later, Margaret arrived on the supply boat. She had received a letter from the council, one that Eleanor had written and never sent, asking whether she might come home for a while. Margaret stood in the doorway of the cottage with her coat still buttoned and her eyes wet.

"Mum," she said. "You could have just asked me."

Eleanor laughed, and it came out cracked. "I'm asking you now."

They climbed the tower together that evening, slowly, one step at a time, Margaret steadying her mother's elbow at every landing. At the top, Margaret took the brass crank in both hands and looked at her mother.

"Show me," she said.

Eleanor did. And for the first time in twenty-two years, the light of Gannet Point was turned by two pairs of hands, and it pulsed out over the dark water exactly as it always had: one flash, a pause, two flashes, a pause, a promise to every ship that the way home was still there.
