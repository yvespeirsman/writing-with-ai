**The Lighthouse Keeper's Last Letter**

For forty-one years, Eleanor Vance climbed the one hundred and twelve iron steps of the Harrow Point lighthouse every evening at dusk. She trimmed the wick, polished the lens, and turned the great brass crank that set the light spinning. Ships passed in the dark, and she never once saw any of them reach the harbor. That was the job. You kept the light burning, and you trusted that it mattered.

The year her eyes began to fail, the mainland sent a young man named Thomas to help her. He was quiet and careful, with ink-stained fingers and a habit of humming hymns he didn't seem to know he was humming.

"Do you ever wonder who's out there?" he asked one night, watching the beam sweep across black water.

"Every night," Eleanor said. "Then I stop wondering, because wondering doesn't keep the lamp lit."

He laughed, but he kept glancing at the horizon, as if he expected someone to wave back.

In the spring, a storm rolled in faster than the forecast had promised. Waves climbed the rocks and shattered against the tower, and the wind howled through the gallery rails. Eleanor's hands shook as she worked the crank. Her vision had blurred to a smear of gray and gold. Thomas found her on the landing, one hand pressed to the cold iron wall.

"Let me," he said.

"You don't know the rhythm."

"Then teach me."

So she did. Between gusts, she called out the count, the turns, the pauses, and he matched her, his voice steady where hers had gone thin. Hour after hour, the light swept out over the churning sea. Somewhere past midnight, a single flare answered from the dark, small and desperate, then another, then a third. The fishing boat had been drifting toward the reef. It turned, slowly, away from the rocks and toward the faint promise of the harbor.

When dawn came, gray and exhausted, Thomas sat on the cold floor of the lantern room with his head in his hands. Eleanor sat beside him.

"Forty-one years," he said. "And you never once knew if they made it."

"No," she said. "But they had a light to steer by. That was always the part that was mine."

He was quiet for a long time. Then he said, "I think I'll stay. If you'll have me."

Eleanor smiled and reached for his hand, finding it easily in the pale morning light.

"Thirty steps down," she said. "Mind the ninth. It's loose."
