The Lighthouse Keeper's Last Letter

Every morning for forty-one years, Edith Marr climbed the one hundred and twelve iron steps of the Gannet Point lighthouse, polished the great lens until it gleamed like a held breath, and trimmed the wick of the lamp that guided ships past the jagged teeth of the coast.

When the automation crews came in the spring of her sixty-fifth year, she did not argue. She simply packed her tin of tea, her spectacles, and a box of letters she had never sent, and moved into the cottage at the foot of the cliff, where the sea sounded closer and the gulls were less impressed with themselves.

The letters were addressed to a man named Thomas Wren, a fisherman's son who had left the village the summer they were both nineteen. He had promised to write. She had promised to wait, though neither promise had been spoken aloud. Instead, there had been a single afternoon on the rocks, a lost ribbon caught in the wind, and then nothing but the tide between them.

For years she had written to him anyway, each letter a small confession she folded into the box instead of the post. Dear Thomas, the storm took the north fence today. Dear Thomas, I saw a boat painted the blue you liked. Dear Thomas, I think I am becoming the kind of woman who talks to lamps.

The cottage had one window facing the water, and on her first evening there, Edith sat in it with the box on her knees and found she could not face the stack of unopened years. Instead, she pulled out the most recent letter, the one written only last winter, and read it aloud to the empty room.

"Dear Thomas," she began, and her voice cracked on the second word. She cleared her throat and tried again. "I have stopped keeping the light. I am no longer needed to watch the dark for anyone. I find I am tired of watching it for no one at all."

The wind pressed against the glass. Out on the water, a single fishing boat was coming home, its lantern swaying gently.

Edith watched it until it reached the harbor. Then she took a fresh sheet of paper, dipped her pen, and wrote the letter she had never managed before.

"Dear Thomas, I am still here. The light is out, but I am not. If you are still out there somewhere, come and find me. The cottage is at the foot of Gannet Point, and the window is always lit."

She sealed it, walked down the cliff path in the dusk, and pushed it into the post box at the crossroads, where the postman would collect it in the morning.

It took eleven days for a reply to arrive. It was written in a hand that had grown shaky with age, but she knew it at once.

"Dear Edith," it read. "I saw your light from the sea every night for forty years. I thought it was only the lighthouse. I am coming home."

She read it three times, then set the kettle on, because some things, she decided, were worth making tea for.
