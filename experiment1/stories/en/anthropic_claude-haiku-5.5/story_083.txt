**The Clockmaker's Daughter**

For thirty-one years, Elena Marsh had opened her father's shop at seven, wound the forty-two clocks on the back wall, and waited for the bell over the door. Mondays brought mill workers with broken alarm clocks. Saturdays brought children with wind-up toys they'd been told not to take apart.

Her father died in March. She kept the shop open because she didn't know what else to do with her hands.

In the drawer beneath the register, she found a brass pocket watch wrapped in a handkerchief that smelled of pipe tobacco. The case was engraved with two initials that were neither hers nor his: *T.R.* The hands had stopped at 4:17. Tucked inside the lid was a slip of paper in her father's cramped handwriting: *Not mine to fix. Wait for the one who comes asking.*

She laughed at that, and then she didn't.

She tried to repair it anyway. Her father had taught her everything: the escapement, the mainspring, the patience. But this movement was unlike anything she knew. The gears were made of a metal she couldn't name, and beneath the balance wheel was a second, smaller one that didn't seem to drive anything at all.

On the fourteenth day, the bell rang.

A woman stood in the doorway, perhaps eighty, in a coat too heavy for the season. "I was told," she said, "that you might have something of mine."

Elena held up the watch. The woman's breath caught.

"He always said it ran a few minutes slow," she said softly. "He said that way he'd never be early for anything important." She smiled. "Did you find it?"

"It's stopped."

"Yes. It stopped the afternoon he didn't come back from the quarry. Forty-one years ago." She reached out, then drew her hand back. "I never could bring myself to wind it."

Elena looked at the stopped hands, then turned the watch over and opened the back case. Scratched into the inner brass, in letters so small she'd missed them before, were words: *Wind me when you're ready to be late.*

She turned the key. Once. Twice. The second balance began to move, slow and certain. The hands jumped forward a few minutes, then settled into a steady tick.

The woman closed her eyes.

"Thank you," she whispered. "I suppose I'll be late for the rest of it."

After she left, Elena sat in the quiet shop with the watch ticking in her palm. For the first time since March, she didn't feel like she was waiting for anything.
