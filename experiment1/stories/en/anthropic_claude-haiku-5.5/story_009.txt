The Lighthouse Keeper's Last Letter

The storm came in off the Atlantic on a Tuesday, the way storms always did on that stretch of coast, with no warning but a change in the gulls. Marion Teale noticed it while she was scrubbing salt from the lamp room windows. The birds had gone quiet, and then they had all turned inland at once, as if someone had called them.

She climbed the iron stairs one more time to check the lamp. Forty-one years she had kept this light, and she still counted the steps without meaning to. One hundred and twelve. Her knees complained at every one of them.

The lamp was fine. The glass was clean, the wick trimmed, the great lens turning on its bath of mercury as smoothly as the day she first saw it. Her husband, Walter, had kept it before her, and before Walter, his father. The light had never once gone dark on their watch. Walter used to say that a lighthouse was just a promise made of glass and brass, and that the trick was to keep the promise even when nobody was looking.

Below her, the mainland was a thin gray line that kept vanishing and reappearing behind the rain.

At six o'clock the radio crackled. It was Danny Okafor from the harbor office, his voice thin with static. "Marion, the *Ellen Rose* is out past the shoals. Coast Guard's been trying to raise her for an hour. Her engine's gone and she's drifting."

Marion gripped the rail. "How many aboard?"

"Two. Father and daughter, from what the registry says. Pete and Jo Lindqvist."

She knew the name. Pete Lindqvist had fished these waters for thirty years and had a habit of ignoring every warning he'd ever been given. She had once pulled him out of a fog bank with a flare and a fair amount of shouting.

"Can the helicopter reach them?"

"Not in this. Wind's gusting past sixty. They're saying the morning, maybe, if it eases."

Marion looked out the salt-smeared glass toward the place where she guessed the shoals lay. Beyond them, somewhere in the churning dark, a small boat was being pushed toward the rocks.

"Then they'll need the light," she said.

"Marion, the light's always on."

"Not like this. The beam's too high. It's sweeping over the water and not through it. If they're down in the troughs, they can't see it at all." She was already moving toward the control panel. "Walter rigged a secondary lens for exactly this. Low-angle. We haven't tested it in twenty years, but it's there."

It took her forty minutes, and twice she nearly fell on the wet catwalk. The old mechanism groaned like something waking from a long sleep, but at last she threw the switch and a second beam, lower and narrower, cut out across the water like a blade.

She stayed at the lens all night, adjusting the shutters by hand every time the wind shifted, feeling the cold seep through her coat. At some point she stopped feeling her fingers and kept working anyway.

Near four in the morning, the radio came alive again.

"Marion." It was Danny, and there was something in his voice she couldn't read. "We've got them. We picked up a signal, a strobe. They're coming in on your bearing. They said... they said they could see the light cutting through the rain. Said it was like a hand pointing the way."

Marion sank onto the stool beside the lens and let out a breath she felt she'd been holding for forty-one years.

By dawn the storm had blown itself out. The sea lay heaving and gray, scattered with wreckage, but the *Ellen Rose* was riding at anchor inside the harbor mouth, her hull scraped but whole. Jo Lindqvist, nineteen and shaking in a borrowed blanket, climbed onto the dock and looked up at the lighthouse on the headland.

Marion watched her from the gallery, a small figure in the window, raising one hand.

She lifted her own hand in return, then turned back to the lamp, which was still burning, as it always had, because the promise was made of glass and brass, and it had to be kept even when no one was watching.

Except this time, someone had been.
