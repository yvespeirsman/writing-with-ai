The Lighthouse Keeper's Last Letter

For forty-one years, Marguerite Ashby climbed the one hundred and twelve iron steps of the Carrow Point lighthouse every evening at dusk. She trimmed the wick, polished the great lens, and turned the crank that set the mechanism moving. Then she lit the lamp, and its beam swept across the black water, telling ships where the rocks were.

No ship had ever thanked her. That was the nature of the work. A lighthouse succeeded when nothing happened: when a freighter passed in the night and its crew never knew how close the reef had come.

In her fortieth year, her hands began to shake. Not much at first, just a tremor when she threaded the wick, a fumble with the matches. By the spring of her forty-first, the doctor in town had a word for it, and the word meant the end of her climbing.

The Coast Guard sent a young man named Teodor to replace her. He arrived on a gray Tuesday with a duffel bag, a tablet full of automated diagnostics, and the careful politeness of someone who had been told not to upset the old keeper.

"They're automating the light," he said, standing awkwardly in her kitchen. "Sensors. It'll adjust itself. You won't have to climb."

"I know," Marguerite said. "I read the memo."

"I just didn't want you to think—" He stopped. "I mean, I'm sorry. I know what this place means."

She looked at him for a long moment, then pushed a cup of tea across the table. "Sit. Drink that. I'm going to show you something before I go."

That evening, she took him up the one hundred and twelve steps. She moved slowly, one hand on the cold iron railing, pausing at every landing. At the top, in the glass lantern room, she showed him the lens, the brass gears, the logbook with its four decades of handwriting.

Then she opened a drawer beneath the lamp and pulled out a bundle of envelopes tied with twine.

"Every year," she said, "on the anniversary of the night my husband's boat didn't come back, I wrote a letter. Not to him. To whoever might be out there, in a boat with the lights off, lost. I told them the rocks were here, the light was on, and someone was watching."

Teodor stared at the bundle. "You never sent them?"

"Oh, I sent them. I put them in bottles and tossed them off the north rocks. Stupid, sentimental. The tide took most of them." She smiled faintly. "But I kept the carbon copies. Forty-one years of them."

She handed him the top envelope. It was addressed simply: *To the one who needs the light.*

"Read it when the lamp is burning and you're alone up here," she said. "That's the only rule."

Teodor was quiet for a while. Then he nodded.

The next evening, Marguerite sat at her kitchen window, listening to the faint hum of the new automated system. Far off, she could just see the beam sweeping the water, steady and certain, exactly as it had always been.

She did not climb the steps again. But she did not need to. Somewhere up in that glass room, a young man was reading a letter by lamplight, and the light was still doing what it had always done.

It was enough.
