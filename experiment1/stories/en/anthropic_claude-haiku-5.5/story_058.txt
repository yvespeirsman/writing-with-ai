The Lighthouse Keeper's Last Letter

For forty-one years, Eleanor Vance had climbed the one hundred and twelve iron steps of the Gannet Point lighthouse every evening before sunset, and she had never once been late.

She climbed them now with her knees complaining and her breath short, a thermos of tea hooked on one finger and a wool blanket over her shoulder. The wind off the North Atlantic pushed at her back as if hurrying her along. Below, the sea churned against the black rocks, patient and indifferent, the way it had been for longer than there had been anyone to watch it.

At the top, she set the thermos down and laid her palm against the cold brass of the lamp housing. It hummed faintly beneath her hand. Tomorrow, the Coast Guard would switch the light to automatic. A machine would do what she had done for four decades, and no one would climb these stairs again.

She had known the date for months. She had not told anyone how much it frightened her.

Eleanor pulled a folded envelope from her coat pocket. The paper had gone soft at the corners from being unfolded and read so many times. It was from her brother, Thomas, who had left the island at nineteen and never come back. The letter had arrived three weeks ago, after twenty-six years of silence.

*Nell,* it began. *I'm sorry. I was a coward, and I let our father's anger become my excuse for leaving you alone with it. I've thought about that every day. If you'll have me, I'd like to come home before the light goes dark.*

She had not answered. She had told herself it was because she was busy, or because the past was a door best left shut. The truth was simpler and more shameful. She had been afraid that if she wrote back, she would have to forgive him, and she was not sure she was done being angry.

The sun slid lower over the water, turning the sky the color of apricots. Eleanor watched the first ferry of the evening cross the channel, a small white speck making its slow way toward the mainland. Somewhere on it, she thought, there might be a man with a suitcase and nervous hands, reading a map he didn't need.

She had watched that ferry a thousand times. She had never once wondered who was on it.

Eleanor turned the envelope over in her fingers. Then she walked to the lamp room's west window, where the old lens caught the last light and threw it in long gold bars across the floor, and she began to write on the back of the letter with the pencil she kept for logging weather.

*Thomas,* she wrote. *The light is still on. Come up the stairs slowly. The last step is loose.*

She folded the paper, tucked it into the frame of the window where the wind could not reach it, and went down the one hundred and twelve steps for the last time, one hand on the cold wall, counting.

Halfway down, she stopped and laughed, surprised by the sound. The stairs had never felt so long, and she had never felt so awake.

Behind her, the great lamp began its last rotation of the evening, sweeping its light across the water toward a ferry that was, at that very moment, turning toward home.
