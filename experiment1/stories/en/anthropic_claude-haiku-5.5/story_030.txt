The Lighthouse Keeper's Last Letter

Every morning for forty-one years, Eli Marrow had climbed the one hundred and twelve iron steps of the Kettle Point lighthouse, polished the great lens until it gleamed like a held breath, and wound the clockwork that turned the light. He had never once missed a night. Not during the hurricane of '79, when the wind tore the railing away and he kept the lamp burning with a rag tied around his bleeding hand. Not the winter his wife, Ruth, was buried on the frozen hill behind the town church.

Now the Coast Guard was automating the station, and his last night was Thursday.

On Wednesday, a girl came up the steps.

She was maybe eleven, wearing a yellow raincoat several sizes too big, and she carried a canvas satchel that thumped against her hip with every step. Eli heard her long before he saw her, the slow, stubborn rhythm of small boots on iron, pausing, continuing.

"You're not supposed to be up here," he said when she reached the top, breathing hard.

"I know. The sign at the bottom says so." She peered past him at the lens. "It's bigger than I thought."

"Most things are, up close." He wiped his hands on his trousers. "What's your name?"

"Nell. Nell Pruitt. My grandmother lives in the blue house on Harbor Street. She says you've been up here since before she was born."

"Your grandmother is Agnes Pruitt?"

"She says you used to bring her oranges."

Eli was quiet for a moment. He remembered Agnes, a fierce young woman who had once rowed out to the rocks in a storm to rescue a boy who'd fallen off the breakwater. He had rowed with her. They had found the boy clinging to kelp, and the whole town had called them heroes for a week. Afterward, Agnes had come to the lighthouse with a bag of oranges, the only thing she could think of to thank him, and she'd been shy about it.

"I did," he said. "Sit down before you fall down."

Nell sat on the bench beneath the windows and unbuckled her satchel. Inside were letters, dozens of them, tied with string, the envelopes yellowed and soft at the corners.

"Grandma found these in her attic," Nell said. "They're from you. To Grandma's sister, Clara. Clara died before you could give them to her, I think. There's a note on the top one that says 'to be delivered when the light goes dark.'"

Eli took the bundle with unsteady hands. He knew the handwriting. It was his own, from a younger man who had been certain he'd someday find the courage to say what he hadn't said.

He had loved Clara Pruitt. For one summer, long ago, before Ruth, before any of it had been settled, he had believed they might build a life together. Then Clara's family moved inland, and the letters he'd written had gone unsent, the way so many things do when people are young and proud and waiting for the right moment.

"Why did your grandmother send you?" he asked.

"She didn't. I took them." Nell looked down at her boots. "She's sick. She's going to a place in the city next week, and she said she didn't want to leave anything unfinished. I thought somebody should deliver them. I didn't think it'd be this hard to find the lighthouse."

Eli laughed, and it surprised them both. "It's the only thing on this coast that's hard to miss."

They sat together while the sun dropped behind the water. Nell asked questions: how the lens worked, why the light turned, whether it was lonely. Eli answered honestly. Yes, sometimes. But the boats needed it, and there was a kind of company in knowing your work reached every ship that came in from the dark.

When the first stars appeared, he stood and lifted the lever. The great light began to turn, sweeping its long gold arm across the sea.

"Here," he said, pressing one letter back into her hands. "This one's addressed to your grandmother. Not Clara. I think it's time she heard it, even if it's forty years late. Tell her the oranges were the best part of every winter."

Nell tucked it carefully into the satchel. "Are you going to be okay? After Thursday?"

Eli looked out at the light he had kept for four decades, now beginning its last full week of his hands.

"I think," he said slowly, "I'm going to go visit some people I've been meaning to see for a long time."

On Thursday night, the Coast Guard found the lighthouse lamp still burning, the lens freshly polished, and the keeper gone, the door left open to the wind. On the bench lay a single orange, peeled in one long unbroken spiral, and beside it, a note in careful handwriting:

*The light is yours now. Keep it turning.*
