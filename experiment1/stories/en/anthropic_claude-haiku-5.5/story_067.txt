The Lighthouse Keeper's Last Letter

For forty-one years, Eli Marrow climbed the one hundred and twelve iron steps of the Carrow Point lighthouse every evening at dusk. He trimmed the wick, wound the great clockwork that turned the lens, and watched the beam sweep across the black water like a slow, patient hand.

The island was small, and the mainland was far enough that most people forgot it existed. Eli liked it that way. He had a cottage with a crooked chimney, a garden of stubborn cabbages, and a tin of tea that lasted exactly as long as he needed it to.

What he did not have was anyone to talk to, except the gulls, who were poor conversationalists, and the sea, which talked too much.

The letters started in the spring of his forty-first year.

They arrived by the supply boat, tucked between the flour sacks and the lamp oil. The first was addressed in careful, looping handwriting to *The Keeper of the Light, Carrow Point.* There was no return address. Inside was a single sentence: *Your light got me home once, when I was nine and lost in the fog. I never thanked you. I am sorry it took so long.*

Eli read it three times, then folded it into his coat pocket and climbed the steps that evening with a strange lightness in his chest.

The second letter came a month later. It was from a woman who had sailed past Carrow Point on her wedding day and seen the beam turn gold over the water. The third was from a fisherman whose brother had drowned in a storm the year before. *I watched your light all that night,* he wrote, *and somehow it made the dark bearable.*

Letters kept coming, one or two a season. Eli began to keep them in a wooden box beneath his bed. He reread them on nights when the wind pressed hard against the cottage walls, and he found himself thinking about the people beyond the horizon, the ones he had never met, whose lives had brushed against his own through a thin thread of light.

One autumn, a letter arrived that was different. The handwriting was shaky, and the paper smelled faintly of lavender.

*Dear Keeper,* it began. *My name is Ada Felling. I am ninety-three years old and I have been watching your light from my window on the hill at Hanmere for sixty years. My husband built that window so I could see the sea when he was away at sea himself. He never came home. Your light was the only thing that kept me company through those long nights.*

*I am writing because I am dying, and I wanted someone who had kept the light for so long to know that it was seen. Not once. Every single night.*

Eli sat down on the edge of his bed with the letter in his hands for a long time.

The next evening, he climbed the one hundred and twelve steps as he always had. He trimmed the wick and wound the clockwork. But before he left the gallery, he stood at the rail and looked toward the hill he could just make out on the far shore, where a small window might or might not be glowing.

He lifted his hand and waved, though he knew she could not possibly see it from so far away.

The beam swept across the water, steady and bright, as it had for forty-one years, and for the first time, Eli understood that the light had never been meant only for ships.

---

*Ada Felling passed away three weeks later. Eli never learned whether his wave had reached her. But he kept climbing the steps every evening, and he kept the light burning, because somewhere out there, someone was always watching.*
