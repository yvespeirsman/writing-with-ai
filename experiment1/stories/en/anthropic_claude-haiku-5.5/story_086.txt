The Lighthouse Keeper's Last Letter

The storm came in off the Atlantic on a Tuesday, the way storms always did on that stretch of coast, with no warning but a change in the gulls. Marina Voss had lived at the Dunmore Point light for thirty-one years, and she could read the sky the way other people read the morning paper.

She climbed the one hundred and twelve iron steps to the lamp room and checked the wick, the glass, the brass gears that turned the great lens. Everything was in order. It always was. Her late husband, Teodor, had been a stickler, and after his heart gave out two winters ago, she had kept his standards out of stubbornness as much as devotion.

Below her, the waves began to climb the rocks.

At nine o'clock, the radio crackled. "Dunmore Light, this is the *Halcyon*, do you copy? We've lost our engine. We're drifting toward the shoals."

Marina pressed the transmit button. "Halcyon, this is Dunmore Light. I copy. Hold your position. Can you drop anchor?"

"Negative. The line's fouled. We're taking water."

She looked out through the salt-streaked window. Somewhere beyond the beam, past the churning dark, a small light flickered. Then it was gone.

She had a choice, and it was not really a choice at all. The rescue boat was forty minutes away, if it could launch in this weather, which it could not. The only thing that could guide the *Halcyon* through the channel was the lamp, and the lamp was her responsibility.

But the lamp could be turned to a stronger setting, one the old regulations forbade except in emergencies. It burned through the oil reserves twice as fast. It would leave the station dark by dawn.

Marina did not hesitate. She opened the reserve tank and began to pour.

For three hours she worked, hauling cans up the stairwell, feeding the flame, adjusting the lens until the beam swept across the water like a sword of white fire. Twice the radio called for her, and twice she answered, her voice steady. Once, through the static, she heard a man's voice say, "There, there, I see it. Keep going."

Near midnight, a searchlight finally pierced the rain from the south. The coast guard cutter had made it through. Its crew found the *Halcyon* wallowing in the channel, its six passengers shivering but alive, guided home by a light that should, by every rule, have been running dry.

When the sun rose gray over Dunmore Point, Marina Voss sat on the cold floor of the lamp room, her hands black with oil, the empty tanks stacked around her like a ring of quiet monuments.

Her supervisor arrived by midmorning with a clipboard and a grim expression. He read her report twice, then looked up at her.

"Mrs. Voss, you know this station will be decommissioned for lack of fuel reserves. Automated systems are going in next month."

"I know."

"And you burned everything anyway."

"I did."

He was quiet for a long moment. Then he set the clipboard down and said, "Teodor used to say you were the only keeper on this coast who understood what the light was for."

Marina looked out the window at the calm, bright sea, where the *Halcyon* was limping toward harbor with its crew waving from the rails.

"It was never really about the oil," she said.
