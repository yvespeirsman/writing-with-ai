**The Lighthouse Keeper's Last Letter**

For forty-one years, Eleanor Voss climbed the one hundred and twelve iron steps of the Carrow Point lighthouse every evening at dusk. She lit the lamp, wound the great clockwork that turned its beam, and watched the dark water swallow the last of the sun.

The island had only nine residents when she arrived as a young widow, and by the year she turned seventy, there were four. The mainland ferry came on Tuesdays if the weather allowed, and the government had long since automated most of the coast's lights. Carrow Point was kept on out of stubbornness, and because Eleanor refused to leave.

Her nephew called every Sunday. "Aunt El, come live with us in the city. There's a room waiting, and a doctor who makes house calls."

"The sea doesn't need me in the city," she would say. "But it might need me here."

What she never told him was about the letters.

Every week for four decades, she had written one letter and placed it in a brass tin at the base of the lamp room. Not to anyone in particular. Just a few lines describing the weather, a ship she'd seen, a bird that had nested in the eaves. She addressed none of them. She simply sealed them and set them away.

Some people might have called it lonely. Eleanor thought of it as keeping a log, the way the old sea captains did, marking each day so the world would know she had been paying attention.

One October, a storm came up from the south with no warning. The ferry didn't run. The radio crackled and died. Eleanor climbed the iron steps through the wind, her knees aching at every turn, and lit the lamp as the waves threw spray against the glass.

All night the beam swept across the black water. Near dawn, she spotted a small fishing boat, its engine sputtering, drifting toward the rocks on the eastern side. She had no radio, no way to call out, only the lamp.

So she kept it steady. Turned it slowly, then quickly, then slowly again, the signal she had learned from a retired navy man decades ago and had never once needed to use. The boat's running lights flickered, then turned toward the harbor channel. Its engine roared, and it slipped past the rocks into safety.

When the sun finally rose pale and gold, Eleanor sat down on the cold floor of the lamp room, her back against the brass tin, and laughed until she cried.

The fishermen came to the island the next week with a crate of lobsters and a letter of their own, written by the boat's young captain. *"You saved my son's life,"* it read. *"I don't know how to thank you. I just wanted you to know someone was watching."*

Eleanor read it three times. Then she walked up the one hundred and twelve steps, opened the brass tin, and for the first time in forty-one years, she added a letter with an address on the front.

It was addressed to her nephew.

*Come visit me this summer,* it said. *I think I've finally got something to show you.*
