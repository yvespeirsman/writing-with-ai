The Lighthouse Keeper's Last Letter

For forty-one years, Ada Marsh climbed the spiral stairs of the Gannet Point lighthouse every evening at dusk, wound the great clockwork mechanism, and lit the lamp. Ships passed the rocks because of her. Fishermen learned to steer by the rhythm of her light: two flashes, a pause, one flash, a pause, and then the long steady glow that meant *home is this way.*

When the automated beacon was installed on the far headland, the harbor board sent a polite letter thanking her for her service. It arrived on a Tuesday. Ada read it twice, folded it into quarters, and put it in the drawer with the others.

She did not stop climbing the stairs.

Her nephew, Tom, drove out from the city to fetch her. He found her at the top of the tower, polishing the lens with a chamois cloth that had long since worn thin.

"Aunt Ada, they've scheduled the decommissioning for next month," he said gently. "You can't keep doing this."

"The light doesn't know it's been decommissioned," she said.

"Ships have GPS now. Radar. The beacon on the headland does everything this one did, and it doesn't need a person to climb up here in the rain."

Ada set down the cloth. Below them, the sea moved in long gray breaths against the rocks. "Do you know how many nights I sat up here, thinking no one would ever see the light?" she asked. "Storms that rolled in so fast the sky went black at noon. I never once knew who I was lighting the way for. I just kept it burning."

Tom was quiet.

"Then one spring," she continued, "a man rowed in from the mainland, just to find me. He'd been caught in a squall years before, he said. He'd seen the light through the rain and steered toward it, and it was the only thing he could see that whole night. He wanted to tell me it had mattered."

"Did it?"

"It did." She smiled, and her eyes were wet. "It mattered to him. That was always enough, though I didn't know it until he came."

That night, Ada lit the lamp one final time. The beam swept across the water, sending its familiar pattern over the dark waves: two, a pause, one, and then the long, steady glow.

In the morning, she wrote a letter to the harbor board, thanking them for their kind words. Then, beneath her signature, she added one more line:

*Somewhere out there, someone is steering home by a light I kept burning. That was the job. It was a good one.*

She sealed the envelope, walked down the spiral stairs for the last time, and did not look back, because she knew the light would keep shining without her eyes on it, the way the best things do.
