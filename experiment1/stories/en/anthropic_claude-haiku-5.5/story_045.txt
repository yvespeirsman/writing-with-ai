The Lighthouse Keeper's Last Letter

The storm came in off the Atlantic just after midnight, the way storms did in November, announcing itself first as a rumor in the rigging of the fishing boats and then as a hammer against the windows of the lighthouse on Carrow Rock.

Thomas Ashby had kept the light for thirty-one years. He knew its moods the way a man knows a difficult dog: the long sulk of fog, the hungry way the wind circled the tower before a gale, the low groan the iron stairs made when the cold got into them. Tonight the lamp turned steadily above him, sweeping its white arm across the black water, and he sat at the small wooden table with a pen in his hand and a blank sheet in front of him.

He had been trying to write to his daughter for three days.

Helen lived in Portland now, with a husband she liked and two boys who called Thomas "Grandpa" in the careful way children use for people they've met only a handful of times. They had not spoken since the summer, when he had said something unforgivable about her mother's grave being neglected, and she had said something back, and the telephone had gone quiet between them like a radio losing signal.

He wrote: *Helen,*

Then he stopped, because the rest would not come in any order that made sense.

Outside, a wave struck the rock hard enough to shiver the glass. Thomas looked up, out at the beam, and thought of his wife Ruth on the evening she had first climbed these stairs, laughing, one hand holding her skirt and the other gripping the rail, saying the place was the loneliest and most beautiful thing she had ever seen. He had thought then that he would spend his whole life proving her right.

He had not known how to be lonely with someone. He had not known how to be anything but the keeper. When Ruth died, he had simply kept climbing the stairs.

The radio crackled on the shelf. The harbormaster's voice, thin and far away, reported a fishing trawler, the *Margaret Doyle*, out past the shoals with her engine failing and four men aboard. Thomas stood, his knees complaining, and turned the lamp's signal up one notch, then another, pouring more light into the storm than the old machinery was meant to bear.

He watched the sea for a long time. Then, through the glass, he saw it: a small, stubborn spark of a running light, rising and falling with the swell, closer than it had any right to be.

He kept the beam steady all night.

By dawn the wind had dropped to a mutter, and the *Margaret Doyle* limped into the harbor under her own last strength, all four men alive and cursing the cold. The harbormaster called the lighthouse to say so, and Thomas, hoarse and gray-faced, said only, "Good," and hung up.

Then he went back to the table and finished the letter.

*Helen,*

*I was wrong about your mother's grave. I was wrong about a lot of things, and I was too proud to say so, which is a thing I learned from your mother's stubbornness and never once admitted was there. I am sorry. I keep the light because I don't know how to do anything else. But I would like very much to learn.*

*If you ever want to see the lighthouse, the boys are welcome to climb the stairs. Tell them to hold the rail. It's a long way up, but the view is the loneliest and most beautiful thing you'll ever see.*

*Love,*
*Dad*

He folded the paper, sealed it, and set it by the door where the morning post would take it. Outside, the sun broke over the water, and the lamp, having done its work, quietly dimmed.
