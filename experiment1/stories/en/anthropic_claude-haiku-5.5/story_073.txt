**The Lighthouse Keeper's Last Letter**

For forty-one years, Edith Marrow climbed the one hundred and twelve iron steps of Gannet Point Light every evening at dusk, and every evening she lit the lamp that guided ships past the jagged teeth of the Sorrow Rocks.

Her husband had kept the light before her. When his heart gave out one February, the town council offered her a pension and a polite suggestion that a widow of sixty-three might prefer a cottage closer to the market. Edith thanked them and kept climbing.

The light was not glamorous work. It meant trimming wicks, polishing the great lens until her reflection stared back at her in a hundred bent fragments, and recording the weather in a logbook whose pages had grown soft with handling. But it meant she was needed, and at night, when the sea breathed against the cliffs far below, she felt like the only person awake in the world who mattered.

In her fortieth year of service, a letter arrived by the supply boat. The envelope was addressed in handwriting she didn't recognize: *To the Keeper of the Light at Gannet Point, from one who once sailed by it.*

Inside was a single sheet.

*I was nineteen the night my ship came through the Sorrow Rocks in a storm. The captain was drunk, the mate was dead, and I was certain we would all drown. Then I saw your light. It did not waver. It did not care about the wind. It simply burned, steady and certain, and I understood that someone on that cliff was refusing to give up on us. We made it through. I have spent sixty years trying to find the words to thank whoever you were. I am dying now, and I could not leave without trying. Thank you. You are the reason I have a life at all.*

Edith read it three times. Then she folded it carefully and tucked it into the logbook, between the pages recording storms and calm seas and the night her husband's last shift ended.

That evening she climbed the one hundred and twelve steps, as always. The sun slid into the water, the sky bruised purple, and the first stars pricked through. She trimmed the wick, checked the mechanism, and turned the great lamp on.

Far out in the dark, a ship's lantern answered with a single, grateful flash.

Edith pressed her palm to the cold glass and whispered, "I see you."

She kept the light burning until dawn.
