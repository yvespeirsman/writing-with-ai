The Lighthouse Keeper's Last Letter

For forty-one years, Eleanor Vance climbed the one hundred and twelve iron steps of the Marrow Point lighthouse every evening at dusk. She trimmed the wick, polished the great lens, and wound the clockwork that turned the light so that ships on the black Atlantic could find their way home.

The island had two hundred residents when she arrived as a young widow, and nineteen when she finally retired. The mainland ferry came twice a week now, when the weather allowed. The village school had closed. Most of the houses along Gull Street stood empty, their windows dark as missing teeth.

Eleanor did not mind the quiet. She had learned to hear the conversation of the sea, the way the tide breathed against the rocks, the particular silence before a storm. What she minded was the letter.

It had arrived in March, postmarked from a city she had never visited, addressed in a careful hand she did not recognize. Inside was a single sheet of paper and a photograph of a boy of about nine, grinning gap-toothed beside a dog.

*Dear Mrs. Vance,* the letter read. *You do not know me, but I believe you knew my father, Thomas Harrow. He spoke of you often before he passed. He said that on the night of the great storm in '87, you kept the light burning when the generator failed, climbing those steps by hand with a lantern for six hours until the power returned. He said that because of you, his boat came home. He wanted me to thank you, and I am doing so now, though I am late in doing it. He was a good man, and I think you should know that his last years were happy, and that he never forgot what you did.*

Eleanor read the letter four times, then set it on the kitchen table beside her tea, which had gone cold.

She remembered Thomas Harrow. A fisherman, quiet and stubborn, who had once argued with her about whether the light should be painted red or white. She remembered the storm, too, the way the wind had screamed like something wounded. She had not thought of it as anything remarkable. She had simply done the job.

That evening, as the sun sank into the water, she climbed the one hundred and twelve steps one more time. Her knees ached. Her breath came short. At the top, she stood in the glass room and watched the great lens begin to turn, throwing its golden beam across the darkening waves.

Somewhere out there, she thought, a boat was coming home.

She wrote back that night, on the good paper she had been saving for no particular occasion.

*Dear Daniel,* she began. *Your father was right. And I am glad you wrote.*

She sealed the envelope, walked it down Gull Street in the blue dusk, and dropped it in the box by the ferry landing. Then she went home, and for the first time in a long while, she slept without dreaming of the sea.
