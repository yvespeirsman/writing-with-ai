The Lighthouse Keeper's Last Letter

Every morning for forty-one years, Arthur Penhallow had climbed the ninety-two iron steps of the Gull Point lighthouse, trimmed the wick, polished the great lens, and watched the gray Atlantic swallow the sun. He had never once missed a night. Not during the storm of '87, when the glass cracked and he kept the beam alive with a hand-cranked generator and a prayer. Not the winter his wife, Margaret, lay dying in the mainland hospital, when he rowed across twice a week and rowed back before dusk to light the lamp.

Now the Coast Guard was automating the station. A panel of blinking lights and a satellite feed would do his job. The letter had come in October, polite and final, and Arthur had folded it into quarters and tucked it in his coat pocket, where it had stayed for three weeks, worn soft at the creases.

On his last night, the sea was calm. Too calm, he thought, the way it sometimes went before a change. He climbed the steps slowly, one hand on the cold railing, and when he reached the lantern room he found a girl sitting cross-legged on the floor beside the lens.

She was perhaps twelve, wrapped in a yellow raincoat several sizes too big, with a canvas bag at her side and a face that looked more curious than frightened.

"You're not supposed to be up here," Arthur said.

"I know. The door was open." She tilted her head toward the great lens. "Does it really work? My grandmother said the light used to be seen thirty miles out."

"Twenty-two miles, on a clear night." He lowered himself onto the bench near the window, his knees complaining. "Who's your grandmother?"

"Ruth Callahan. She lives in the cottage by the harbor. She said you used to row out to the mainland every Tuesday and Thursday, and that you were the only man on the coast who never once let the lamp go dark." The girl hesitated. "She said to tell you the light was seen from the mainland, even when you were over there. Someone was always watching for it."

Arthur was quiet for a long time. Then he said, "Ruth Callahan. I haven't heard that name in thirty years."

"She remembers you. She said you once pulled her brother out of the surf, down at the south rocks."

"Ah." He rubbed his jaw. "Tommy. He was a foolish boy."

"He's in Boston now. He sends her postcards."

They sat together while the sky went from amber to indigo. The girl unpacked her canvas bag: a thermos of cocoa, two cups, a tin of shortbread shaped like stars. She poured one cup and held it out.

"Grandmother said you'd probably be sad about the automation," she said. "She said to bring you something so you wouldn't be alone up here tonight."

Arthur took the cup. His hand trembled a little, and the cocoa was too sweet, and he drank it anyway.

"It's a good light," he said finally. "Never failed in forty-one years. Not once."

"Then maybe you should be proud of that instead of sad," the girl said, with the blunt wisdom of twelve. "The new lights won't have anyone who remembers."

When the lamp came on at dusk, it swept across the water in its steady rhythm, one white pulse every ten seconds, the same as it had been since 1931. Arthur watched it turn. He imagined a fishing boat somewhere out past the reef, a captain squinting through the spray, finding his bearings in that familiar sweep of light.

At dawn, the girl was gone. Her cup sat on the windowsill, rinsed and upturned. Beside it lay a postcard, a faded picture of Gull Point under a summer sky. On the back, in careful handwriting: *Thank you for keeping the light. —R.C.*

Arthur tucked the postcard into his coat, beside the Coast Guard's letter. Then he climbed down the ninety-two steps for the last time, and walked out into the morning, and did not look back at the light, because he did not need to. He knew it would keep burning without him.
