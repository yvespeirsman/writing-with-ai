**The Lighthouse Keeper's Last Letter**

For forty-one years, Edwin Marsh climbed the one hundred and twelve iron steps of Gull Point Lighthouse every evening at dusk, and he never once forgot to light the lamp.

The island was small, a hump of granite and sea grass twelve miles off the coast of Maine. The mainland was a smudge on the horizon on clear days and a rumor on foggy ones. Edwin had come there at twenty-two, sent by a government that needed someone willing to be alone, and he had been willing. He had told himself it was temporary. Then it was a year. Then it was his whole life.

His wife, Clara, had lasted three winters. She left on the supply boat with two suitcases and a promise to write, and she did, every week, for almost a decade. Her letters came in faded blue envelopes, filled with news of a town he barely remembered: the bakery that changed hands, the church choir where she sang alto, the grandson of a neighbor who had learned to whistle. Edwin read each one at the kitchen table, then folded it into the tin box beneath his bed. He wrote back, but his letters were short. What was there to say about waves?

Then the letters stopped.

He waited a month before he understood something had gone wrong. He sent a telegram through the radio operator on the mainland. The reply came back in three words: *Clara passed. Peacefully.*

Edwin did not go to the funeral. The weather closed in that week, and the boat couldn't come. He told himself that was why. He lit the lamp that night, same as always, and that night he sat at the top of the stairs until the sun came up.

The next spring, the Coast Guard announced the light would be automated. A machine would do what he had done. His service would end on the first of October.

In August, a girl arrived on the supply boat. She was perhaps sixteen, sent to help with the summer inventory, and her name was Ruth. She asked a great many questions. She wanted to know whether he got lonely, whether the sea ever frightened him, whether he had ever wanted to leave.

"Every day," he said, and surprised himself by meaning it.

On her last afternoon, Ruth found the tin box. She didn't open it; she only asked about it, and Edwin, who had not spoken about Clara to anyone in thirty years, told her everything. The bakery. The alto voice. The whistling boy.

Ruth listened quietly. Then she said, "You never wrote her the things you didn't tell her about the sea."

"What things?"

"The things you actually saw. Not the weather. The things that made you stop."

He thought about it for a long time after the boat had gone. Then he took out a sheet of paper, and for the first time in decades he wrote without worrying about being brief.

*Clara,* he began. *The light came on tonight at 6:14. A seal was sleeping on the rocks below, and when the beam swept over it, it lifted its head as if to say hello. I wanted to tell you that every night for forty years. I think I was afraid that if I said it, I'd have to admit how much I missed you.*

He wrote for three hours. He never mailed it. He didn't need to. He had finally said it, and the lamp kept turning, and the sea kept its quiet company, and for the first time in a very long while, Edwin Marsh did not feel alone.
