The Lighthouse Keeper's Last Letter

Every morning for forty-one years, Edwin Marsh had climbed the one hundred and twelve iron steps of the Carrow Point lighthouse, trimmed the wick, polished the great lens, and watched the sea. The sea had never once asked anything of him. That was its kindness.

The Coast Authority was closing the station in October. Automated beacons, they said, mounted on steel poles at the harbor mouth, would do the work for a fraction of the cost. Edwin had received the notice in a cream-colored envelope, and he had read it twice at the kitchen table, then folded it into quarters and tucked it behind the clock where his wife used to keep the spare house key.

Margaret had been gone three winters now. She had always been the one who wrote letters. Edwin was a man of logbooks and weather notations, of terse entries like *Wind NW, 20 knots. Fog bank 3 miles out. Lamp lit 6:42 p.m.* She had filled the drawers of the cottage with envelopes addressed to sisters, cousins, a pen pal in Norway she had never met in person, and once, memorably, to the editor of the local paper about a pothole on Wren Street.

On his last night of watch, Edwin climbed the steps slowly, his knee complaining at every turn. At the top, the lamp room smelled of brass polish and paraffin, as it always had. He lit the lamp at 6:42, out of habit, though the sun still hung pale above the water.

Then he sat down on the wooden stool beside the lens and took out a sheet of paper.

*Dear Maggie,* he wrote, and stopped, because it felt absurd to address a letter to someone who was not there to read it. He kept going anyway.

*The notice came today. Forty-one years, and they'll switch me off with a memo. I suppose I should be angry. I was, for about an hour. Then I went out on the rocks and watched the gulls and thought about how you used to say the light was for the ships, but the keeper was for the light, and nobody ever thought to ask who kept the keeper.*

*I kept the light. I kept it through the storm of '79 when the gallery window cracked and we nearly lost the lens. I kept it the winter you were sick and I drove to the mainland every Tuesday for treatments and came back in the dark to climb these steps. I kept it, I think, because it gave me a reason to get up. The ships never knew my name. That was fine. I knew theirs.*

*Do you remember the trawler, the Ellen Sayer, that came in with her engine dead? You said I was too soft on the crew, letting them stay in the boathouse when the surf was up. I said a man should be able to sleep knowing somebody was watching the water. I think that's what I've been doing. Watching.*

He paused and looked out the window. A single freighter was crawling along the horizon, its lights small and steady. Somewhere out there a captain was checking a chart, trusting a beam he would never see up close, the way Edwin had trusted the rocks and the tides for four decades.

*The new beacons will do the job, I expect,* he wrote. *They're probably better than me. They don't get tired or sad or stop to watch a sunset that's gone orange over the water. But I hope someone still writes the logbook for them. I hope someone notices when the light is lit.*

*I'm going to sign off now. The sun's nearly down and I have to climb back to the lamp at dusk.*

*Your Edwin.*

He folded the letter carefully, then unfolded it again and added one more line beneath his name, in smaller handwriting:

*Lamp lit 6:42 p.m. Visibility excellent. All quiet.*

Edwin tucked the letter into his coat, descended the one hundred and twelve steps for the last time, and walked out onto the rocks where the wind smelled of salt and the water rolled in, patient and endless, beneath a sky going gold.

Behind him, the light turned on, sweeping its long, faithful arc across the dark water, as it had every night for forty-one years.
