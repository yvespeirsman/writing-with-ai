The Lighthouse Keeper's Last Letter

For forty-one years, Elias Brandt climbed the one hundred and twelve iron steps of the Carrow Point lighthouse every evening at dusk, lit the great lamp, and turned the lens so its beam could sweep across the black water and warn the ships away from the rocks.

He was good at it. He was good at most things that required patience, and patience was the whole job. Trim the wick. Polish the brass. Wind the clockwork that turned the light. Log the weather in a leather ledger in neat, slanted handwriting. The sea did not care about you, he liked to tell his wife, but it respected a man who showed up on time.

His wife, Marta, had died eleven winters ago. The lighthouse had been quieter since. Elias did not mind the quiet so much as he minded the particular shape it took in the small cottage at the base of the cliff, where her chair still sat angled toward the window and her knitting still lay folded in its basket, unfinished, a sleeve of gray wool that would never become a sweater.

The letter arrived on a Tuesday in October, carried up the path by the supply boy, Tomas, who was nineteen and talked too much and had a habit of whistling off-key.

"Mail, Mr. Brandt. Looks official."

It was official. The Northern Lights Authority was decommissioning Carrow Point. A new automated beacon would be installed on the eastern shoal by spring. The keeper's position would be eliminated. His pension, the letter assured him in careful, regretful language, would be honored in full. They thanked him for his decades of service.

Elias read it twice standing in the doorway of the lamp room, the wind pushing at his coat. Then he folded it along its creases and put it in his breast pocket, and he climbed the one hundred and twelve steps, and he lit the lamp, because it was dusk and that was what he did.

---

He did not sleep that night. Somewhere past three, he took out a sheet of paper and began to write.

He wrote to the Authority first, a short and dignified reply saying he had received their notice and would comply. Then he sat for a long time looking at the blank space below.

He wrote instead to Marta, which was a thing he had not done before, though he had spoken to her every day for eleven years.

*My dearest,*

*They are turning off the light. Not tonight, not this month, but in the spring. A machine will do what I have done. I suppose I should be angry, or sad, but mostly I feel as if I have been standing at a window for forty-one years waiting for someone to come up the path, and now someone has told me there is no one coming, and that I may go.*

*You would have laughed at me. You always said I loved that lamp more than I loved you. I told you that wasn't true, but I think it was a little true. The lamp never asked me questions I couldn't answer. It only needed me to be there.*

*I found your sleeve this morning. The gray one. I started to finish it, and then I stopped, because I did not know how the pattern went, and you were the only one who ever did. Forgive me for leaving it so long.*

He stopped. The pen hovered. Outside, faintly, the beam swept across the window of the cottage, pale and patient, as it had every night for four decades. He had not been there to turn it on this evening; he had been too lost in his own thoughts. But the clockwork had kept going, the way it always did, and the light had come on by itself.

He frowned at that, then smiled a little, a crooked smile.

*The light came on tonight without me,* he wrote. *I thought I should have been alarmed. Instead I found I was glad. It means the thing I loved was built well enough to keep going, even when I stepped away for a minute. Maybe that's all any of us are for. To build something that keeps going after we've climbed down the stairs.*

*I'm going to finish the sleeve. I think I can work out the pattern from the stitches you left. And then I'm going to take a walk down to the harbor and see who is out there in the boats. There is more sea than this point, and I have been looking at only one piece of it for a very long time.*

*Keep the light for me, love. Just for tonight.*

*E.*

He folded the letter, sealed it, and set it on the mantel beside her photograph.

In the morning, Tomas whistled his way up the path with the supply crate and found the keeper sitting on the cottage step with a half-finished gray sleeve in his lap, the needles clumsy in his big, careful hands, a row of uneven stitches drawn tight across the wool.

"Mr. Brandt? You all right?"

Elias looked up. The October sun was on the water, and for once he was not thinking about the rocks.

"Tomas," he said, "do you know how to knit?"

"No, sir."

"Good. Then neither of us will be any use at it. Sit down anyway. It's a long job, and I could use the company."

---

*The lighthouse at Carrow Point was decommissioned the following April. The lamp was dark for the first time in ninety-three years. Visitors sometimes asked the old keeper, by then living in a small house in the harbor town, whether he missed it.*

*He would tell them that he missed the climbing, and the brass, and the ledger. But mostly, he said, he missed the feeling of being needed by something that could not say thank you.*

*And then he would smile, and say that he had found, late in life, that there were people who could say it, and that it was a far better thing to hear.*
