The Lighthouse Keeper's Last Letter

For forty-one years, Eleanor Vance climbed the spiral stairs of the Blackrock Lighthouse at dusk, wound the great lamp, and watched the sea swallow the sun. She had come to the island as a bride of nineteen, following a man who loved the sea more than he loved her, and when he drowned in a winter storm, she stayed. There was nowhere else she knew how to be.

The island had one ferry a week, one store, and sixty-three residents who had long since stopped asking her to come to town. They left her alone, and she left them alone, and the lamp turned through the dark like a patient heartbeat.

On the first cold morning of October, a letter arrived on the ferry. It was addressed in handwriting she had not seen in thirty years: her daughter's.

Eleanor had not spoken to Margaret since the funeral of her husband, when Margaret, twenty-two and furious, had told her that a woman could not live her whole life for a light that never thanked her. Eleanor had said something unforgivable in return. Neither of them had written since.

She sat at the kitchen table for a long time before she opened it.

*Mum,*

*I'm not writing to ask for anything. I'm writing because the doctor says I have perhaps a year, and I found that I didn't want to leave with the last word being the one we said at Dad's funeral. I was wrong about the light. I think I was wrong about a lot of things. I have two children now. Eleanor, after your granddaughter, is seven and she asks about you. I have never known what to tell her.*

*If you want to come, there is a room made up for you. If you don't, I understand. I only wanted you to know.*

*Your daughter,*
*Margaret*

Eleanor read it three times. Then she folded it carefully and set it beside the brass barometer on the windowsill, where it caught the grey light.

That evening she climbed the stairs as she always did. At the top, she paused with her hand on the cold iron railing and looked out over the water. The lamp was warm beneath her palm. For the first time in forty-one years, she considered that the light had never needed her to stay. It had only needed her to keep showing up, and she had mistaken the habit for a duty, and the duty for her whole life.

She wound the mechanism, lit the lamp, and watched its beam sweep across the waves, steady and bright, reaching out to ships that would pass by in the night.

Then she went back down the stairs, packed a single suitcase, and wrote a reply on the back of the old ferry schedule.

*Margaret,*

*Tell Eleanor her grandmother is coming on Tuesday's boat. And tell her the lighthouse taught me how to find my way home, even if it took me a long time to see the shore.*

*Mum*

The ferry came on Tuesday. Eleanor was waiting on the dock, one hand on her suitcase, the other shading her eyes against the wind as the boat drew closer across the grey water, and for once she did not look back at the light.
