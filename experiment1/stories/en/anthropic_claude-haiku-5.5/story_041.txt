The Lighthouse Keeper's Last Letter

The storm came in off the Atlantic on a Tuesday, the way storms always did on that stretch of coast, with no warning but a change in the gulls. Marguerite Vance had lived at the Halden Point light for thirty-one years, long enough to read the sky without thinking, and she knew by the afternoon that this one would be bad.

She climbed the spiral stairs one last time before dark, her knees complaining at every turn, and checked the lamp. The great lens turned on its brass bearing, steady as a heartbeat. Her husband, Thomas, had polished those brass fittings every Sunday for the first decade they were married. After he died, she had kept the ritual alone, mostly out of stubbornness and partly because the lighthouse had become the only place where she felt him still near.

Below her, the supply boat was late. It was always late now. The mainland had been pulling resources from the outer stations for years, and the last relief keeper had been reassigned in the spring. Marguerite had been told the light would be automated by the new year. She had not argued. There was no one left to argue with.

By nine o'clock the wind was screaming through the gallery rails, and rain came sideways against the glass. She sat at the small oak desk in the watch room with a kerosene lamp beside her and a sheet of paper in front of her, and she began to write.

*To whoever comes after me,*

*The light has never once gone dark on my watch. I want you to know that, because I suspect you will hear otherwise from people who never climbed these stairs in January. Every night for thirty-one years, I wound the clockwork, trimmed the wick, and turned the lens by hand whenever the motor failed, which was more often than the engineers in the city ever admitted.*

*I am writing this because tonight I am not sure I can make it up the stairs again. My hip has been bad since October, and the doctor in town says surgery, but surgery means leaving, and leaving means the light goes to someone who has never watched a ship turn toward it in fog and understood that it was looking back at them.*

She paused, listening. Over the howl of the wind she heard something else, faint and irregular, a horn perhaps. She stood, gripping the desk, and went to the window. Nothing but black water and white spray.

Then she saw it: a small red running light, low on the water, drifting toward the rocks at Caul's Teeth. A fishing boat, and it had no business being out in this.

Marguerite did not think. She went down the stairs faster than she had in years, pain forgotten, and hauled open the lamp room door. The lens was already turning. She checked the signal, adjusted the shutter to the emergency pattern her husband had taught her, and watched the beam sweep out over the sea, bright and unwavering.

The red light stopped drifting. It turned. Slowly, painfully, it came around, following the beam toward the channel.

She kept the light burning until the sky grey-lined with dawn, when the wind finally eased and the boat, battered but whole, limped past the point and into the harbor. A man on the deck raised his hand to the tower. Marguerite raised hers back.

Later, when the relief boat finally arrived and the young officer climbed the stairs with his clipboard and his apologies, he found her asleep in the watch room chair, one hand resting on an unfinished letter. He read it quietly, then folded it and set it carefully in his coat pocket.

He finished the sentence for her, in the margin, in pencil:

*The light has never gone dark. It will not start now.*
