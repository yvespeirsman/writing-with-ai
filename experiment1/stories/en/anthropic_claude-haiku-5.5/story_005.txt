The Lighthouse Keeper's Last Letter

For forty-one years, Eleanor Marsh climbed the one hundred and twelve iron steps of the Gull Point lighthouse every evening at dusk. She trimmed the wick, polished the lens, and turned the great brass crank that set the beam sweeping across the black water. Ships used it to find their way past the rocks. Nobody on those ships ever knew her name, and that was how she liked it.

The island was small, a hook of granite and sea grass at the edge of the map, and in winter the wind came off the Atlantic with teeth. Eleanor had come to it at twenty-two, a widow's daughter with a letter of appointment and one trunk, and she had stayed when the supply boats grew less frequent and the mainland families moved away. She stayed when the automation men came to talk about replacing her with a motor and a clock. She stayed through two hurricanes and one fire in the oil room. Mostly she stayed because the light needed someone to keep it, and she had never been very good at leaving things unfinished.

In her forty-second autumn, a letter arrived by the last boat of the season.

It was addressed in a looping hand she didn't recognize: *To the Keeper of Gull Point Light.* Inside was a single sheet, folded many times, smelling faintly of cedar.

*Dear Keeper,*

*You won't remember me. Thirty-eight years ago I was a boy of nine, travelling with my father on the ferry that ran past your island. The fog came down so thick that the captain lost all sense of where we were. Everyone was frightened. I remember standing at the rail, sure we were going to hit the rocks, and then I saw your light sweep over us, once every few seconds, steady as a heartbeat. The captain turned the wheel. We passed the point with less than a hundred yards to spare.*

*My father never believed in much, but he stood beside me at that rail and said, "Somebody is awake out there." I have thought of that sentence my whole life. I became a teacher. I have buried two friends and raised three children. Whenever I felt lost, I thought of your light.*

*I am writing because my father has died, and in clearing his papers I found a logbook from that night. He had written in it: "Keeper on Gull Point saved us. Must thank her someday." He never did. I suppose this is my way of doing it for him.*

*Thank you for being awake.*

*—Daniel Achebe*

Eleanor read it three times standing by the window. Then she sat down on the cot in the corner of the watch room, the letter in her lap, and cried in a way she had not cried since her mother's funeral.

She had kept the light for forty-one years. She had logged weather, tide, and passing vessels in neat columns. Some nights she had wondered whether anyone noticed at all, or whether she was simply a pair of hands doing a job that a machine could do cheaper. She had never once imagined that a single night's work might echo for decades in the life of a boy she'd never seen.

The next evening, as the sun slid into the sea, she climbed the one hundred and twelve steps as she always had. She trimmed the wick. She polished the lens until it gleamed. She turned the crank, and the beam swept out across the water, steady and bright.

Somewhere out in the dark, a boat she would never see was finding its way home.

She took out her logbook, uncapped her pen, and wrote the date. Then, beneath the weather and the tide, in the same plain hand she had used for four decades, she added a single line:

*Light burning. Someone is awake out here.*
