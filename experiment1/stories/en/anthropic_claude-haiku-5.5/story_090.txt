The Lighthouse Keeper's Last Letter

For forty-one years, Eleanor Vance had climbed the one hundred and twelve iron steps of the Marrow Point lighthouse every evening at dusk. She lit the lamp, wound the clockwork that turned the great lens, and watched the ships slide past the rocks like careful thoughts.

The town below had grown small over the decades. The cannery closed in '79, the ferry stopped in '92, and now only a few dozen households clung to the crooked streets. But the light still turned, and Eleanor took some quiet pride in that.

The letter arrived on a Tuesday in October, carried up by the young postal clerk, Danny Okafor, who was too out of breath to speak when he reached the top.

"Sit down before you fall down," Eleanor told him, and he did, gratefully, on the cold iron bench by the railing.

The envelope was thick and cream-colored, addressed in handwriting she had not seen in thirty years.

She opened it standing by the window, where the sea wind pulled at her gray braid.

*Nora,*

*I know you've probably thrown this away unread. I wouldn't blame you. But if you're reading it, then the world has done what it does and carried it to you anyway.*

*I'm dying, Ellie. The doctors gave me until spring. I'm not writing to ask forgiveness. I know what I did when we were twenty-two, leaving you at the pier with the two tickets. I know you waited until the last whistle.*

*There is something I never told you. The night I left, I wasn't running from you. I was running from a debt my father owed the Harlan brothers, and they came for him the night before we were to sail. I didn't leave because I stopped loving you. I left because I thought they would hurt you to get to me.*

*I was wrong, I think. Or maybe I was a coward who told himself a noble story. I have spent most of my life deciding which it was.*

*The enclosed key opens the boathouse at the foot of your cliff. I had the caretaker keep it dry. Inside there is a box with everything I could never say, and the ring I bought you that night. You never have to come. But I wanted you to know it was there.*

*Forever, or what's left of it,*
*Thomas*

Eleanor read it twice. Then she folded it along its creases and tucked it into the pocket of her wool coat.

"Bad news?" Danny asked softly.

"Old news," she said. "Finally arriving."

She looked out at the water, where the evening ferry-that-no-longer-ran would have crossed, and at the beam she had sent across the dark for four decades. She thought of the boathouse, the cold key in her hand, and the thirty years she had spent deciding not to care.

"Danny," she said, "do you know how to run a lamp?"

He blinked. "Ma'am?"

"The light has to be lit by sundown. The clockwork needs winding every four hours through the night. There's a log. Write down every time." She pressed the brass winding key into his palm. "I'll be back before the morning ships."

"Where are you going?"

Eleanor pulled on her gloves and started down the one hundred and twelve steps, faster than she had in years.

"To answer a letter," she said.

---

The boathouse door groaned open. Inside, under a fine layer of dust and a tarp that smelled of salt, sat a wooden box with her name carved into the lid, in handwriting that had grown shakier with each letter.

She knelt, lifted the lid, and for the first time in thirty years, Eleanor Vance let herself cry.
