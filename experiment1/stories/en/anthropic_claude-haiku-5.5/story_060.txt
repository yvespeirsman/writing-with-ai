The Lighthouse Keeper's Last Letter

The fog came in on a Tuesday, the way it always did in October, rolling over the gray water of Harrow Bay until the lighthouse disappeared into it like a candle swallowed by milk.

Thomas Wren had kept the light at Harrow Point for thirty-one years. He knew the sound of the foghorn the way other men knew their own heartbeats, knew which gulls screamed before a storm and which ones only pretended. He knew that the rocks at the eastern edge of the bay had taken eleven ships since 1887, and that the town had never once forgotten a single name.

This was his last season. The automated beacon was being installed on the mainland next spring, and the Coast Guard had sent a polite letter thanking him for his service. He had folded it into quarters and tucked it behind the clock on the kitchen wall, where it sat beside a photograph of his wife, Marian, who had died four winters ago, and a child's drawing of a lighthouse that his granddaughter had sent him, the sun in the corner wearing sunglasses.

That evening, with the fog pressing against the glass of the lantern room, Thomas climbed the iron stairs one last time to check the lens. Each step rang beneath his boots. He wiped the glass with a soft cloth, polished the brass, trimmed the wick of the backup lamp out of habit, though the machinery was perfect and had been for decades.

Halfway through his inspection, the radio crackled.

"Harrow Point, Harrow Point, this is the *净 Mariner*—" The signal broke into static, then came back thin and frightened. "We've lost our engine. We're drifting toward the eastern shelf. Can you see us?"

Thomas grabbed the handset. "*Mariner*, this is Harrow Point. I can't see you through this fog. Can you give me your position?"

"Northeast of the buoy. I think. I can hear the surf. God, I can hear it."

He shut his eyes and listened. Beneath the static, he heard it too: the low, hungry breathing of the water against stone. He knew that sound. He had spent his whole life listening to it.

He pulled the lever that started the foghorn. The long, mournful note rolled out over the bay, and he kept it going, one blast every thirty seconds, while he ran the numbers in his head the way Marian had taught him to do with crossword puzzles, patient and unhurried. Wind from the southwest. Tide coming in. The *Mariner* had perhaps forty minutes before the shelf.

"*Mariner*, keep your horn sounding if you can. I'm going to give you a bearing. Turn your bow toward my voice. Keep the sound on your left."

"We don't have power for the horn."

"Then shout. Anybody on that deck, shout. I'll hear you."

For a long minute there was only the foghorn and the surf. Then, faint and ragged, came voices, a man calling out, then several at once, a child's high cry in the middle of it all.

Thomas leaned into the mic and began to talk. He did not stop. He described the light, the color of the paint, the number of steps to the gallery, the smell of the coffee he had burned that morning. He talked the way he would have read aloud to Marian in the hospital, steady and warm, anything at all so long as it kept them listening and moving toward the sound.

Forty minutes later, a dark shape slid out of the fog a hundred yards off the eastern shelf, low in the water, its running lights dead, its hull scraped but whole. Thomas watched it come through the big lens he had polished that afternoon, and he did not realize he was weeping until the boat's spotlight found the tower and held it, the way one hand reaches for another in the dark.

They tied up at the harbor in the small hours. The captain, a heavyset woman with salt in her hair, climbed the lighthouse to thank him, and found him sitting on the bottom step with a cup of cold coffee, watching the beam sweep patiently across the water.

"You saved us," she said.

Thomas shook his head. "The light did. It just keeps doing what it's meant to do."

She laughed softly. "Well, somebody has to be out here to keep it going."

After she left, he sat a long while in the quiet. Then he went up to the kitchen, unfolded the Coast Guard letter, and smoothed it flat on the table. Beside it, he set down a fresh sheet of paper and began to write.

*Dear Marian,* he wrote. *The fog came in tonight. I think I finally understand what you meant about staying.*

Outside, the beam kept turning, and the sea, for once, went quiet.
