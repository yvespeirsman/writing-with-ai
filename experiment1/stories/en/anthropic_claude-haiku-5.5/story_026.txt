The Lighthouse Keeper's Last Letter

For forty-one years, Margaret Hale had climbed the one hundred and twelve iron steps of the Carrick Point lighthouse every evening at dusk, and every evening she had lit the lamp. She had done it through gales that rattled the glass, through fog so thick she could not see the railing, and through the winter her husband drowned off the eastern rocks. The light had never once failed while she was its keeper.

Now the Coast Authority wanted it automated, and the letter announcing it sat unopened on the kitchen table beside her tea, going cold.

She did not open it because she already knew what it said. The new lamp would be run by a timer and a sensor in a steel box. A supply boat would come once a month to check the batteries. Nobody would need to climb the stairs anymore.

On the last night of her service, she climbed anyway.

The wind was gentle for once, and the sea below was the color of pewter, slow and patient. She reached the top, her knees complaining at every step, and stood among the great lenses that her predecessors had polished and she had polished after them. She touched the brass rail. It was warm from the afternoon sun.

She lit the lamp one final time, and the beam swept out over the water, reaching the far buoy and the distant headland and the small fishing village huddled behind the dunes.

Down in the village, a boy named Tomas stood on his grandmother's porch, watching the light come on the way he had every night of his life. He had never thought about it. It was simply there, the way the tide was there.

Tonight, though, he noticed. He noticed that the beam turned with a steady rhythm, that it paused for a heartbeat over the water each time it passed the village, as if checking that everyone was home. He turned to his grandmother.

"Who's up there?"

"Margaret Hale," she said. "She's been up there longer than your mother has been alive."

"Is she going to stop?"

"Tomorrow, the machines take over." The old woman pulled her shawl tighter. "Some things don't need people to do them. But some things need someone to care whether they get done."

Tomas watched the light turn, and turn again.

At the top of the tower, Margaret watched it too, from the outside gallery, until the sun was gone and the stars came out over the water. Then she went down the one hundred and twelve steps for the last time, and she did not look back, because she had never needed to. The light would keep going, and so would the village, and so would the boy who had finally seen it.
