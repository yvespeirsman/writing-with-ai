The Lighthouse Keeper's Last Letter

For forty-one years, Eleanor Vance climbed the spiral stairs of the Gannet Point lighthouse every evening at dusk. She wound the clockwork, trimmed the wick, and turned the great lens so its beam could sweep across the black water and warn the ships away from the rocks.

She had come to the island as a young bride, following her husband Thomas, who had taken the post because it paid well and asked little of a man except that he be patient. Thomas had loved the sea the way some men love a horse: with respect, with wariness, and with a deep certainty that it would someday throw him. It did. A winter storm took him off the eastern breakwater when Eleanor was thirty-four, and she was left with the lamp, a cottage full of his unfinished carpentry, and a silence the wind never quite filled.

She kept the light burning. There was no one else to do it.

Over the years the island emptied. The fishing families moved inland to the mainland towns, one boat and one household at a time, until only Eleanor remained, along with the gulls and the occasional supply boat that dropped off flour, oil, and the newspaper three weeks late. The mainland sent her a pension and a polite letter every spring reminding her that the post might soon be automated. She wrote back each time that the light did not need automating. It needed her.

On the night of her forty-first year, a fog rolled in so thick she could not see the railing from the gallery. She lit the lamp anyway and wound the mechanism by hand, counting the turns the way Thomas used to, aloud, to keep his rhythm.

Near midnight, through the gray murk, she heard an engine. Small, struggling, coughing. Then a voice calling out across the water, young and frightened.

"Light! Is that a light? Hello!"

Eleanor leaned over the rail and shouted until her throat burned, guiding the boat with her voice where the lamp could not reach. She had no radio that worked anymore, no flare she trusted. She had only the beam, which she kept turning, and her voice, which she kept raising.

The boat came around the point just after one in the morning, its hull scraping the shallows of the small cove below the cliffs. Two figures climbed onto the rocks, soaked and shaking. A boy of perhaps seventeen and a girl a little younger, both from the mainland, both lost after their father's boat had failed in the squall. Eleanor brought them up the cliff path with a lantern and sat them by the stove in the keeper's cottage, wrapped in Thomas's old wool blankets, which still smelled faintly of cedar and tobacco.

The girl stared at the walls. "Do you live here all alone?"

"I do," Eleanor said.

"Isn't it lonely?"

Eleanor considered this. She thought of the forty-one years, the unfinished chairs, the quiet that had teeth some nights. She thought of the beam sweeping the water every dusk, and of the boats that had passed safely because she had been there to turn the lens.

"Sometimes," she said. "But the light has to be kept by someone. It's a kind of promise. The sea is going to be dark, and people are going to be out in it. Someone has to show up."

In the morning the fog lifted, and the mainland coast guard came for the children. The boy shook her hand with both of his. The girl hugged her without asking.

That evening, Eleanor climbed the spiral stairs as she always had, wound the clockwork, and turned the great lens toward the water. Then, because the day had been long and her hands were tired, she sat down on the gallery floor and wrote a letter to the lighthouse board.

*Do not automate the light,* she wrote. *I will keep it for as long as I am able. And if I am not able, I trust that someone else will climb these stairs when the fog comes in, because someone always should.*

She sealed it, set it on the table, and went back to the lamp, which had never once failed to show the way home.
