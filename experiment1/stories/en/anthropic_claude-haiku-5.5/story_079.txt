The Lighthouse Keeper's Last Letter

For forty-one years, Eleanor Vance climbed the spiral stairs of the Gull Point lighthouse every evening at dusk. She wound the clockwork, trimmed the wick, and turned the great lens so its beam could sweep across the black water and warn the ships away from the rocks.

She had come to the island as a young widow, grieving and restless, looking for somewhere the world would leave her alone. The island had only forty residents then, and fewer each winter. The mainland supply boat came twice a month, bringing flour, paraffin, newspapers three days old, and sometimes a letter.

Her brother Thomas wrote to her every month for thirty-eight of those years. He lived in the city, worked as a clerk, married late, and buried his wife young. His letters were short and careful, full of weather and small news. She answered them just as carefully. Neither of them ever wrote the thing that mattered, the thing about their father and the quarrel that had sent Eleanor north in the first place.

In the spring of her forty-second year on the island, the letters stopped.

She waited through the summer. The supply boat came and went, and no envelope with Thomas's cramped handwriting appeared. In autumn she wrote to him, and her letter came back stamped *Return to Sender* in red ink. She sat at her kitchen table a long while after that, the kettle going cold beside her.

The mainland, she discovered, was a place that moved on without you. Thomas had moved to a boarding house across the river. Then he had gone into the hospital on Carver Street. Then, in the way of things, he had died, and his neighbor had written a kind but brief note to the lighthouse authority, which had forwarded it to the island office, which had sent it on the next supply boat, two months too late for her to have done anything about it.

Eleanor read the note standing on the lookout gallery, the wind tugging at her skirt. Below her, the sea hammered the rocks. Above, the lamp was not yet lit, but she could feel its weight, the way she always could, a thing that needed tending whether or not anyone was grateful for it.

That evening she climbed the stairs as usual. She wound the clockwork. She trimmed the wick and struck the match. The lamp caught, and the great lens began its slow turn, throwing its light out over the water in a long bright arc.

She had thought, when she first came here, that she was hiding. Now, watching the light sweep out and return, reach and return, she understood that she had been keeping watch all along. Not for Thomas, not for their father, but for anyone out there in the dark who needed to know where the rocks were.

She pulled out a fresh sheet of paper and began a letter. She didn't address it to anyone. She simply wrote the truth she had never managed to say: *I was wrong. I am sorry. I think of you every night when the light turns.*

Then she folded it, tucked it into the drawer with the others, and went back up to the lamp, where the beam kept sweeping across the water, faithful and unhurried, as it had for forty-one years and would for as long as she had the strength to climb.
