The Lighthouse Keeper's Last Letter

For forty-one years, Eleanor Vance climbed the one hundred and twelve iron steps of the Marrow Point lighthouse every evening at dusk. She trimmed the wick, polished the lens, and turned the great brass crank that set the light spinning. Ships out past the reef had depended on that light since before she was born, and she never once let it go dark.

The island had only one other resident, a boy named Teodor who came across on the supply boat every second Thursday with flour, lamp oil, and whatever news the mainland had decided was worth sending. He was seventeen that spring, all elbows and questions, and he had a habit of reading aloud from whatever book he'd brought, even when no one asked.

"You know," he said one afternoon, leaning against the doorframe of the keeper's cottage, "the mail boat's been late three times this month."

"The sea is slow to forgive anyone," Eleanor said, not looking up from her mending.

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one I've got."

He grinned, and she pretended not to find it charming.

What she did not tell him was that she had been writing letters. Every week for the past two years, she had written to a man named Anselm Hale, a cartographer she had known briefly in her twenties, before the sea and duty had pulled them in opposite directions. She had never sent them. They sat in a tin box beneath her bed, forty-two envelopes sealed and addressed to a street in the city that might not even exist anymore.

That night, a storm came in from the west faster than the almanac had promised. Eleanor climbed the steps in the rain, her lungs burning, and lit the lamp as the wind hammered the glass. Below, she could see the dark shapes of two fishing boats struggling toward the harbor mouth. She turned the crank until her hands bled through her gloves.

Teodor appeared at the top of the stairs, soaked and breathless. "The radio says the mail boat went down near the reef," he gasped. "Two men aboard, one of them named Hale."

Eleanor stopped turning the crank.

"Hale," she repeated. "Anselm Hale?"

"I don't know his first name. They only said Hale."

The lamp swept on, steady and indifferent, across the black water. Eleanor stared at the spinning light for a long moment. Then she set her jaw, and turned the crank again.

"Then we keep the light burning," she said. "Whatever else happens, we keep it burning."

They worked through the night together, the boy feeding oil and the old woman turning the crank, until the storm finally broke at dawn. The fishing boats came into harbor, battered but whole. The mail boat was never found.

In the weeks that followed, Eleanor took the tin box from beneath her bed and read every letter aloud to Teodor, one by one, sitting by the window with the sea going silver below. When she finished the last one, she folded it carefully and set it on the sill.

"He would have been seventy-three this year," she said quietly. "I suppose I'd have liked to tell him that the light never went out."

Teodor said nothing. He only reached over and held her hand, and together they watched the horizon, where the ships kept coming home.
