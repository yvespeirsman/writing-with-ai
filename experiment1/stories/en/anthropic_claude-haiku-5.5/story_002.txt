The Lighthouse Keeper's Last Letter

For forty-one years, Eleanor Vance climbed the one hundred and twelve iron steps of the Cape Merrow lighthouse every evening at dusk, and every evening she lit the lamp. She had done it through blizzards that buried the railing in ice, through a summer when the gulls nested so thick on the gallery that she had to sweep feathers before she could reach the glass, and through the winter her husband Thomas died, when she lit the lamp with hands so numb she could barely feel the matches.

The town below had stopped expecting anything of her long ago. The ferry men waved when they passed. The children who once dared each other to touch the lighthouse door had grown up and moved away, and their children had never dared anything at all. The new harbormaster, a brisk young man named Okafor, had arrived three years earlier with a tablet full of automated systems and a plan to replace the old lamp with a satellite-linked beacon that needed no keeper.

"It's a matter of efficiency, Mrs. Vance," he had said, not unkindly, standing in her kitchen with his clipboard. "The Coast Guard approved it last spring. Your position will be retired in the autumn."

She had nodded and poured him tea he did not drink.

Now autumn was coming, and Eleanor sat at her kitchen table with a sheet of thick cream paper and a pen that kept skipping. She had been trying to write a letter for three days. Not to Okafor, and not to the Coast Guard. To the sea itself, she supposed, or to the ships that had passed through her light across four decades and never once known her name.

*To whoever steers by this light,*

*I have kept it burning for you since before you were born. I do not know your faces. I know only the shape of your passing: a freighter's slow dark bulk, a fishing boat's bobbing lanterns, the white wake of a ferry making its nightly crossing with a child asleep against a window. I have watched you come out of the fog like something surfacing from a dream, and I have watched you slip back into it, and I have never once asked where you were going.*

*I hope you got there.*

She stopped, rereading the lines, and found her throat tight in a way that surprised her.

On the last evening before the change, Eleanor climbed the steps slowly, one hand on the cold rail. The wind was sharp and smelled of salt and cut hay from the farms inland. Below, the harbor lights were winking on one by one, and the water had gone the deep pewter color it turned just before full dark.

She reached the lamp room and stood for a moment with her palm against the great lens, which had been polished so many times the brass frame had worn smooth as river stone.

Then she lit it.

The beam swept out over the water, slow and steady, the way it always had. She watched it cross the bay, touch the breakwater, pass over the empty pier where Thomas used to sit with his fishing rod and his terrible thermos of coffee, and reach out past the rocks toward the open dark.

Far out, a single running light answered. Green, then steady. A boat she didn't recognize, heading in.

Eleanor pressed her hand to her chest and found, to her own astonishment, that she was smiling.

"I see you," she said quietly, to no one at all. "Come on home."

She stayed until the boat was safely past the rocks and into the harbor, until its engine went silent and a voice called out a greeting to someone on the dock. Then she turned the lamp down to its evening glow, gathered her coat, and started down the one hundred and twelve steps for what would be the last time as keeper.

At the bottom, she paused and looked back up at the light, turning patiently against the stars.

It would keep burning without her now. That was the strange mercy of it. Some things, once begun with enough care, could go on for a while even after the hands that tended them had let go.

She folded the unfinished letter into her coat pocket, meaning to finish it tomorrow, and walked home along the shore with the sea murmuring beside her in the dark.
