**The Last Lamplighter of Harrow Lane**

Every evening at a quarter to seven, Tobias Crane climbed the iron ladder bolted to the lamppost at the corner of Harrow Lane and Pell Street, struck his brass key against the glass, and coaxed the gas to life. He had done it for forty-one years. The city had switched to electric lamps a decade ago, and the council had sent a polite letter asking him to retire. He had thanked them and kept climbing.

Nobody on Harrow Lane argued with him. The children called him the Lamplighter, and the older residents tipped their hats when they passed. Mrs. Okonkwo at number nine left a thermos of tea on his step every winter. The baker gave him day-old rolls. It was an arrangement no one had ever discussed.

What nobody knew was why he kept the one lamp, the one at the corner, burning when the others had gone dark.

Fifty years earlier, a girl named Margaret Hale had walked home along Harrow Lane every night from the textile mill, her shawl pulled tight, her boots scuffing the cobbles. Tobias was nineteen and newly apprenticed to his uncle, who had the lamp route then. He lit the corner lamp at a quarter to seven, and she passed beneath it at seven exactly, and every night for a year he watched for her from the top of the ladder.

He never said a word to her. He was too shy, and then she was engaged to a clerk from the bank, and then she was gone, moved to the coast with her new husband. Tobias married a woman named Ruth, who laughed at his awkward jokes and loved him fiercely for forty years, and he was content. He truly was. But on winter evenings, when the gas hissed and the light pooled gold on the wet stones, he sometimes thought of the girl with the shawl.

Ruth had known. She'd found him once, years ago, standing at the corner with his hand on the ladder long after the lamp was lit. She hadn't scolded him. She'd only said, "Go on, then, light it for her. I don't mind. Someone should."

Ruth had been gone two winters now.

On a cold Tuesday in November, Tobias climbed the ladder as he always did. His knees ached, and his breath clouded in the dusk. He struck the key, and the lamp caught with a soft *whump*.

When he climbed down, there was a woman standing beneath it, her shawl pulled tight against the wind. She was older, her hair the color of ash, but she looked up at the lamp with the same quiet attention he remembered.

"They told me you were still doing this," she said. "I didn't believe them."

Tobias opened his mouth, found nothing in it, and tried again. "Margaret?"

She smiled, and it was the same smile. "I was in the city for my sister's funeral. I thought I'd walk the old way home."

They stood together under the lamp while the evening deepened around them. Somewhere a window opened, and a child called out, and the baker's bell rang down the lane.

"You never said anything," Margaret told him gently. "Back then."

"No," said Tobias. "I didn't."

"Well," she said, tucking her hands into her sleeves, "I'm not in any hurry."

And for the first time in two winters, Tobias Crane laughed.
