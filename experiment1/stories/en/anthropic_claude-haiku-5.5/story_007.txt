The Lighthouse Keeper's Last Letter

For forty-one years, Eleanor Vance climbed the one hundred and twelve iron steps of the Carrow Point lighthouse every evening at dusk, and every evening she lit the great lamp that guided ships past the jagged rocks the locals called the Teeth.

She had come to Carrow Point at twenty-three, a bride who had married a fisherman's son named Thomas, and she had stayed after the sea took him. The town was small enough that everyone knew her, and quiet enough that she could hear the gulls from her kitchen window. She never minded the solitude. The light needed tending, and tending it gave her days a shape.

In her sixty-fourth year, the government announced that Carrow Point would be automated. A machine would do what she had done, a mechanical eye that never blinked or grew tired. The letter arrived on a Tuesday, crisp and official, thanking her for her service and offering a modest pension.

Eleanor read it twice, folded it neatly, and set it on the windowsill beside the brass telescope Thomas had given her.

That night she climbed the steps as usual. Halfway up, her knee ached, and she stopped to rest. Through the narrow window she could see the harbor below, where the lights of Carrow Point's few houses flickered like scattered coins. A boy named Samuel Pike, no more than ten, was sitting on the seawall with his fishing line, as he did most evenings. His grandfather had worked the boats with Thomas.

She lit the lamp, and its beam swept out across the water, pale and steady, the way it always had.

The next morning, Samuel came up the steps, which surprised her. He was breathing hard, clutching a folded piece of paper.

"My grandad says you're leaving," he said.

"Not yet. Sometime this year, I suppose."

He held out the paper. It was a drawing: the lighthouse, the lamp, and a little stick figure at the top with a big smile. Beneath it, in careful letters: *For the lady who keeps the light on.*

Eleanor was quiet for a long moment. Then she laughed, a small, surprised sound, and pressed her hand to her mouth.

"I'll keep it," she said. "Right beside my telescope."

Over the following months, she kept climbing the steps. She taught Samuel how to read the tide tables and how to tell a storm's approach by the color of the sky. When the automated system was finally installed, a young engineer came to show her the controls. He was polite and quick, and he spoke of efficiency and reliability.

On her last evening, Eleanor climbed the one hundred and twelve steps one final time. She stood at the top, her hand resting on the cold iron railing, and watched the sun sink into the gray Atlantic.

Then she lit the lamp, and the beam swept out across the water, pale and steady.

Below, on the seawall, a small figure stood and waved his arms high above his head.

Eleanor raised her hand in return, and she did not let go of the railing until the sky had turned completely dark.

---

The machine took over the next morning. It never missed a night. But the fishermen of Carrow Point still said, when they rounded the Teeth in fog, that they could sometimes see a woman's silhouette in the lamp room window, watching over them all the same.
