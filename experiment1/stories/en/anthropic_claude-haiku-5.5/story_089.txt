The Lighthouse Keeper's Last Letter

For forty-one years, Elias Brandt climbed the spiral stairs of the Carrow Point lighthouse every evening at dusk, wound the great clockwork mechanism, and lit the lamp that guided ships through the treacherous rocks below. He had never missed a night. Not during the storms that tore shingles from the keeper's cottage, not the winter his wife Marta died, not the summer his hands shook so badly he could barely strike a match.

The lighthouse was his life, and it was also, increasingly, a place of silence.

In the spring of his forty-second year, a letter arrived by the supply boat. The envelope was addressed in a looping, unfamiliar hand: *To the Keeper of Carrow Light.*

Inside was a single sheet of paper.

*Dear Keeper,*

*You won't remember me. Thirty-one years ago, my father was a fisherman named Tomas Vell, and on a night of fog and bad luck, his boat drifted onto the Teeth, the reef south of your point. He never came home. My mother used to say the light had been dim that night, that the ships had gone blind. I was seven. I grew up hating that lighthouse for a reason I didn't fully understand.*

*Last month I found his logbook in a box in my attic. He had written about the fog, and about the lamp. He wrote that you had been fixing the lens that week, and that the light flickered and then came back strong. He wrote: "The keeper's lamp saved us twice tonight. I'll tell him so when we dock."*

*He never docked. But I wanted you to know that he was grateful. And so, I think, was I, when I realized that the dim light my mother blamed wasn't your failure. It was a man doing his best in weather no one could control.*

*I'm sorry for the years I spent blaming you. Thank you for every night since.*

*—Ilse Vell*

Elias read the letter three times. Then he sat at the wooden table in the cottage and wept, the way he had not wept for Marta, because for the first time in thirty-one years he understood that the nights he had thought were ordinary had mattered to someone he'd never met.

That evening, he climbed the stairs as always. The mechanism turned. The lamp caught and swept its beam across the black water, and far out on the dark, a single ship's light answered it, steady and sure.

Elias stood at the glass for a long while, watching it come closer.
