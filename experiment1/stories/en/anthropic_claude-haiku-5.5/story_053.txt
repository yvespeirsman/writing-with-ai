**The Lighthouse Keeper's Last Letter**

For thirty-one years, Elias Vane climbed the iron stairs of the Harrow Point lighthouse every evening at dusk. He polished the great lens, trimmed the wicks, and turned the crank that set the light in motion. Then he sat by the window and watched ships pass in the dark, trusting that someone out there could see the warning he kept burning.

The town below had forgotten him, mostly. The automated buoys had been installed the year his wife, Margaret, died, and the harbor authority had sent a polite letter about "modernization." Elias had read it twice, folded it into his coat pocket, and kept climbing the stairs anyway.

One October evening, a storm rolled in faster than the forecast had promised. Rain hammered the glass, and the wind moaned through the gallery railings like something grieving. Elias was lighting the lamp when he saw a small fishing boat, its running lights flickering, drifting toward the rocks at the mouth of the bay.

The buoys were dark. The radio crackled with static. There was no one else.

He didn't think. He just ran.

By the time he reached the cliff path, his lungs burned and his knees screamed. He stumbled down to the shore with a flashlight in his teeth and waded into the surf until the cold stole his breath. Two young men were clinging to the wreckage of their hull, their faces white with terror. Elias grabbed the nearest one's collar and hauled him toward the rocks, then went back for the other.

When the coast guard finally arrived an hour later, they found the old keeper sitting on the wet stones, wrapped in a stranger's blanket, his hands shaking as the rescued men wept beside him.

The next morning, the harbor authority published a small notice in the local paper. It didn't mention modernization. It said simply: *The Harrow Point light was burning all night. Thank you, Mr. Vane.*

Elias read it at his kitchen table, folded the paper carefully, and set it beside Margaret's photograph.

Then he went back up the stairs, because the light was still needed, and so was he.
