The Lighthouse Keeper's Last Letter

For forty-one years, Eleanor Vance climbed the one hundred and twelve iron steps of the Carrow Point lighthouse every evening at dusk, and every evening she lit the lamp that guided ships past the jagged rocks the locals called the Teeth.

She had come to Carrow Point as a bride of twenty, following a man named Thomas who loved the sea more than he loved most people, and who had taken the keeper's post because it promised solitude. Thomas drowned in her third winter there, when a fishing boat he was helping to tow broke its line in a storm. The lighthouse stayed. So did she.

The years folded into one another the way tide folds into tide. She learned to trim the wick and polish the great lens until it threw light like scattered diamonds. She learned the names of the gulls and which ones were bold enough to steal crusts from her windowsill. She learned that the sea never truly slept, not even when it looked calm.

In the autumn of her forty-first year as keeper, the mainland council sent a letter informing her that the lighthouse would be automated. A machine would do what she had done. Her pension would be modest, and she would have to leave by the first of the month.

Eleanor read the letter twice at her kitchen table, then folded it carefully and set it beside the teapot. She did not cry. She had cried for Thomas, and the sea had taken that tear along with everything else.

That evening she climbed the steps as usual. At the top, the lens caught the last orange light of the sun, and she stood for a long while watching the water turn from gold to slate to black.

Then she noticed a small light bobbing far out past the Teeth. A rowboat, unmistakably, with a single lantern swinging from its bow. Someone was out there, trying to find the harbor in the fading light, and their lantern was nearly lost against the vast dark.

Eleanor's hands moved before her mind had finished deciding. She turned the lamp to its brightest setting, and sent the beam sweeping out over the water in its steady, patient rhythm. Long sweep. Pause. Long sweep. Pause.

The little light wavered, then turned. It drew closer, steering by her beam, until at last it slipped between the rocks and into the calm water of the harbor mouth.

Eleanor kept the lamp burning all night, as she always had.

In the morning, a young fisherman came up the iron steps, breathless and red-cheeked. He was Thomas's grandson, though she had never met him. He had come to see the lighthouse before the machines arrived, he said, and had been caught out in the dusk. "I didn't know anyone would still be watching," he told her. "I kept looking for the light, and it was the only thing I could see."

Eleanor smiled and handed him a cup of tea.

That afternoon she wrote a reply to the council. She did not argue or plead. She simply wrote: *The light has never needed to be seen by many. It only needs to be there when someone is lost. I will be here until the last ship has come home.*

She sent the letter, and then she climbed the steps once more to wait for dusk.
