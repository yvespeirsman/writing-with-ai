The Lighthouse Keeper's Last Letter

For forty-one years, Eleanor Vance climbed the spiral stairs of the Marrow Point lighthouse every evening at dusk. She carried a kerosene lamp, a tin of matches, and a thermos of coffee that had long since gone cold by morning. She never missed a night, not even the night her husband Thomas drowned three miles out, when the storm was so loud she could not hear her own thoughts.

Now the lighthouse was going automatic. A steel box with a solar panel would replace her, blinking its patient white light from a cheap aluminum tower by the road. The Coast Guard had sent a letter. Her retirement, they called it, as if she had simply stopped working rather than been erased.

On her last night, Eleanor climbed the stairs slowly, one hand on the cold iron railing. Her knees complained at every step. At the top, the great lens caught the last orange light of the sun and threw it across the water in long, trembling bars.

She lit the lamp. She wound the clockwork that turned the lens. She did everything exactly as Thomas had taught her, in the first year, when he still believed the sea could be bargained with.

Then she sat down on the wooden stool and took out the letter she had been writing for six months and never finished.

*Tom,*

*They're taking the light away tonight. I suppose you knew it would come to this. You always said the world would forget us before the water did.*

*I used to think keeping the light was about the ships. Some nights I was sure it was about you, that if I kept it burning you would see it from wherever you were and find your way home. I was foolish. I know that now. You were never lost. You simply went where the water took you, and I kept burning the light for a man who had already reached the shore.*

*But I want you to know I kept it. Every night. Through the fog and the ice and the years when no one but the gulls ever thanked me. Three thousand, one hundred and sixty-two nights, by my count. Not one missed.*

*Tonight I'm going to let it go. Not because I've stopped loving you. Because I think you'd want me to come down the stairs and walk into the town and see who's still there.*

She stopped writing. Below her, the sea breathed against the rocks.

A pair of headlights swept up the coast road. A truck pulled into the gravel lot beside the keeper's cottage, and a young man stepped out with a clipboard and a hard hat. He looked up at the tower, then waved.

Eleanor folded the letter, tucked it into her coat, and went down the stairs for the last time, leaving the lamp burning behind her.

Behind her, for the first time in forty-one years, no one was watching the light but the sea.
