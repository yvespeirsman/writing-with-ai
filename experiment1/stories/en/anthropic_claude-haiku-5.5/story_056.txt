The Lighthouse Keeper's Last Letter

For forty-one years, Margaret Hale climbed the one hundred and twelve stone steps of the Carrick Point lighthouse every evening at dusk. She trimmed the wick, polished the great lens, and turned the crank that set the mechanism turning, so its beam could sweep across the black water and warn the ships away from the rocks.

She was the last keeper. The automated systems had come to the coast a decade earlier, and the government had promised a clean, modern light that needed no human hands. But the storms that rolled in off the Atlantic had a way of knocking out power, and so the town of Carrick Harbor kept Margaret on, in a small room at the base of the tower, with her cat Biscuit and a radio that picked up only static and the occasional weather report.

Her son, Daniel, lived in the city and called on Sundays. "Mum, come inland," he always said. "You can't keep climbing those stairs at seventy-three."

"The light doesn't know how old I am," she would reply, and he would sigh the way his father used to.

One October evening, a fierce wind came up from the southwest. The sky turned the color of slate, then ink. Margaret climbed the steps as usual, her knees aching with each one, and when she reached the lamp room, she saw a small boat far out past the reef, its single lantern bobbing wildly in the swell.

She lit the lamp, cranked the mechanism, and watched the beam sweep out over the water. The boat's lantern flickered, then steadied, then began to turn toward the harbor mouth. Slowly, carefully, it made its way through the channel and into the safety of the quay.

Margaret stayed in the lamp room long after the boat had gone, her hand resting on the cold brass rail. The wind howled around the glass. Below her, the town's few lights glowed through the rain.

The next morning, a young fisherman came to the lighthouse door, cap in his hands. "Mrs. Hale," he said, a little shyly. "That was my father's boat last night. He's a stubborn old man, and he wouldn't turn back when the storm came. But he said he could see your light the whole way in."

Margaret smiled. "Tell him the light was always there," she said. "He just had to keep looking for it."

When the fisherman had gone, she sat down at her kitchen table and wrote a letter to Daniel. It was not a letter asking him to let her stay, nor one begging her to leave. It was simply a record of the night: the wind, the lantern, the way the beam had reached out over the dark and brought someone home.

She signed it, sealed it, and set it by the door for the postman. Then she climbed the one hundred and twelve steps once more, because the sun was going down, and somewhere out there, someone might be looking for the light.
