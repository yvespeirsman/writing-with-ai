The Lighthouse Keeper's Last Letter

For forty-one years, Eli Marsh climbed the one hundred and twelve iron steps of the Carrow Point lighthouse every evening at dusk, and every evening he lit the lamp. He never missed one. Not during the storm of '87, when the glass cracked and he kept the flame burning with his own coat wrapped around the lens. Not the winter his wife died, when he sat up all night in the lantern room because it was the only place that felt like hers.

The lamp was his life, and the ships out past the rocks were his family, though none of them knew his name.

In his last year of service, the mainland sent a young relief keeper named Tomas Greer, twenty-three and nervous, with a duffel bag full of books and a habit of asking questions.

"Doesn't it get lonely?" Tomas asked on his first night, watching Eli trim the wick.

Eli considered the question the way he considered weather. "Lonely is when nobody's looking for you," he said. "Out here, somebody's always out there in the dark, looking for the light. That's not lonely. That's a job with a purpose."

Tomas didn't say anything, but he watched the old man carefully over the following weeks: how Eli checked the tide tables twice, how he wrote in a logbook with handwriting so steady it looked machine-made, how he paused at the window each night, lamp-lit, and murmured something too quiet to hear.

In October, a fishing trawler called the *Marguerite* went missing in a squall. The coast guard searched for three days. Eli climbed the steps every night and kept the lamp burning, even though the search was called off on the fourth morning.

On the fifth night, Tomas found him sitting in the lantern room with a sealed envelope in his lap.

"Who's it for?" Tomas asked gently.

"My son," Eli said. "Danny. He was on the *Marguerite*."

Tomas sat down slowly. Eli had never mentioned a son. Not once.

"We didn't speak for nineteen years," Eli said, turning the envelope over in his weathered hands. "I was a hard man. Proud. I told him the sea was no life for a family, and he went to sea anyway, just to spite me. I stayed out here so I wouldn't have to watch him fail or see him succeed without me." He gave a small, bitter laugh. "I was keeping a light for every ship but the one that mattered."

The wind pressed against the glass. Below them, the sea churned silver in the moonlight.

"I wrote to him last spring," Eli continued. "Never sent it. Didn't know how to say I was sorry without sounding like I was asking for something."

Tomas reached for the envelope, then stopped. "Maybe you don't have to send it," he said. "Maybe you just have to say it. Out loud. Where he might hear."

Eli looked at him for a long moment. Then he stood, crossed to the great lens, and rested his palm against the cool brass.

"Danny," he said quietly, his voice breaking open like old wood. "I should have come down off this rock. I should have come find you. I'm sorry. I'm so sorry, son."

The lamp swept its beam across the black water, steady and unhurried, as it had for forty-one years.

Far out past the rocks, where the sea met the sky, nothing answered. But Eli kept his palm on the glass until dawn, and for the first time in nineteen years, he did not feel like the one standing in the dark.

---

The *Marguerite* was found two weeks later, drifting, her crew safe on a rocky island eleven miles south. Among them was a man with Eli's jaw and a sea-worn letter in his coat pocket, written in steady handwriting, that had been sealed for nineteen years and had never been sent at all.
