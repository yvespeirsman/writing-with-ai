**The Lost Property Office at Harrow Station**

Every Tuesday, a woman named Edith Marr came to the lost property office at Harrow Station and asked if anyone had turned in an umbrella.

She had done this for eleven years. Walter Pike, who had worked the counter for twenty-six years, knew her by sight before he knew her name. She was always neatly dressed, always carried a black umbrella with a bent spoke, and always asked the same question in the same soft voice.

"Anything come in with a green handle? Wooden. Sort of crooked at the top."

"Not this week, Mrs. Marr."

"No. Thank you, Walter."

He had never asked her about the umbrella. It seemed like the kind of thing a person did not want asked about.

But one wet October afternoon, when the platform was empty and the radiator in the back room was knocking like someone wanting out, Walter found himself saying, "Can I ask what it is about that umbrella?"

Edith looked at him for a long moment. Then she set her handbag on the counter and sat on the stool beside it, as though she had been expecting this conversation for years.

"My husband bought it for me the spring we were married," she said. "He had a terrible sense of direction and a worse sense of weather. He'd carry it everywhere, even on sunny days, because he said it was lucky. It was the cheapest thing in the shop. The handle was carved by a man at the market who was missing two fingers, and Thomas always said it was the only honest thing he'd ever owned."

Walter nodded. "And you lost it here?"

"On the 4:12 to the coast. I left it in the rack by the door. I got off at Harrow Cross and didn't realize until I was halfway home." She turned her gloves over in her hands. "Thomas was supposed to meet me at the station that night. He'd promised to bring the car. He was always late, but he always came."

Walter waited.

"He didn't come. There was an accident on the ring road. He was driving to get me." She smiled, but it wasn't a smile. "I didn't go back for the umbrella. I couldn't. And then I kept thinking that if I could just find it, if it were sitting somewhere waiting, then it would mean he was still out there somewhere, still on his way."

The radiator knocked again. Outside, a train rattled through without stopping.

Walter got up slowly and went into the back room, where the unclaimed things were kept on wire shelves: scarves, phones, a violin, a single child's boot, three hundred and forty-one umbrellas in every color except green. He had catalogued them all. He knew where each one was.

He stood in the back room for a while with his hand on the cold metal shelving.

Then he went to the far corner, where a box had been sitting since the spring, marked *Misc, unclaimed, do not discard*. He had never opened it, because it was the box that had come with the old station manager's papers, and the old station manager had been an unkind man who liked to keep things nobody else wanted.

Inside, under a folded tartan blanket, was a black umbrella with a bent spoke. The handle was wooden, a little crooked at the top, with a pattern of small worn leaves carved into it.

Walter stood there holding it, feeling something loosen in his chest. He thought of all the Tuesdays. All the questions. Eleven years of a woman asking a man for a thing he had been keeping in the wrong box.

When he came back out, Edith was still on the stool, her hands folded.

"I think," Walter said carefully, "you should check the tag."

He passed her the umbrella. She took it the way you might take the hand of someone you had not seen in a long time. Her fingers found the carved leaves. She turned the handle over once, twice, then pressed her thumb against the place where the wood had worn smooth.

"Oh," she whispered. "Oh, Thomas."

She did not cry. She simply held it, and after a while she opened it, looked up at the ceiling of the little office as though checking for rain, and laughed quietly.

"It's sunny outside," Walter said.

"I know," said Edith Marr. "He always said it was lucky anyway."

---

She came back the next Tuesday, and every Tuesday after that, but she no longer asked about umbrellas. She just sat on the stool for a few minutes, talking about the weather, about the trains, about nothing much. And Walter, who had never been very good at talking, found that he liked listening to her.
