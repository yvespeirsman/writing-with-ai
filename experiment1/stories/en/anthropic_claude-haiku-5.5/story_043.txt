The Lighthouse Keeper's Last Letter

For forty-one years, Eleanor Marsh climbed the one hundred and twelve iron steps of the Gull Point lighthouse every evening at dusk. She trimmed the wick, polished the great lens, and turned the crank that set the mechanism turning, so that its beam could sweep across the black water and tell the fishing boats where the rocks were.

The Coast Guard had automated the light in 1987. Nobody told Eleanor, not really. They sent a letter, polite and final, and a young man in a pressed uniform who shook her hand and said the new system was very reliable. She nodded and thanked him and went home. The next evening, at dusk, she climbed the steps anyway.

The light still turned, of course. Electricity had made it simple. But Eleanor found that she could not stay away. She would sit in the small iron room at the top, watching the sea darken, and she would feel the beam leave the tower every few seconds like a heartbeat she was responsible for.

Her granddaughter, Nora, found her there one October evening, wrapped in a wool coat with a thermos of tea going cold at her feet.

"Gran, they're going to fine you for trespassing," Nora said, though she was smiling.

"Let them. I'm not hurting anything. I'm just keeping watch."

"Keeping watch over what?"

Eleanor gestured toward the water. "Those boats out there. Most of them know this coast by heart. But the young ones, the new ones, they don't trust what they can't see. They like to know someone's still awake."

Nora sat beside her on the cold bench. For a while neither of them spoke. The beam swept over them, pale and steady, and then moved on.

"Did you ever get tired of it?" Nora asked.

"Every single day." Eleanor laughed softly. "Storms that lasted three nights. Snow in the stairwell. My husband dying in the spring, and me coming up here anyway because the light didn't know I was grieving. But no. I never got tired of being the one who was here."

She reached for the thermos, poured a cup, and handed it to her granddaughter.

"That's what I'd want you to understand," she said. "Sometimes the job isn't the light. It's being someone who shows up when it's dark."

Nora looked out at the water, where a single fishing boat was coming in slowly, its running lights blinking red and green, following the beam home.

"I think I understand, Gran."

"Good," said Eleanor. "Then you can help me down before the cold gets into my knees."

They climbed down together, one hundred and twelve steps, and behind them the lighthouse kept turning, sending its patient message across the dark water: *you are not lost, not yet. Someone is still here.*
