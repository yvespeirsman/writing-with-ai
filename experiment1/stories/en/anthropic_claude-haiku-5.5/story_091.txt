The Lighthouse Keeper's Last Letter

The storm had been building for three days when Maren Holt climbed the spiral stairs of the Gannet Point lighthouse for the last time.

She was seventy-one years old, and her knees had opinions about every one of the hundred and sixty-two steps. But the lamp had to be lit, and there was no one left on the island to light it but her.

The mainland had sent word a month ago. The automated system would take over by spring. "A modern solution," the letter said, as if the sea had ever been interested in modern solutions. Maren had folded that letter into quarters and tucked it in her coat pocket, where it had stayed ever since, softening at the creases.

At the top, she wiped salt from the glass and checked the wick the way her father had taught her, the way he had taught her before that, back when the Holt family had kept the light for four generations. Outside, the sky was the color of a bruise, and the waves threw themselves against the rocks below with a violence that made the tower hum.

She lit the lamp. The beam swept out across the water, pale and steady, and somewhere far off, she knew, a fishing boat would see it and find its way home.

That was the thing no one on the mainland understood. The light wasn't for the lighthouse. It was a promise, repeated every night for a hundred and twelve years: *someone is watching, and you are not alone out there.*

Maren sat down on the iron stool beside the lamp and pulled out the letter one more time. Then she took a pen from her pocket and began to write on the back of it.

*To whoever comes after me,*

*The wick needs trimming every Thursday, though the gulls will tell you it's Friday. Don't trust the north window in winter; it lies about the wind. The third step from the bottom creaks, but it's the only one that does, so it's a good friend to have. And when the fog comes in so thick you can't see your own hand, keep the light burning anyway. Someone out there is counting on it, even if they'll never tell you so.*

*I was never brave. I was just never willing to stop.*

*Yours, from the top of the stairs,*
*Maren*

Hours passed. The storm pressed hard against the glass, and she kept the lamp turning, keeping watch as she had for fifty years.

Near dawn, the wind began to ease. Through the thinning spray, she saw a small boat, battered but upright, its engine sputtering, angling toward the harbor mouth. It was steering by her light.

Maren smiled, folded the letter, and tucked it beneath the lamp housing, where whoever came next would find it.

Then she went back to watching the water, as the sea went quiet and the gulls began to call.
