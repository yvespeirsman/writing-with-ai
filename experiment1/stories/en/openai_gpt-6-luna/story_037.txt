### The Lighthouse That Remembered

Every night, the lighthouse swept its beam across the sea.

Every night, the sea returned it.

Mara had lived in the lighthouse for six months, long enough to learn its ordinary sounds: the groan of the stairs, the hiss of rain against the windows, the lamp’s steady mechanical click. She had come to the island to be alone. The lighthouse seemed to understand.

Then, one foggy night in November, the beam found a boat.

It was small and battered, with no lights and no one visible aboard. As the beam passed over it, the boat turned toward shore. Mara grabbed her coat and ran down the stairs.

By the time she reached the rocks, the boat had grounded itself gently on the beach. Inside lay a boy, shivering and soaked. He couldn’t have been more than ten.

“Where did you come from?” Mara asked, wrapping him in her coat.

The boy looked up at the lighthouse. “From the place you left.”

Mara went still.

Six months earlier, she had left her hometown after her younger brother, Eli, died in a winter storm. They had argued that morning. She’d told him she didn’t care if he ever spoke to her again. That afternoon, he’d taken his boat out, and the fog had swallowed him.

The boy climbed to his feet. He had dark hair, a small scar beside his eyebrow, and Eli’s crooked smile.

“You can’t be here,” Mara whispered.

“I know,” he said. “I just wanted you to hear the rest.”

The wind pressed against the shore. Far above, the lighthouse turned its bright eye over the waves.

“I was angry too,” the boy said. “But I knew you loved me.”

Mara reached for him. Her hands passed through his coat like smoke.

“I’m sorry,” she said.

“I know.”

The boy turned toward the water. “You should go home sometime.”

Then the fog folded around him, and he was gone.

In the morning, Mara found the boat sitting empty on the beach. There were no footprints in the sand, only a little silver compass tucked beneath the seat. Eli’s compass. The one they had searched for after the storm and never found.

That spring, Mara left the island.

She didn’t stop missing her brother. But she stopped believing that missing him meant she had to stay where he had disappeared.

And every night, somewhere out at sea, the lighthouse swept its beam across the dark—just in case someone needed help finding the shore.
