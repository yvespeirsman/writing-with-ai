### The Lighthouse Keeper’s Last Light

Every night for forty years, Mara climbed the lighthouse stairs and lit the lamp.

She did it through storms that shook the windows, through summers when the sea lay flat as a sheet of glass. She did it after her husband died, and after the harbor emptied, and after the town’s last fishing boat was hauled onto land and turned into a flowerbed.

The lighthouse was no longer needed. Ships carried their own lights now, and the new harbor had a beacon bright enough to color the clouds. Still, at sunset, Mara climbed the stairs.

“Why keep it going?” asked the mayor, who visited one gray afternoon with a clipboard. “There’s nobody left to guide.”

Mara looked past him, toward the sea.

“You never know,” she said.

The mayor wrote something down and left.

That night, the wind came from the north. It dragged rain across the water and rattled the lighthouse door. Mara climbed to the lantern room, lit the lamp, and sat beside it with her knitting.

Near midnight, she heard a knock.

It came from below.

Mara set down her needles and went to the entrance. When she opened the door, a boy stood on the step, soaked to the skin and shivering. He couldn’t have been more than ten. In one hand he held a cracked wooden toy boat.

“Are you lost?” Mara asked.

The boy nodded.

“Where’s your family?”

He pointed toward the black sea.

Mara brought him inside, wrapped him in a blanket, and made tea. While he drank, she listened to his story: a boat, a storm, a flash of lightning, then the water swallowing everything but him and the little wooden boat.

Mara called the harbor master, though she knew what he would say. No boats had been out. No one had reported a missing child.

When she returned, the boy was staring through the window at the lamp.

“Does it shine all the way across?” he asked.

“As far as it can.”

“Will someone see it?”

Mara sat beside him. “Someone might.”

They waited together until dawn. The storm softened. The clouds lifted, and the sea turned silver. The boy fell asleep in the chair, his toy boat clutched against his chest.

At sunrise, Mara went to wake him.

The chair was empty.

Only the blanket remained, folded neatly on the seat. On the floor lay the wooden boat, dry and whole, though its cracked hull had been mended with a thin line of gold.

Mara carried it to the window. Far out beyond the harbor, a small fishing boat was making its way toward shore. On its deck, a man stood waving.

The mayor returned a week later with his clipboard.

“I’ve been told the lighthouse is to be closed,” he said.

Mara turned the lamp’s key. “Then you’d better tell the sea.”

That evening, she climbed the stairs and lit the lamp.

Far out in the dark, something answered: one bright flash, then another.
