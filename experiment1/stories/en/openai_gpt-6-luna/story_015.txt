### The Last Train to Bellweather

Every night at 11:17, a train passed through Bellweather.

It didn’t stop. The station had been closed for years, its ticket windows boarded and its clock frozen at 2:04. Weeds grew between the tracks. In winter, snow gathered on the platform in smooth, untouched drifts.

Still, at 11:17, the train came.

Mara heard it from her bedroom: a low whistle, then the soft clatter of wheels crossing the bridge over the river. She had grown up with the sound. Her father used to say it was the midnight express, hurrying somewhere important. Her mother said it was only the wind.

But on the night before Mara left town, her father sat beside her on the porch and said, “If you ever hear it stop, don’t get on.”

“Why not?”

He took a long time to answer. “Because it only stops for people who are ready to leave something behind.”

Mara thought he was joking. Her father had been getting sentimental lately, ever since her mother died. He’d started speaking to the garden, keeping the kettle warm, and leaving two plates on the table.

At 11:17, the whistle sounded.

Then came the clatter.

Then silence.

Mara stood.

Down at the station, a train waited beneath the yellow lamps. Its windows glowed warmly, and its doors stood open. No sign marked its destination.

Her father remained on the porch. “You don’t have to go,” he said.

Mara looked at the suitcase by the door. Inside were three shirts, a photograph of her mother, and the letter that had offered her a job in the city.

“What would I be leaving behind?” she asked.

Her father glanced toward the darkened house. “That’s for you to decide.”

The train gave a gentle chime.

Mara walked to the station. With each step, she expected to feel excitement, or fear, or the weight of all the things she might never see again. Instead, she felt the small, familiar tug of a loose thread.

At the platform, she looked back.

Her father was still on the porch. The kettle steamed in the kitchen window behind him. For a moment, he seemed very far away.

Mara understood then. She wasn’t leaving him behind. She was leaving behind the hope that grief could be undone—that if she stayed long enough, the house might become what it had been before.

She climbed aboard.

The doors closed. The train began to move.

Through the window, she watched her father lift one hand. She lifted hers in return. The station slid away, then the river, then the dark outline of Bellweather.

Only when the town had vanished did Mara open her suitcase. The photograph of her mother lay on top. On its back, in her mother’s handwriting, were the words:

*Go see what the morning looks like somewhere else.*

Mara smiled.

Ahead, the train carried her into the night. Somewhere beyond the windows, dawn was waiting.
