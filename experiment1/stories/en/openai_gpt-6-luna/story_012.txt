### The Lighthouse That Remembered Names

Every night, Mara climbed the lighthouse stairs with a lantern in one hand and a list in the other.

The list was kept in a blue notebook, its pages swollen from sea air. Names filled it from cover to cover: sailors, fishermen, one traveling magician, and a boy who had vanished from the harbor before anyone could learn where he was going.

Mara read each name aloud as she lit the lamp.

“Jonas Bell,” she said. “Miriam Vale. Tomas Reed.”

Then she turned the great glass lens toward the dark water.

The villagers said the lighthouse had once guided ships through a terrible storm. A hundred years ago, its keeper had stayed at his post while the waves tore the roof away and swallowed the road. By dawn, every ship had reached shore.

After that, people claimed the light remembered everyone it had saved.

Mara didn’t believe them. She believed in oil, gears, and clean glass. She had inherited the lighthouse from her grandfather, who had taught her that a light was useful only if someone kept it burning.

Then, one winter evening, she found a new name at the bottom of the list.

**Mara Vale.**

The handwriting was her grandfather’s.

She stared at the page until the letters blurred. Her grandfather had been dead for three years. The notebook had been in her desk the entire time.

That night, the sea was calm. Still, as Mara turned the lens, the beam caught something far beyond the harbor: a small boat, its sail torn, its single occupant waving a lantern.

Mara grabbed the speaking trumpet.

“Come toward the light!” she shouted.

The boat turned.

She stayed at the window for hours, watching its slow approach. At last it reached the harbor, and a woman climbed out—soaked, shivering, but alive.

“Thank you,” the woman said. “I thought no one was there.”

Mara looked back at the lighthouse. Its lamp glowed warmly above them.

In the morning, she opened the notebook. Beneath her own name, a new line had appeared:

**Elian Voss.**

Mara began to understand. The names weren’t only those the lighthouse had saved. They were the people who still needed saving.

So she kept reading.

Years passed. Mara grew old. Her hands stiffened, her eyesight dimmed, and the stairs grew steeper. But every evening she climbed them, calling the names into the quiet.

One night, a storm came in hard and fast. The wind rattled the windows. The sea rose over the harbor wall.

Mara reached the top of the stairs and found the lamp already burning.

Beside it stood her grandfather, young again, wearing his old keeper’s coat.

“You’re late,” he said.

Mara laughed, though she was frightened. “I’m tired.”

“I know.”

Below them, the storm battered the coast. Out in the darkness, a ship’s horn sounded once, then again.

Her grandfather held out his hand.

Mara looked at the lantern. Its beam swept across the water, searching.

“Will it be all right?” she asked.

“It will be seen,” he said.

And together they turned the great glass lens toward the sea.
