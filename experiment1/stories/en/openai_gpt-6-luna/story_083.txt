### The Lighthouse That Remembered

Every night, Mara climbed the lighthouse stairs with a lantern in one hand and a cup of tea in the other.

The lighthouse had been dark for forty years. Its great glass eye was cracked, its iron door rusted shut, and gulls nested in the rafters. But Mara climbed anyway.

“Just in case,” she told the gulls.

She had inherited the lighthouse from her grandfather, along with a brass key and a note that said: *It knows what to do.*

Mara had no idea what that meant.

Then, one winter evening, the fog rolled in so thick that the sea vanished. From somewhere beyond it came a ship’s bell—one faint clang, then another.

Mara set down her tea and hurried to the lantern room. She tried the old switch. Nothing. She struck the glass with her palm. Nothing.

“Please,” she whispered.

Deep inside the lighthouse, something clicked.

A golden beam burst through the fog.

It swept across the water once, twice—and then stopped, pointing toward a narrow gap between the rocks.

The bell rang again. Closer now.

Mara watched the beam hold steady as a ship emerged from the whiteness, its sails torn and its deck crowded with people. It followed the light through the gap and into the harbor, where fishermen rushed to help.

By dawn, the ship was safe.

Its captain came to the lighthouse carrying a small wooden box. Inside lay a stack of letters, each one addressed to Mara’s grandfather.

“He saved my mother’s ship when she was a girl,” the captain said. “She always said the light remembered every vessel it had guided home.”

Mara looked up at the great glass eye. In the morning sun, it was only a cracked old window.

Still, that night, she climbed the stairs with a lantern in one hand and a cup of tea in the other.

“Just in case,” she said.

And somewhere inside the lighthouse, the gears began to turn.
