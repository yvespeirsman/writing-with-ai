### The Lighthouse That Remembered

Every night, Mara climbed the lighthouse stairs and lit the lamp.

She had done it for thirty years, ever since her father had shown her how: turn the brass wheel, trim the wick, polish the glass. Then watch the beam sweep across the dark water.

“Ships need a light,” he’d told her. “Even when they don’t know they’re lost.”

The village below had grown quiet over the years. The fishing boats were gone. The harbor shop had become a café, then a place with a sign in the window that said **CLOSED**. Still, Mara kept the lighthouse burning.

One winter night, as snow skittered sideways across the rocks, she noticed a light out at sea.

Not a ship’s lamp. Not a star. It blinked three times, paused, then blinked three times again.

Mara seized the old signal flag and climbed to the lantern room. The light came again—three blinks, a pause, three blinks.

She knew that signal.

It was the one her father had taught her as a child. *I’m here.*

Mara leaned toward the glass. “I’m here too,” she whispered.

Then she turned the lighthouse beam toward the sea and let it sweep slowly across the waves.

At dawn, a small boat appeared in the harbor. An old man stepped out, soaked and shivering, carrying a wooden box.

“I saw your light,” he said. “I thought the lighthouse had been abandoned.”

Mara looked at the box. “What’s in there?”

He opened it. Inside lay a brass compass, scratched and salt-stained.

“My father’s,” he said. “He sailed with yours. They were lost together in a storm, long ago. I’ve been trying to bring this home.”

Mara touched the compass. For one brief moment, she remembered her father’s warm hand over hers, guiding the wheel.

That night, she lit the lamp as usual.

But as the beam crossed the water, it seemed to her that another light answered from somewhere far beyond the horizon.
