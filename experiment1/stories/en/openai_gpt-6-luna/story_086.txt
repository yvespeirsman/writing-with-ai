### The Last Light in Bellweather

Every evening at six, Mara climbed the hill to light the lighthouse.

She had done it since she was twelve, when her grandfather’s knees grew too weak for the stairs. She did it after he died, too, though the harbor had been closed for years and no ships came near Bellweather Bay.

“Some lights are for the people who are lost,” he used to tell her. “Others are for the people who are coming home.”

Mara never knew which he meant.

On the first night of winter, a storm rolled in from the east. Wind shoved rain against the lighthouse windows, and the old lamp flickered as Mara turned its brass handle. She was halfway down the stairs when she heard a knock.

Not on the lighthouse door.

On the glass.

Mara hurried back up. Beyond the window, through sheets of rain, stood a boy on the balcony. He looked about ten, soaked through, with a red scarf wrapped around his neck.

She opened the door. “How did you get up here?”

The boy glanced toward the dark sea. “I was looking for the way home.”

“There’s no way home out there,” Mara said gently. “The harbor’s been closed a long time.”

“I know,” he said. “But I saw the light.”

Mara fetched a blanket and sat him beside the lamp. His name was Eli. He said he had been walking along the shore, though Mara knew there was no shore path on that side of the bay—only cliffs and deep water.

When she asked where his parents were, he looked down at his red scarf.

“My mother used to tie this for me,” he said. “She told me if I ever got lost, I should find the light.”

The lamp turned slowly above them, sending a bright sweep across the storm.

Mara stared at the scarf. Her grandfather had kept one just like it in a drawer, though she had never asked why.

Outside, a bell rang from somewhere beyond the rain.

Eli stood.

A small boat appeared beneath the cliffs, its lantern bobbing on the waves. A woman stood in the bow, calling his name.

He ran to the door, then turned back. “Will you keep lighting it?”

Mara looked at the great brass lamp. Its flame trembled but held.

“Every evening,” she promised.

Eli hurried down the stairs. By the time Mara reached the balcony, the boat had vanished into the mist. The storm was already fading.

In the morning, she found a red scarf folded beside the lamp. It was dry and warm, as if someone had just taken it off.

That evening, at six, Mara climbed the hill.

For the first time, she understood what her grandfather meant.

Some lights were for people who were lost.

Others were for the people who were coming home.
