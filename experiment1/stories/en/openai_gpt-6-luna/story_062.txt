## The Last Light on Bellwether Street

Every evening at six, the streetlights on Bellwether Street came on in order, one by one.

The first stood outside the bakery. The second watched over the bus stop. The third leaned above the narrow alley where cats gathered after dark. And the last stood at the end of the street, beside the house where Mara had grown up.

When she was little, her father told her the lights were waking up.

“From what?” she’d asked.

“From the day,” he said, as if that explained everything.

Years later, after he was gone and the house had been sold, Mara returned to Bellwether Street with a suitcase and a key that no longer fit any door. She rented the upstairs room over the bakery and took a job at the library. She told people she was back because the city was cheaper. The real reason was harder to say: she had nowhere else she wanted to be.

The streetlights still came on at six.

Only the last one had stopped.

The city sent someone to repair it, but the electrician found no broken wires, no burnt bulb, no fault at all. “Old lamp,” he said, shrugging. “Sometimes they just give up.”

Mara thought of her father, who had sat beneath that light on summer nights, reading the newspaper long after the sun had set. She thought of the little girl she’d been, pressing her face to the window to watch him walk home.

That evening, she carried a chair to the end of the street and sat beneath the dark lamp.

At six, the other lights woke in their familiar order.

The bakery. The bus stop. The alley.

Then silence.

Mara sat until the air turned cold. At last, she stood and placed her hand on the lamp post.

“I’m here,” she said.

The lamp flickered.

She pulled her hand away. A moment passed. Then the light came on—not bright, exactly, but warm, like a kitchen window seen from far down the road.

The next evening, Mara returned with a book. The evening after that, she brought tea. Soon she began waving to the neighbors as they passed. A boy walking his dog stopped to ask what she was doing.

“Keeping the light company,” she said.

He nodded as if this made perfect sense. The next night, he came back with a football. Then a woman from the bakery brought a folding table. Someone brought a deck of cards. Someone else brought a radio that only played old songs.

By the end of the month, people had begun gathering beneath the lamp each evening. They came with stories, with worries, with cakes still warm from the oven. They learned one another’s names.

One night, Mara looked around the little circle of faces and realized she had stopped counting the days since her father died.

The lamp above them hummed softly.

On the anniversary of his death, Mara brought the newspaper he’d left behind, folded neatly to the page he’d been reading. She didn’t open it. She simply held it in her lap and listened while the others talked.

At six, the light came on.

Not because the day was over, she thought.

Because the night had begun.
