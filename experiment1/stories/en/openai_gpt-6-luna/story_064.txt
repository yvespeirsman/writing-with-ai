### The Lighthouse Keeper’s Map

Every evening, Mara climbed the lighthouse stairs with a cup of tea in one hand and a question in the other.

The question changed. The tea did not.

“Why do ships still follow the old channel?” she asked the lighthouse, setting the cup beside the lamp.

The lighthouse, which had stood on the headland for two hundred years, said nothing. It was very good at saying nothing.

Below, the sea folded itself against the rocks. Beyond it lay the old channel, a strip of dark water marked by three crooked buoys. The harbor had built a new channel years ago, wider and safer. Yet every few weeks, some stubborn captain still steered through the old one.

Mara’s father used to say it was because sailors trusted what they could see.

“Then why don’t they trust a map?” she’d asked him.

“Because a map is only a promise,” he’d said. “The sea is the part that keeps it.”

He had been gone nine months now. His coat still hung on its peg downstairs.

On the first stormy night of winter, a ship appeared on the horizon. Its lights bobbed wildly, and its horn sounded once—low and frightened.

Mara rushed to the lamp room. The beam swept over the water, revealing the ship heading straight for the old channel.

She reached for the signal lever, then stopped.

The storm had washed away one of the crooked buoys. Without it, the channel was nearly impossible to find. The ship would strike the reef.

Mara looked toward the harbor. The new channel was clear, but the wind was pushing the ship sideways. No one could steer there in time.

Then she remembered the question she had asked her father.

Why don’t they trust a map?

Because a map is only a promise.

Mara grabbed her coat and ran downstairs.

The old keeper’s boat was tied below the lighthouse. She had never taken it out alone. The waves slapped its sides as if trying to talk her out of it.

She untied the rope.

Halfway to the channel, the storm swallowed the lighthouse beam. For a moment, Mara could see nothing but rain and black water. Then she heard the ship’s horn again.

She found the first buoy by touch. The second by memory. Where the third should have been, she found only empty water.

Mara stood in the boat, soaked and shaking. She lifted her lantern high.

Once. Twice. Three times.

The ship’s lights shifted.

It was following her.

Mara guided it along the channel, keeping the reef to her left and the rocks to her right. When the boat struck a wave, she nearly lost the lantern. When the wind tore the hood from her head, she kept rowing.

At last the ship passed into safe water.

Its horn sounded—three long notes this time, steady and warm.

By dawn, the storm had moved east. The ship was anchored in the harbor, and Mara was back at the lighthouse, wrapped in her father’s coat.

She climbed the stairs with a cup of tea in one hand and a new question in the other.

“Do you think he would have done that?”

The lighthouse said nothing.

But out beyond the windows, the old channel shone in the morning light. For the first time, Mara thought it looked less like a danger and more like a path.
