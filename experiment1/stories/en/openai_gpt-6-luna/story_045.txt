### The Lighthouse That Kept the Sea

On the night the sea disappeared, Mara was in the lighthouse polishing the lamp.

She had been polishing it for twelve years, ever since her father had gone out in a storm and never come back. Every evening, she climbed the spiral stairs, trimmed the wick, cleaned the glass, and turned the great light toward the water.

It was a lonely job, but it was an important one. Ships needed the lighthouse. The village needed the ships. And Mara needed to believe that keeping the light burning was a way of keeping her father safe somewhere beyond the dark.

Then, just before midnight, the foghorn sounded.

Mara froze.

The horn was meant to warn ships away from the rocks. But there were no ships scheduled, and the sky was clear. She hurried to the window.

The ocean was gone.

Where the waves should have been, a vast stretch of pale sand sloped away beneath the moon. Far out, stranded fishing boats leaned in the mud. The sea had pulled itself back from the shore so completely that the village’s old stone road lay exposed, leading toward the horizon.

Mara grabbed her coat and ran.

At the end of the road stood a boy, barefoot and dripping wet. He was about ten years old, with black hair plastered to his forehead and a silver fish clutched in both hands.

“Where’s the sea?” Mara shouted.

The boy looked at her solemnly. “It’s hiding.”

“Why?”

He glanced toward the lighthouse. “It doesn’t like the light.”

Mara looked back at the bright tower. “The light guides ships.”

“It guides other things, too,” the boy said. “Things that have been lost.”

The silver fish wriggled free and flopped toward the dark distance. As it moved, Mara heard a sound from beyond the stranded boats: a low, familiar whistle.

Her father’s whistle.

She took one step down the road.

The boy caught her sleeve. “If you follow, the water will come back over you.”

Mara stared into the darkness. “Is he out there?”

“I don’t know,” the boy said. “But the sea does.”

For twelve years, Mara had kept the light burning because she thought it might help her father find his way home. Now she understood the crueler truth: perhaps the light had been calling him all this time—and the sea had been holding him back.

She ran to the lighthouse.

At the top, she reached for the lamp’s great brass wheel. It was heavy, stiff with age. She pushed with both hands. The beam swung away from the open water and turned toward the village.

Darkness spread across the horizon.

For a moment, nothing happened.

Then the wind lifted. Far off, the ocean began to roar.

The boy appeared at the lighthouse door. “It’s coming back!”

Mara kept her hands on the wheel. “I know.”

The tide rushed toward the shore, filling the road, lifting the stranded boats. And in the last strip of exposed sand, just before the water covered it, a man stood facing the lighthouse.

He raised one hand.

Mara raised hers.

Then the sea returned, and he was gone.

That night, Mara let the lamp go dark.

In the morning, the villagers found the ocean shining as it always had. And when they asked why the lighthouse had failed, Mara told them the truth: that the sea had needed a night without a guide.

After that, she lit the lamp only when ships were in danger.

And sometimes, on very quiet evenings, she climbed to the top of the tower and listened.

From somewhere beyond the waves came a whistle, faint but clear.

Mara always answered.
