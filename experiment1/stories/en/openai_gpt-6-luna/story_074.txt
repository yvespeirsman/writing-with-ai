The Last Train to Bellweather

Every night at 11:17, a train passed through Bellweather.

It didn’t stop. There was no station anymore—just a cracked platform under the weeds, a timetable bleached white by rain, and a brass clock that had been stuck at 4:03 for as long as anyone could remember.

Still, the train came.

Mara heard it from her bedroom window: a low whistle, then the distant clatter of wheels. She had heard it every night since moving into her grandmother’s house. Her grandmother had heard it, too, and her mother before her.

“Where does it go?” Mara once asked.

Her grandmother had shrugged. “Somewhere people are going.”

That winter, her grandmother died. The house filled with relatives, casseroles, and quiet arguments about what should be sold. On the last night before Mara was to leave, she went down to the abandoned platform.

At 11:17, the rails began to hum.

The train emerged from the fog, its windows glowing amber. It slowed as it passed her. In one window, Mara saw a little girl with a red scarf. The girl pressed her palm against the glass.

Mara recognized the scarf. She had worn it at six years old, the night her mother left town.

The train stopped.

Its doors opened with a sigh. Inside, the seats were empty except for a woman sitting by the window. Mara’s mother, older than the photographs but exactly as Mara remembered her.

“Mara,” she said.

All the years between them seemed to gather on the platform: the birthday calls that never came, the letters that stopped, the questions Mara had learned not to ask.

“You can come with me,” her mother said. “If you want.”

Mara looked back toward the dark town. In the distance, her grandmother’s house had one lit window. She thought of the life waiting there—not an easy one, but a real one, still hers to make.

“Not tonight,” she said.

Her mother nodded as if she had expected that. She held out a small paper ticket.

The doors closed. The train began to move.

Mara watched it disappear into the fog. Then she turned the ticket over. On the back, in her grandmother’s handwriting, were four words:

*Somewhere people are going.*

At 4:03, the brass clock gave a single, bright tick.
