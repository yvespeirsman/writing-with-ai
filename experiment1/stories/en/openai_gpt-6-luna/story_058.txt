### The Lantern Keeper

Every night, Mara lit the lantern at the end of the pier.

She lit it in summer, when the sea lay calm and silver beneath the moon. She lit it in winter, when waves hurled themselves against the rocks and the wind shoved at her back. She lit it even on the nights when fog swallowed the shore, though she knew no sailor could see more than a few steps ahead.

“Someone might,” her father used to say.

He had been the lantern keeper before her. One night, years ago, he sailed out to help a fishing boat caught in a storm and never returned. After that, Mara took his place. She kept the lamp polished, the glass clear, and the flame steady.

The people in town called it a sad job.

Mara didn’t mind.

Then, one October evening, the lantern refused to light.

She trimmed the wick. She cleaned the glass again. She struck match after match, but each flame vanished with a soft hiss.

Outside, the fog was so thick that the pier seemed to end at the first wooden post. From somewhere beyond it came three slow knocks.

Mara froze.

Knock. Knock. Knock.

She grabbed her coat and stepped into the fog. At the end of the pier, she found a boy standing beside the railing, soaked to the skin. He couldn’t have been more than ten.

“Are you lost?” Mara asked.

The boy shook his head. “I’m looking for my mother’s boat.”

“No boats can come in tonight.”

“She said she’d find the light.”

Mara looked back toward the dark lantern house. “I’m trying to make it work.”

The boy reached into his coat and drew out a small brass compass. Its needle spun wildly, then stopped—pointing straight at the lantern.

“My mother gave it to me,” he said. “She told me that if I was ever afraid, I should follow what I knew was true.”

Mara looked at the dark wick. She looked at the boy. Then she understood.

The lantern had no oil.

She had used the last of it the night before and forgotten to send for more. For years, she had kept the lamp burning with supplies delivered by boat. Tonight, there was nothing left.

Mara glanced at the boy’s compass. “Your mother is out there?”

He nodded.

Together, they carried the lantern’s spare oil tin from the storeroom. It was nearly empty, but a thin layer still coated the bottom. Mara poured it carefully into the lamp.

The wick caught.

A small flame rose, trembled, and steadied.

Far out in the fog, a horn answered.

The boy began to cry. Mara put an arm around his shoulders as a fishing boat emerged from the gray, moving slowly toward the pier. At the bow stood a woman with one hand raised.

When the boat reached them, the boy ran into her arms.

The woman looked at Mara. “We were beginning to think the light had gone out.”

“So was I,” Mara said.

Later, after the boat had sailed safely into harbor, Mara returned to the lantern room. The flame burned low, and she knew it would not last until morning. But beyond the window, the fog was lifting. The dark water had opened enough to show the stars.

For the first time in many years, Mara let the lamp go out.

Someone, she thought, would know how to find the shore.
