### The Last Light in Bellweather

Every night at 9:17, the lighthouse blinked.

It had been dark for twenty years, ever since the town built a modern beacon on the headland. But the old lighthouse still flashed once each night: a pale, silver wink across the water.

People blamed faulty wiring. The electrician blamed the wiring in the electrician’s office. Eventually, everyone agreed it was easier not to wonder.

Then, one winter evening, the light blinked twice.

Mara saw it from the harbor. She was twelve, and she knew the old lighthouse better than anyone because her grandfather had once been its keeper. Before he died, he’d given her a brass key and told her, “If it ever flashes twice, go find out why.”

So Mara pulled on her boots and climbed the hill through the snow.

The lighthouse door opened with the key. Inside, the spiral stairs smelled of salt and dust. At the top, beneath the dead lamp, she found a small wooden box. It hadn’t been there the last time she visited.

Inside was a letter.

*Dear Mara,* it began. *If you’re reading this, the light has found someone who still looks up.*

Her grandfather’s handwriting trembled across the page. He told her about a ship caught in a winter storm, years ago, when the new beacon had failed. He’d kept the old light burning by hand until the ship found the harbor. A young woman aboard had promised that one day she’d return the favor.

At the bottom of the letter was a name: **Elian Vale**.

Mara took the letter to the harbor master. He knew the name. Elian Vale had been a sailor, he said. She’d died long ago—but her daughter still lived in town.

The next day, Mara found the daughter in a blue house by the docks. She was old and silver-haired, and when Mara showed her the letter, she pressed it to her heart.

“That was my mother,” she whispered. “She searched for the keeper for years.”

That night, at 9:17, the lighthouse blinked once.

Then, from the blue house, a lantern shone back.

Mara smiled. The old light had done its work at last.
