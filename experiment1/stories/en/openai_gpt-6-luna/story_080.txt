### The Lighthouse That Forgot the Sea

Every night, Mara climbed the lighthouse stairs and lit the lamp.

Every morning, she climbed them again and put it out.

She had done this for forty years, though no ships had passed the island in nearly thirty. The harbor was silting up, the fishing village had emptied, and the sea had withdrawn so far that, from the lighthouse door, you could see grass where waves used to be.

Still, Mara kept the lamp burning.

“Who are you warning?” her brother had asked before he left for the mainland.

“Whoever needs it,” she’d said.

Then he’d shaken his head and gone.

One winter night, the lamp refused to light.

Mara cleaned the glass. She trimmed the wick. She carried up fresh oil. But when she struck the match, the flame shivered blue and died.

She tried again.

Nothing.

Outside, the wind shoved at the tower. In the darkness below, something made a soft, dragging sound across the empty seabed.

Mara took her lantern and went to investigate.

At the foot of the cliff lay a small wooden boat, stranded in the grass. A boy stood beside it, soaked to the knees and clutching an oar. He couldn’t have been more than twelve.

“Where did you come from?” Mara asked.

“The sea,” he said, as if that explained everything.

“It isn’t here.”

The boy looked out across the plain. “It was when I started.”

Mara brought him inside and wrapped him in a blanket. He told her he’d been sailing with his mother when fog swallowed the boat. Then he’d heard a bell—and followed its sound toward shore.

“There’s no bell,” Mara said.

“I heard it.”

Mara glanced up at the dark lighthouse.

The boy followed her gaze. “Your light went out.”

“It’s an old lamp.”

“No,” he said. “It’s lonely.”

Mara nearly laughed. Instead, she asked him where his mother was.

“Still looking.”

All night, they waited by the window. The wind eased. The boy slept on the kitchen floor. Mara sat beside him, listening to the quiet.

At dawn, a bell rang.

Not from the village. Not from the lighthouse.

From far out across the grass.

Mara woke the boy. Together they hurried down the hill. In the distance, a woman ran toward them, carrying a brass handbell. The boy broke into a run.

His mother embraced him so tightly he squeaked.

Then the woman turned to Mara. “We followed your light,” she said.

“But it was out.”

The woman held up the bell. “This was.”

Mara looked back at the lighthouse. In the morning sun, its glass flashed bright as a star.

That evening, she climbed the stairs once more. She poured in the oil, struck a match, and watched the flame take hold.

For the first time in years, she wasn’t lighting the lamp for whoever might need it.

She was lighting it for the people who had found their way home.
