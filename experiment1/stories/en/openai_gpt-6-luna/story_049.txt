### The Lighthouse That Remembered

Every night, the lighthouse flashed twice toward the sea and once toward the cliffs.

No one knew why.

The old keeper, Mr. Vale, said it had always done so. The town’s records said otherwise. A hundred years ago, the lighthouse had flashed twice toward the sea and twice toward the cliffs. Then, on one stormy night, one of its great glass panes had shattered. Ever since, the third flash had been missing.

“Lighthouses don’t remember,” said Mara, who had come to the island for the summer.

“Maybe not,” Mr. Vale replied. “But this one has been waiting a long time.”

Mara thought he was joking. Then the storm arrived.

It came at dusk, rolling over the water like a dark hill. Wind rattled the windows. Waves hammered the cliffs. Mr. Vale climbed the lighthouse stairs to keep the lamp turning, but halfway up, he stopped, gripping the rail.

“My leg,” he gasped. “Mara, the lamp—”

She ran the rest of the way.

At the top, the lamp had gone dark. Rain streaked the glass. Mara found the oil reservoir and the old match tin, but her hands shook so badly she dropped the first match.

Below, out in the black water, a ship’s horn sounded.

Mara struck another match. It flared, went out.

The horn sounded again, closer.

She cupped her hands around the next match and touched it to the wick. The flame caught. Gears groaned awake. Slowly, the great light began to turn.

Once, it swept across the sea.

Twice, it swept across the sea.

Mara watched the beam swing toward the cliffs—and saw the broken pane.

The missing section was just wide enough for a person to climb through. Beyond it, a narrow ledge ran along the outside wall, slick with rain.

Through the storm, she spotted a small boat tossed against the rocks below. Someone was standing in it, waving a lantern.

Mara looked at the lamp. If she left it, the turning light would stop. If she stayed, the person on the boat might never see the safe channel.

She grabbed the lantern and climbed out onto the ledge.

The wind shoved at her. Water slapped her boots. She crawled along the stone until she reached the edge of the broken pane, then raised the lantern high.

A flash answered from the boat.

Mara moved the lantern left, toward the narrow channel. The boat followed.

When she made it back inside, soaked and shivering, the lighthouse’s beam was still turning. Its broken glass cast a strange pattern across the water: two bright sweeps toward the sea, then one steady glow toward the cliffs.

In the morning, Mr. Vale found her asleep beside the lamp.

“You saw it, didn’t you?” he asked.

Mara rubbed her eyes. “The missing flash?”

He smiled. “No. The reason.”

Years later, when Mara became the keeper, she replaced the shattered pane.

But she never changed the light.

Twice toward the sea. Once toward the cliffs.

And, on the darkest nights, a little lantern glow from the ledge.
