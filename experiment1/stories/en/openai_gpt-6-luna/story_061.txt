### The Borrowed Moon

Every night at 8:17, the moon disappeared.

Not behind a cloud. Not below the horizon. It simply blinked out, leaving the sky empty for exactly one minute.

People argued about it on television. Scientists built machines. The mayor declared a town-wide emergency, then quietly canceled it when nobody could agree what to do.

Mara didn’t mind the dark. She minded that her father was still awake to see it.

He sat by the window in his old blue chair, wrapped in a blanket, watching the patch of sky above the rooftops. The doctors had said there might not be many more nights. Her father had nodded politely, as though they were discussing the weather.

At 8:16, Mara brought him a cup of tea.

“Do you think the moon is going somewhere?” she asked.

He turned from the window. “Everything goes somewhere.”

“That’s not an answer.”

“It’s the only one I’ve got.”

At 8:17, the moon vanished.

Her father reached for her hand. His fingers were cool and light. For one minute, the dark pressed against the glass. Then the moon returned, pale and whole.

“There,” he said. “It came back.”

Mara tried to smile. “Maybe it just needed a minute.”

Her father closed his eyes. “Maybe we all do.”

After he fell asleep, Mara went outside.

The town was quiet under the restored moon. She walked to the hill behind the house, where the grass silvered in the night. At the top stood an old pay phone, left over from the days before everyone carried a phone in their pocket. Nobody knew why it still worked. Nobody had used it in years.

Mara lifted the receiver.

There was a dial tone.

She didn’t know who she meant to call. Her mother had been gone for eleven years. Her father was asleep behind her. Still, she put a coin in and dialed the number she remembered from childhood.

It rang once.

A woman answered.

“Hello?”

Mara’s breath caught. “Mom?”

A pause. Then, softly: “Is that you, little bird?”

Mara gripped the phone. She had forgotten that nickname. Or thought she had.

“I miss you,” she said.

“Oh, sweetheart,” her mother said. “I know.”

The moon blinked out.

In the dark, Mara heard the distant hush of the town holding its breath.

“Will you come back?” she asked.

“I can’t,” her mother said. “But I’m not gone from every place.”

The minute stretched thin.

Mara wanted to ask a thousand questions. Instead, she said, “Dad’s waiting for the moon.”

“He always did like a good ending.”

“He’s scared.”

“So are you.”

The moon returned. Light spilled over the hill, and the pay phone went quiet.

Mara stood there a while, listening to the dial tone.

Then she went home.

Her father was awake again, watching the window.

“Did you see it?” he asked.

“I did.”

“Good.” He patted the chair beside him. “Sit with me till it goes.”

So Mara sat. At 8:17, the moon disappeared.

This time, when the room darkened, she didn’t reach for the light.

She held her father’s hand and waited with him for the sky to remember.
