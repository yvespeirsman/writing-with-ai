### The Lighthouse Keeper’s Map

Every morning, Mara found a new island on the map.

She lived in a lighthouse on a rocky point where the sea was usually gray and the village was usually quiet. The map hung above her kitchen table, framed in dark wood. It showed the coast as it had been for a hundred years: one harbor, three coves, and a long stretch of empty water.

But each morning, somewhere beyond the familiar shoreline, there was a new island.

Some were no bigger than a hat. Others had forests, mountains, even tiny, curling rivers. None appeared on any map in the village, and none could be seen from the lighthouse windows.

Mara marked each island with a small blue pin.

“Perhaps the map is remembering places that sank,” said Mr. Bell, who sold newspapers and believed most strange things had sensible explanations.

“Perhaps it’s drawing places that haven’t arrived yet,” said Mara.

Then winter came, and the map stopped changing.

For weeks, not one new island appeared. The sea grew rough, the wind rattled the lighthouse windows, and the village kept to its firesides. Mara checked the map each morning anyway.

On the first clear day of spring, she noticed something else: the blue pins had begun to move. One by one, they were sliding toward the edge of the map nearest the harbor.

Mara leaned close.

There, in the empty water between the last island and the coast, a narrow black line had appeared. It looked like a crack.

That night, she climbed to the lantern room and swept the beam across the waves. For a moment, the light caught something that should not have been there: a small boat, bobbing in the dark. Someone stood in it, holding one hand above their eyes.

Mara ran down the tower and out into the storm.

The boat scraped against the rocks. Its passenger was a girl about twelve years old, soaked through and shivering. She clutched a wooden box to her chest.

“Where did you come from?” Mara shouted over the wind.

The girl pointed toward the open sea. “An island. It was there yesterday.”

Mara looked at the boat. It was empty except for the girl and the box.

“What happened to it?”

“I don’t know. The shore started disappearing. My father put me in the boat and told me to follow the light.” She swallowed. “He said he’d follow when he could.”

Mara brought her inside, wrapped her in blankets, and set a pot of soup on the stove. The girl told her that her island had a red mountain, a river that ran uphill, and a village where no one had ever heard of the mainland.

“What’s your name?” Mara asked.

“Lio.”

Mara glanced at the map. The black line had widened. The blue pins trembled at its edge.

“What’s in the box?” she asked.

Lio opened it. Inside lay a small brass compass. Its needle spun wildly, then settled—not north, but toward the map.

Mara understood then. The islands weren’t places that had sunk or places yet to come. They were places people had lost: homes forgotten, worlds left behind, stories no one carried anymore.

And now Lio’s island was being forgotten, too.

“Can the light bring it back?” Lio asked.

Mara looked up at the great lantern above them. “I don’t know,” she said. “But it can help someone find the way.”

Together, they climbed the winding stairs. Lio held the compass while Mara turned the great lamp toward the sea. The beam swept out across the water.

“Tell me about your home,” Mara said.

So Lio began.

She told Mara about the red mountain, the river that ran uphill, the baker who made cinnamon bread, and her father, who sang off-key whenever he was nervous. As she spoke, the compass needle steadied. On the map, the black crack began to close.

Mara kept the light moving. Lio kept talking.

Just before dawn, a second light appeared on the horizon. Then a third. They blinked across the sea, drawing nearer.

Lio pressed her face to the glass. “Those are our boats!”

By morning, the village harbor was full of strangers. They came carrying stories, songs, and names for the places they had nearly lost. Mr. Bell gave them a room above his newspaper shop. The baker borrowed Lio’s recipe for cinnamon bread. And Mara added a new pin to the map.

This one was green.

After that, the map kept changing. Some mornings it showed islands. Some mornings it showed only waves. Mara never knew what she’d find until she came downstairs.

But she always left the lantern burning a little longer.

Just in case someone, somewhere, was trying to find their way home.
