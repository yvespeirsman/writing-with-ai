### The Lighthouse Keeper’s Last Light

Every night, Mara climbed the lighthouse stairs with a lantern in one hand and a cup of tea in the other.

The stairs spiraled upward through the stone tower, past narrow windows where the sea flashed silver in the moonlight. At the top, she lit the great lamp and watched its beam sweep across the dark water.

Her father had been the keeper before her. He taught her that the light was not meant to guide ships toward shore.

“It’s meant to remind them,” he’d said, “that shore is there.”

One winter night, a storm rolled in so quickly that the harbor bells began to ring. Wind battered the lighthouse. Rain rattled against the glass. Mara climbed to the lantern room and lit the lamp, but the beam vanished almost at once into the thick, driving fog.

She kept it turning.

Then, through the storm, came three flashes of light—faint and uneven—from somewhere out at sea.

A ship in trouble.

Mara grabbed the signal flags and ran down the tower steps. At the harbor, she found the rescue boat chained to its post. The crew had gone home when the warning bells sounded; the waves were too dangerous.

She could wait for them.

Instead, she cut the chain.

The boat bucked beneath her as she pushed into the water. Spray filled her eyes. She steered by the distant flashes, calling to the lost ship whenever the wind let her voice carry.

At last, a shadow rose through the fog: a small fishing boat, its mast broken, its deck crowded with frightened sailors. Mara threw them a rope. One by one, they climbed aboard her boat and made for shore.

By dawn, the storm had passed. The sailors were safe, and Mara sat on the harbor wall, soaked through and shivering.

An old fisherman settled beside her. “Your father would’ve been proud.”

Mara looked back at the lighthouse. Its lamp still turned, steady and bright, though the morning had already begun.

“I think,” she said, “he knew the light was only half the job.”

The fisherman smiled. “And what’s the other half?”

Mara stood, reached for the empty lantern, and started up the stairs.

“Going out to meet them.”
