Every night at 11:17, the lighthouse blinked twice.

The people of Bellwether had learned to ignore it. Lighthouses blinked all the time, after all. But this one had been dark for twelve years, ever since the storm that took the keeper and his daughter.

Then, on the first night of winter, it blinked twice.

Mara saw it from her kitchen window. She set down her tea and ran.

The lighthouse door was locked, just as it had always been. But a warm line of light shone beneath it. Mara knocked.

“Go home,” called a voice from inside.

She knew that voice. She had heard it in her dreams for twelve years.

“Dad?”

The light under the door vanished.

Mara had been nine when the storm came. She remembered her father lifting her onto the kitchen table as water rushed through the cottage. She remembered his yellow raincoat, his hands on her shoulders.

“Stay here,” he’d said. “I’ll guide the boats in.”

The boats had made it. He hadn’t.

Mara knocked again. “I’m not nine anymore.”

For a long time, nothing happened. Then the lock clicked.

Her father stood on the other side, soaked and silver-haired, holding a lantern. He looked exactly as he had the night he left.

Mara could not move.

“I’m sorry,” he said.

She was suddenly nine again, angry and frightened and full of all the words she’d saved for him. “You’re sorry? You just—disappeared.”

“I know.”

“Everyone said you died.”

“I did.”

The lantern trembled in his hand. Behind him, the lighthouse stairs spiraled upward, and from somewhere above came the steady ticking of a clock.

Her father explained it quietly. The storm had torn a hole in the night, he said. He’d gone through it to keep the beacon lit. On the other side, time barely moved. Each year, he could return for one night—but only when the sea was calm enough, and only if someone came looking.

“I thought you’d come back sooner,” Mara said.

“So did I.”

She looked past him at the dark stairwell. “Can you stay?”

He didn’t answer.

Mara had become a lighthouse keeper herself. She’d spent years tending the lamp, though it had never once needed to shine. She’d told herself it was habit. Now she understood: she’d been keeping the door open.

Her father reached for her hand. “You don’t have to do that anymore.”

“No,” she said. “But I want to.”

Together, they climbed the stairs. At the top, the great lamp waited beneath its glass dome. Outside, the sea was black and still.

Her father showed her how to turn the old brass wheel. The beam swept across the water, bright as a road.

For the first time in twelve years, the lighthouse shone.

At dawn, her father was gone. But the lamp remained lit, and the clock had stopped ticking.

Mara stayed on as keeper. Every night, she sent the beam out over the sea—not because she expected anyone to return, but because somewhere, in the darkness, someone might be trying to find their way home.
