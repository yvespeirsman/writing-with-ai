### The Last Light in the Harbor

Every night, Mara climbed the lighthouse stairs and lit the lamp.

She had done it for forty years, ever since her father’s hands grew too shaky to strike a match. She had lit it through storms that shoved waves over the harbor wall, through summers when the sea lay flat as glass, and through winters when ice sealed the windows.

“Someone is always coming home,” her father used to say.

Mara believed him, though she couldn’t remember the last time a ship had needed the lighthouse. The harbor had electric buoys now. The fishing boats carried their own navigation screens. Even the gulls seemed to know which way to go.

Still, each evening, Mara climbed the stairs.

One November night, she reached the lantern room and found a boy sitting on the floor.

He couldn’t have been more than ten. His coat was soaked, and he held a small wooden boat in both hands.

“Are you lost?” Mara asked.

The boy shook his head. “My mother is.”

Mara looked out at the black sea. “Where is she?”

“Out there.” He pointed toward the horizon. “Her boat was supposed to be back before dark.”

Mara glanced at the clock. It was nearly midnight.

“What’s your name?”

“Eli.”

Mara crouched beside him. “How long has she been gone?”

“Since yesterday.”

For a moment, neither of them spoke. The lighthouse hummed around them, a great turning heart.

Then Mara stood. “We’ll give her a light.”

She lit the lamp. Its beam swept across the water, past the harbor mouth and over the dark waves. Once. Twice. Again.

Eli watched the light travel. “Do you think she’ll see it?”

“I think people can see a lot when they’re looking for home.”

They stayed until dawn. Mara made tea in the little kitchen below, and Eli fell asleep in a chair with the wooden boat tucked under his arm.

At first light, a bell rang from the harbor.

Mara ran to the window. A small fishing boat was coming through the mist, its engine coughing, its hull battered. A woman stood at the bow, waving both arms.

Eli woke at the sound and ran down the stairs so quickly Mara feared he’d tumble. She followed more slowly, one hand on the rail.

By the time they reached the harbor, the woman had stepped onto the dock. Eli crashed into her, and she wrapped both arms around him, laughing and crying at once.

“I saw the lighthouse,” she said. “I thought I’d missed the harbor. Then I saw it again.”

Mara looked back at the tower. The lamp was still turning, pale in the morning sun.

That evening, the harbor master came to visit. He carried a clipboard and a neat stack of papers.

“They’re shutting down the lighthouse,” he said. “The new system makes it unnecessary.”

Mara looked past him, toward the water. On the far side of the harbor, Eli and his mother were walking home together.

“Perhaps,” she said.

She signed the papers.

Then she climbed the stairs one last time and lit the lamp.

Not because the ships needed it.

Because sometimes, a person did.
