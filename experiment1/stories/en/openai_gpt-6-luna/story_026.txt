### The Lighthouse That Remembered

Every night, Mara climbed the lighthouse stairs and lit the lamp.

Every morning, she climbed them again and put it out.

She had done this for forty years, ever since her father’s hands had grown too unsteady to strike a match. The village below had changed: the bakery became a bookshop, the bookshop became a café, and the harbor filled with boats that could find their way home without any lighthouse at all.

Still, Mara lit the lamp.

“Habit,” she told people.

But the lighthouse knew better.

One winter evening, a storm rolled in so thick that the sea disappeared. The wind rattled the windows, and waves climbed the cliffs like dark, grasping fingers. Mara was halfway up the stairs when she heard a knock.

Not on the door below.

On the glass at the top.

She hurried upward. Beyond the lantern room’s windows, the storm churned—and in the middle of it, a small boat spun helplessly, its mast snapping in the wind.

Mara struck the lamp to life.

Its beam swept across the waves. Once. Twice. Then it caught the boat.

At that very moment, the lighthouse shuddered. A pane cracked, and the great lamp went dark.

Mara stared out into the storm. The boat was gone.

Then, through the wind, she heard a bell.

A tiny bell, ringing from the rocks below.

She grabbed her coat and ran down the stairs. On the shore, she found a boy clinging to a piece of wreckage. His red scarf was caught on a stone. He was shivering, but alive.

By dawn, the storm had passed. The boy was safe in the village, where everyone wanted to know how he had found the shore in such darkness.

“I saw a light,” he said.

Mara looked back at the lighthouse. Its lamp was still broken.

For the first time in forty years, she had no answer.

That night, she climbed the stairs anyway. She sat in the lantern room and listened to the sea.

Then, from somewhere inside the old stone walls, a soft golden glow appeared—warm as a candle, steady as a heartbeat.

Mara smiled.

The lighthouse remembered how to shine.
