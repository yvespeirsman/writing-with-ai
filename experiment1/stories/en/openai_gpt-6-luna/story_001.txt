### The Last Light on Bellweather Street

Every evening at 8:17, the streetlamp outside Mara’s apartment flickered twice and went dark.

The city had sent three electricians. They replaced the bulb, checked the wiring, and blamed the old brick buildings. Still, at 8:17, the light blinked twice and vanished.

Mara thought nothing of it until the night she came home in the rain and found a boy standing beneath the dead lamp.

He looked about ten, in a yellow coat too big for him. Water dripped from his sleeves, though the pavement around him was dry.

“Do you know what time it is?” he asked.

Mara glanced at her phone. “8:18.”

The boy sighed. “I’m late again.”

“For what?”

He pointed down the street, where the fog had gathered thick between the buildings. “My mother’s waiting.”

Mara followed his gaze. The street was empty.

“Where does she live?”

The boy frowned, as if the question were difficult. “Up there.” He pointed toward the dark windows of the old hotel at the corner. It had been boarded up for years.

Mara pulled her coat tighter. “You shouldn’t be out here alone.”

“I’m not.” He looked at her. His eyes were the pale gray of rainwater. “You are.”

Then he stepped into the fog.

Mara hurried after him, calling out. But by the time she reached the hotel, the boy was gone. On the door, beneath layers of dust, she saw a brass plaque.

**BELLWEATHER HOTEL — OPENED 1891**

And below it, scratched into the wood, a name:

**ELIAS VALE, 1912**

The next morning, Mara went to the library. She found an old newspaper story about a fire at the hotel. A family had been trapped on the third floor. The mother was rescued. Her son, Elias, was never found.

That evening, Mara waited beneath the streetlamp.

At 8:17, it flickered twice and went dark.

The boy appeared.

“You’re late,” Mara said.

He gave a small, surprised smile. “So are you.”

Together, they walked toward the hotel. This time, Mara saw the third-floor windows glowing warmly through the fog. A woman stood behind one, watching the street.

Elias ran ahead.

At the door, he turned back. “Thank you for walking with me.”

Then he went inside.

The light in the window went out.

The next evening, the streetlamp stayed lit past 8:17. It shone steadily over the empty sidewalk, bright enough to show the rain and, beneath it, the faint footprints of two people heading home.
