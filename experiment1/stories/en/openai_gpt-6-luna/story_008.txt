### The Last Light on Briar Island

Every evening, Mara climbed the lighthouse stairs to turn on the lamp.

She had done it for thirty years, ever since her father’s hands grew too stiff to work the old brass switch. The island had changed in that time. The fishing boats got bigger, the harbor got quieter, and the village shop closed. But the lighthouse remained, white against the cliffs, its beam sweeping the dark water in a patient circle.

Then, one October morning, a letter arrived from the mainland.

**The lighthouse will be decommissioned at the end of the month.**  
**Modern navigation systems have made it unnecessary.**

Mara read the letter twice. Then she folded it and placed it beneath the sugar jar.

That night, she climbed the stairs as usual. At the top, she opened the lantern room door and found someone sitting on the floor.

A boy, perhaps twelve, with soaked boots and a red scarf knotted around his neck.

“You’re not supposed to be here,” Mara said.

“I know.” He stood quickly. “I’m sorry. I saw the light from the shore.”

“What are you doing on the island?”

He looked toward the window. Far below, the sea pressed black against the rocks.

“My dad’s boat didn’t come home.”

Mara’s hand tightened on the railing.

“What’s his name?”

“Jonas Bell.”

She knew the boat: *Morning Star*. It had gone out before dawn, though the weather had been turning. Mara looked at the boy’s face and saw her own son there—the sharp chin, the hopeful eyes. Her son had left for the city fifteen years ago and sent fewer letters each year.

“Your father knows these waters,” she said.

“He knows the old way,” the boy replied. “The fog came in. He said the lighthouse would help him find the channel.”

Mara glanced at the letter under the sugar jar, though it was miles away in her kitchen. The lighthouse would be switched off in three weeks. For now, its lamp was still burning.

She turned the brass wheel to full power.

The beam swung out over the sea, brightening the fog in a long, pale blade. Mara fetched her radio and called the harbor master. Then she called the coastguard. The boy stood beside her, watching the water as if he could summon his father by staring hard enough.

They waited.

The lamp swept the dark. Once. Twice. Again.

Near midnight, a horn sounded from beyond the reef.

The boy ran to the window. “That’s him!”

A small green light appeared in the fog, vanished, then appeared again—closer this time. The *Morning Star* eased into the channel, guided by the lighthouse beam and the coastguard’s instructions.

When the boat reached the harbor, Jonas Bell climbed onto the pier and wrapped his son in both arms. The boy laughed and cried into his father’s coat.

Mara watched from the lighthouse window until the two figures disappeared up the path.

The next morning, she took the decommissioning letter from beneath the sugar jar. She carried it to the village council, where she found half a dozen people waiting with their own letters: one from a schoolteacher, one from a fisherman, one from the woman who ran the island’s last café.

They wanted to know what could be done.

Mara laid her letter on the table.

“First,” she said, “we make sure the light stays on.”

And that evening, when she climbed the lighthouse stairs, she did not go alone. The boy in the red scarf followed close behind, carrying a spare lamp bulb under one arm.

At the top, Mara showed him how to turn the brass switch.

Outside, the dark water waited.

Together, they turned on the light.
