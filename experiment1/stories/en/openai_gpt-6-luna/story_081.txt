### The Last Light

Every night, Mara climbed the hill to light the lighthouse.

The town had stopped sending ships out years ago. The harbor was silted, the old boats sat on their sides like stranded whales, and the lighthouse’s beam had nothing left to guide. Still, at sunset, Mara climbed the hill with a tin of oil and a box of matches.

“Who are you expecting?” the baker asked her once.

“No one,” Mara said.

This was true, though not the whole truth.

Her father had been a lighthouse keeper. When Mara was small, he taught her how to trim the wick and clean the glass. “A light isn’t only for people who are lost,” he told her. “Sometimes it’s for people who need to know where home is.”

Then, one winter night, he took a skiff into a storm and never came back.

Mara had kept the light burning ever since.

On the first night of spring, she reached the lighthouse and found a boy waiting at its door. He was soaked through, shivering, and clutching a canvas bag.

“Are you the keeper?” he asked.

“I suppose.”

“I saw the light from the cliffs.”

“There’s no path down the cliffs.”

“I know.” He looked toward the dark sea. “I was trying to find the town.”

Mara brought him inside, wrapped him in her father’s old wool blanket, and set a pot of tea on the stove. The boy’s name was Eli. He’d left the city that morning after an argument with his mother. He had meant to keep walking until he reached somewhere that didn’t know him.

“Did the light tell you where to go?” Mara asked.

“No,” Eli said. “It just made me think there might be somewhere to go.”

That night, Mara let him sleep in the keeper’s room. In the morning, she walked him down the hill and into town. His mother was waiting at the harbor, her coat unbuttoned, her face pale with worry. When she saw Eli, she ran.

Mara watched them hold each other.

That evening, she climbed the hill as usual. But when she opened the lighthouse door, she found the lamp’s glass broken. The storm had cracked it; the wick was damp, the oil spilled across the floor.

For the first time in years, the light would not burn.

Mara stood in the darkness, listening to the sea. Then she noticed a small glow beyond the window: lanterns moving along the road below. One by one, the townspeople were climbing the hill. The baker carried a new pane of glass. The blacksmith had brought oil. Eli and his mother came last, hand in hand.

They repaired the lamp together.

When the beam swept out across the water, Mara felt her father’s old words settle quietly inside her.

A light wasn’t only for people who were lost.

Sometimes it was for people who needed to know they weren’t alone.
