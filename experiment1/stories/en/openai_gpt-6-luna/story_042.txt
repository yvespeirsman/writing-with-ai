### The Lighthouse That Remembered

Every night, at exactly 11:17, the lighthouse turned its beam toward the empty sea.

Mara knew because she had lived beneath it for twenty years. She knew the click of the gears, the groan of the old stairs, and the way the light swept across the water—past the harbor, over the rocks, then out toward nowhere.

Her grandfather had built the lighthouse. He’d also told her, when she was little, that it was looking for someone.

“Who?” she’d asked.

“Someone who promised to come home,” he’d said.

Mara had always assumed it was a story. Then, one foggy November night, the light stopped turning.

She climbed the stairs with a lantern in one hand. At the top, the great lamp stood dark. Beside it lay a wet footprint.

Mara froze.

Another footprint appeared on the dusty floor. Then another, leading to the window.

Outside, the fog shifted. A small boat rocked beside the rocks, though no one had heard it arrive. A man stood in it, one hand gripping the mast.

Mara pressed her palms to the glass.

“Grandpa?” she whispered.

The man looked up. He was young—no older than she was—and his coat was the style in the old photographs downstairs. In one hand, he held a brass pocket watch.

The lighthouse shuddered. Its lamp flickered awake.

The beam swept across the sea and fell on the boat.

The young man smiled, though Mara couldn’t hear him through the glass. He lifted the watch, turned it over, and pointed to the name engraved on its back.

**ELIAN.**

Mara had seen that name on the lighthouse’s oldest plaque. Elian Vale, lost at sea in 1912. Her grandfather’s older brother.

The beam turned once more. The fog opened, revealing a strip of moonlit water leading toward the harbor.

The boat began to move.

Mara ran down the stairs and out into the night. By the time she reached the shore, the boat had vanished. The sea was empty, the fog unbroken.

But in the wet sand lay a brass pocket watch, ticking steadily.

When Mara opened it, she found a tiny folded note inside.

*Tell them I found the way home.*

The next night, at 11:17, the lighthouse turned its beam toward the harbor.
