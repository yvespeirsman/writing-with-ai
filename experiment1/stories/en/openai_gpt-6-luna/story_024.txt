### The Lost-and-Found

Every Thursday, at exactly 3:17, the station’s lost-and-found received something that hadn’t been lost yet.

On the first Thursday, it was a red mitten, still warm from someone’s hand. On the next, a paperback with a train ticket tucked inside, dated three days ahead. Then came a silver key, a child’s drawing of a house with smoke curling from its chimney, and one small glass bird with a crack through its wing.

Mara catalogued each thing and placed it on the shelf behind her desk.

“People lose things all the time,” her manager said when she asked what to do. “That’s why we have a lost-and-found.”

“But they haven’t lost them yet.”

He shrugged. “Then they’re ahead of schedule.”

Mara had worked at Bellwether Station for eleven years. She knew the rhythms of the place: the 8:12 commuter train, the announcement that always crackled halfway through, the way rain made the old roof smell like pennies. She knew which travelers hurried and which ones pretended not to be waiting for someone.

She also knew that at 3:17 each Thursday, the station clock gave one tiny shiver.

The glass bird arrived in November.

Mara was studying its broken wing when the doors swung open and a little girl hurried in, clutching her father’s hand. The girl stopped short at the desk.

“That’s mine,” she said.

Her father glanced at the bird. “No, sweetheart. We’ve never seen that before.”

The girl’s face crumpled. “I’m going to break it tomorrow.”

Mara looked from the child to the clock. It was 3:16.

“Why?” she asked gently.

The girl’s father answered before she could. “We’re moving. She’s upset.”

“I’m not upset,” the girl said. “I’m mad.”

The clock shivered.

The glass bird appeared in Mara’s hand.

The girl stared. Her father went pale.

Mara set the bird on the counter. “It looks like it’s already broken.”

“Not yet,” the girl whispered.

“Maybe,” Mara said, “you could decide what happens next.”

The girl reached for the bird, then stopped. “Can I fix it?”

“I don’t know,” said Mara. “But you can try.”

Her father knelt beside her. For a moment, neither spoke. Then he said, “We can take it with us. We can find glue.”

The girl nodded, carefully closing her fingers around the bird.

As they turned to leave, she looked back at Mara. “Do you get to keep the things that don’t belong to anyone?”

“Sometimes,” Mara said.

That night, after the last train had gone, Mara took the key from the shelf.

It was brass, worn smooth at the edges. She had known, somehow, from the moment it arrived, what it opened.

Her mother’s house had been empty for six months. Mara had inherited it after the funeral and had not once managed to unlock the front door. She’d carried the key in her coat pocket, but it had never fit. The one on the shelf did.

At the house, the key turned easily.

Inside, dust silvered the furniture. The hallway smelled faintly of oranges and woodsmoke. On the kitchen table sat an envelope, addressed in her mother’s careful handwriting.

Mara opened it.

*You always thought coming home meant going backward,* the letter began. *It doesn’t. Sometimes it’s just where you get the courage to start from.*

Mara read it twice. Then she opened the curtains.

The next Thursday, at 3:17, a blue scarf appeared on the desk.

Mara smiled and wrote in the ledger:

**Blue scarf. Owner not yet known. Handle with care.**
