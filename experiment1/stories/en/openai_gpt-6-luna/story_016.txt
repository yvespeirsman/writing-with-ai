### The Lighthouse That Remembered

Every night, Mara climbed the lighthouse stairs with a lantern in one hand and a cup of tea in the other.

The lantern was unnecessary. The lighthouse had been electric for decades. But her grandfather had carried one up those stairs, and his grandfather before him, and Mara liked the warm circle of light moving ahead of her.

At the top, she opened the glass door and stepped onto the narrow balcony. Below, the sea folded itself against the rocks. Beyond it lay a black horizon, wide enough to hold every place she had never been.

Mara had lived beside the lighthouse all her life. She knew the sound of rain on its windows, the groan of the wind in its ironwork, and the way the beam swept the water in a slow, patient circle.

She knew, too, that the light sometimes showed things that weren’t there.

A ship where no ship had sailed. A row of windows shining beneath the waves. Once, a white horse running across the sea, its mane streaming like foam.

Her grandfather had called these “the lighthouse’s memories.”

“Buildings remember,” he’d said. “People just don’t ask.”

Mara had laughed then. She was twelve, and the world seemed to her a straightforward place: land stayed put, ships stayed afloat, and memories belonged to people.

Now she was twenty-seven, and her grandfather was gone. She still climbed the stairs each night.

On the first evening of November, the beam caught a small boat just beyond the rocks.

Mara leaned over the railing. The boat drifted with its sail torn and its single oar trailing in the water. There was someone inside—small, hunched, and motionless.

She ran downstairs, called the harbor, and sprinted to the shore.

By the time help arrived, the boat had bumped gently against the sand. Its passenger was a girl about ten years old, wrapped in a yellow coat. She was cold but unharmed.

“Where did you come from?” Mara asked as they carried her toward the harbor station.

The girl looked back at the lighthouse.

“I don’t know,” she said. “I was following a light.”

The next day, the harbor master found the boat’s name painted beneath the peeling whitewash: *Ada Belle*. It had vanished during a storm nearly fifty years earlier, with a family of three aboard.

No one could explain how the girl had arrived in it. The girl could not remember her parents, or the storm, or anything before the light.

Mara took her home. The town took turns looking after her. They named her June, because that was the first month she could remember liking.

Years passed. June grew tall and quick and learned to climb the lighthouse stairs two at a time. She loved the sea, though she never wanted to sail on it.

On her eighteenth birthday, she found Mara on the balcony with the old lantern.

“Do you think it really remembered me?” June asked.

Mara watched the beam travel over the dark water. “Maybe.”

“And what does it remember now?”

For a moment, nothing appeared but waves.

Then the light reached the far edge of the bay, and a small, bright scene opened on the water: two girls running barefoot along the shore, one carrying a lantern, the other laughing so hard she could barely breathe.

June took Mara’s hand.

The picture faded. The sea became only sea again.

But the lighthouse kept turning, keeping its quiet watch over everything that had been lost—and everything that had found its way home.
