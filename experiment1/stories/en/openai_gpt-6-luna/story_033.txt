### The Lighthouse That Remembered

Every night, Mara climbed the lighthouse stairs with a lantern in one hand and a cup of tea in the other.

The lighthouse had been dark for twenty years. Its great lamp was cracked, its windows clouded with salt. But Mara climbed anyway, because her father had climbed, and his mother before him, and someone had to keep watch over the little town of Bellwether.

“Watch for what?” people asked.

“Whatever needs watching,” she said.

One November evening, as rain rattled the windows, Mara reached the top and found a stranger sitting beside the dead lamp. He was a boy, perhaps twelve years old, wearing a blue coat that dripped seawater onto the floor.

“You’re late,” he said.

“I didn’t know I was expected.”

The boy pointed through the glass. Far out on the black water, a small yellow light bobbed beneath the waves.

Mara set down her tea. “Is that a boat?”

“It’s a memory,” said the boy.

Before she could ask what he meant, the light vanished.

The boy stood. “My mother sent me to find the way home.”

“Where is home?”

He looked toward the sea. “I don’t remember.”

Mara studied his face. It was pale and solemn, but familiar in a way she couldn’t explain. Then she noticed a small brass compass pinned to his coat. Her father had owned one just like it. He’d disappeared at sea when Mara was very young.

“Come on,” she said.

Together they descended the stairs. At the base of the lighthouse, a narrow door opened onto the beach. The tide was low, revealing a path of wet stones stretching into the water.

Mara followed the boy.

The sea rose around their ankles, then their knees. The rain stopped. Above them, the clouds parted, revealing a sky scattered with stars.

“Your mother,” Mara said. “What does she look like?”

The boy hesitated. “She has your eyes.”

Mara stopped walking.

The boy kept going, turning back only when he reached the last stone. “You knew her,” he said. “When you were little. She used to sing while she mended nets.”

Mara remembered then: a woman with warm hands and a low, comforting voice, gone before Mara was old enough to understand that gone could mean forever.

The boy reached for her. “She’s waiting.”

Mara took his hand. It was cold as seawater.

A wave rolled toward them, silver in the starlight. On the far shore stood two figures: a woman with dark hair, and a man holding a brass compass.

Mara began to cry.

Her father raised one hand. He looked exactly as she remembered him from the old photograph—young, smiling, alive in the way people remain alive in the deepest parts of us.

The boy squeezed her fingers. “You don’t have to come.”

“I know,” Mara whispered.

She let go.

The boy ran across the water toward the waiting figures. They gathered him between them, and together they turned toward a light blooming beyond the horizon.

Then the sea drew back.

Mara stood alone on the beach, the hem of her coat dripping. Behind her, the lighthouse gave a sudden shudder. High above, the cracked lamp flickered once, twice, and came alive.

The next morning, the people of Bellwether woke to find its beam turning over the water for the first time in twenty years.

Mara climbed the stairs with a fresh cup of tea.

She kept watch.

But now she knew what for.
