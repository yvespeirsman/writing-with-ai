## The Lighthouse That Remembered

Every night, the lighthouse flashed three times toward the sea.

Every night, the sea flashed back.

Mara was the only person who noticed.

She lived alone in the old keeper’s house, where the windows rattled in the wind and the stairs groaned even when no one was on them. The village had moved inland years ago, after the harbor silted up. Now the lighthouse stood on a cliff above empty water, its beam sweeping over waves that had no ships to guide.

Still, Mara climbed the stairs every evening and lit the lamp.

“Habit,” she told anyone who asked.

No one asked.

Then, one stormy night, the sea answered with four flashes.

Mara froze beside the lantern. The storm hammered the glass. She watched the darkness between the waves.

Three flashes from the lighthouse.

A pause.

Four from the sea.

Her hand tightened on the railing.

The next night, she tried again. Three flashes.

Four returned.

On the third night, she flashed twice.

The sea flashed twice back.

Mara hardly slept. At dawn, she went down to the rocks, searching the shoreline for a signal lamp, a wreck, anything that might explain it. She found only a strip of blue cloth tangled in a crack between the stones.

It was the color of her brother Tom’s old scarf.

Tom had gone out fishing when they were children and never come home. Their father said the sea had taken him. Mara had believed it because there had been nothing else to believe.

That night she stood in the lantern room with the blue cloth in her pocket.

She flashed once.

The sea flashed once.

Then, with shaking hands, Mara flashed three times—the signal she and Tom had invented as children. It meant: *Are you there?*

For a long moment, the waves gave no answer.

Then the sea lit up—not with flashes, but with a pale glow beneath the surface. It spread outward in a wide circle, silver-blue, until the whole cliffside shimmered.

And there, far below, stood a boy on the water.

He looked exactly as he had the last time Mara saw him: wet hair, bare feet, one sleeve torn at the elbow.

She pressed both hands to the glass.

Tom raised his hand.

The lighthouse beam swept across him, and the glow faded. The water was dark again.

In the morning, Mara found the blue cloth on her bedside table, folded neatly. Beside it lay a small shell she had never seen before, smooth and white as bone.

That evening, she climbed the stairs and lit the lamp.

Three flashes toward the sea.

And somewhere in the dark, a familiar light answered.
