### The Last Light in Bellweather

Every evening at exactly six, the lighthouse on Bellweather Point flashed once across the bay.

It had been doing so for 142 years—through storms, wars, and one particularly stubborn seagull that nested in the lantern room for an entire summer. The keeper, Mr. Vale, never missed a night.

Then, on the first evening of November, the light went dark.

Mara noticed from the harbor. She was carrying a basket of mended nets, but she set it down and stared at the black tower.

“Perhaps he’s ill,” said the fishmonger.

“Perhaps the lamp has broken,” said the baker.

Mara knew it was neither. Mr. Vale had taught her how to trim the wick when she was little, before her family moved inland. He had told her the lighthouse didn’t shine for the boats already in the harbor.

“It shines for the ones who don’t know where home is,” he’d said.

So Mara took her coat and climbed the path.

The wind shoved at her all the way up. At the top, the lighthouse door stood open. Inside, she found Mr. Vale sitting beside the cold lamp, holding a small brass key in his hands.

“I’m too old to climb the stairs,” he admitted.

Mara looked up the narrow spiral. “Then I’ll climb them.”

At the top, she found the lantern glass clouded with salt. The lamp itself was fine. She wiped the glass clean with her sleeve, but the flame would not catch.

“Try the key,” Mr. Vale called from below.

Mara turned it in a little brass box beside the lamp. Inside lay a folded note, yellow with age.

She opened it carefully.

*For whoever keeps the light: if it ever goes out, remember that a lamp is only a promise. Someone still has to carry the flame.*

Mara looked at the old keeper’s oil lantern hanging from a hook. She lit it, then touched its flame to the wick.

The lighthouse came alive.

Its beam swept across the black water, past the rocks, past the sleeping boats, and out toward the open sea.

Far beyond the bay, a ship answered with three bright flashes.

Mr. Vale smiled when Mara came downstairs.

“Someone was lost?” she asked.

“Someone always is,” he said.

That night, Mara stayed at the lighthouse until dawn. And the next evening, at six, the light flashed again.

This time, she was the one who kept it.
