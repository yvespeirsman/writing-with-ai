### The Last Light in Bellweather

Every evening at seven, the lighthouse on Bellweather Point blinked once.

It had been dark for nearly twenty years. The town had replaced it with a row of red buoys, and gulls had claimed the railing. But every evening, just as the sun slipped behind the water, one pale flash appeared in the lighthouse window.

Mara saw it on her first night home.

“Probably a reflection,” her brother said.

“From what?”

He shrugged. “The sea.”

Mara had returned to Bellweather to sell their grandmother’s house. She had no intention of staying. She had a job in the city, a suitcase by the door, and a list of practical things to do.

But the next evening, she went to the lighthouse.

The door stuck, then gave way with a groan. Inside, the air smelled of salt and old rope. She climbed the spiral stairs to the lamp room, where the great lens stood cloudy with dust.

At seven, the light flashed.

Not from the lens—from a small brass lantern on the floor.

Mara picked it up. A note was tucked beneath its handle, in her grandmother’s careful handwriting.

*For whoever comes looking: the light is not for ships. It is for the ones who think they have missed their way home.*

Mara read it twice.

Then she heard footsteps on the stairs.

A boy appeared in the doorway, soaked to the knees, with a fishing net tangled around one boot. He couldn’t have been more than twelve.

“I saw the flash,” he said. “My dad’s boat hasn’t come in.”

Mara looked out at the dark water. Far beyond the buoys, a tiny white light rocked and vanished, rocked and vanished.

She lifted the lantern.

The wick caught with a soft, steady flame.

Together, they carried it to the window. Mara raised the lantern high, and the boy leaned beside her, calling his father’s name into the wind.

For a long time, there was only the sea.

Then, far out in the darkness, a horn answered.

The boy laughed, and Mara felt something loosen inside her—a knot she hadn’t known she’d been carrying.

The next morning, she called the city and asked for another week.

By the end of it, she had unpacked her suitcase. By the end of the month, the lighthouse door no longer stuck. And every evening at seven, when the lantern flashed across the water, someone in Bellweather would pause, look up, and remember that even the lost could still find their way home.
