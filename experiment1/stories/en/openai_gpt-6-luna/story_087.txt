### The Lighthouse Keeper’s Clock

Every night at midnight, the lighthouse clock stopped.

Mara had tried oiling it, winding it, even threatening it with a hammer. Nothing helped. The hands always froze at 12:00, and the lighthouse beam faltered for exactly one minute before sweeping across the dark sea again.

“Old clocks have their moods,” her grandfather used to say.

But her grandfather had been gone for seven years, and Mara had never believed in moods.

On the stormiest night of winter, the clock stopped as usual. The beam blinked out. In the sudden darkness, someone knocked at the lighthouse door.

Mara grabbed a lantern and opened it.

A boy stood on the steps, soaked through, holding a small wooden boat. He couldn’t have been more than ten. Behind him, the sea roared.

“Have you seen my father?” he asked.

Mara looked past him, but the steps were empty. No boat waited below. No footprints marked the rain.

“What’s your name?” she asked.

“Eli.”

The name struck her like a memory almost remembered.

Mara led him inside and wrapped him in a blanket. While he warmed himself by the stove, he told her his father had gone out fishing and promised to return before midnight.

“Is he often late?” Mara asked.

Eli shook his head. “He always comes back.”

The clock ticked once.

Mara stared at it. The hands had moved—not forward, but backward, to eleven minutes before midnight.

She hurried to the old logbook. Pages yellowed with age, filled in her grandfather’s handwriting. She flipped through the entries until she found the date of the great winter storm, the night her grandfather had rescued a fishing boat from the rocks.

A child had been aboard.

Eli.

Mara looked up. The boy was watching her, his small boat clutched tight.

“What happened to your father?” she whispered.

Eli’s face crumpled. “He told me not to be afraid.”

The clock began to move again.

Mara understood then. Her grandfather hadn’t rescued the father. The boat had gone down before the lighthouse beam could find it. He had brought Eli ashore—but the boy had not survived the cold.

For years, the clock had stopped at the minute the light failed.

Mara turned the brass key and wound it, though it was already tight.

“Your father kept his promise,” she said. “He came back for you.”

Outside, the storm quieted. A pale line of dawn opened along the horizon.

Eli smiled, and the little wooden boat in his hands grew warm with reflected light.

Then he was gone.

The clock struck midnight.

This time, it kept ticking.
