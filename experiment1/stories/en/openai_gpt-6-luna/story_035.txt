### The Lighthouse That Remembered

Every night, Mara climbed the hill to the old lighthouse and lit its lamp.

The sea had forgotten the way to shore—or so the villagers said. Ships passed in the dark, their windows glowing like lost stars, and never turned toward home. But Mara kept the light burning anyway.

“One day,” her grandfather had told her, “someone will need it.”

Then he’d sailed away and never returned.

Years went by. The lighthouse grew weather-beaten, the village grew quiet, and Mara grew old enough to doubt the stories she’d been told. Still, each evening, she climbed the hill.

One stormy night, as rain lashed the windows, Mara heard a knock at the lighthouse door.

On the step stood a boy in a soaked red coat. He held a small wooden boat in both hands.

“Are you the keeper?” he asked.

“I am.”

“My mother is out there,” he said, pointing toward the black sea. “She said she’d follow the light home.”

Mara looked at the lamp. Its flame had nearly gone out.

She hurried up the winding stairs, but the wind had cracked the glass. Rain swept in, snuffing the wick.

For the first time in forty years, the lighthouse went dark.

Mara turned to the boy. “I’m sorry.”

He shook his head. “My mother says lights can be made from lots of little things.”

He set his wooden boat on the floor. Inside it was a tiny candle.

Mara found a match. The candle caught, small and steady. Then she carried it to the great lamp and held it there.

Nothing happened.

The boy came beside her and cupped his hands around the flame. Mara did the same. Together, they shielded the little candle from the storm.

Its glow touched the lamp’s mirrored glass.

The whole lighthouse blazed alive.

Far out at sea, a ship answered with a horn.

By dawn, the boy’s mother was home.

And after that, whenever someone asked Mara why she still lit the lighthouse, she would smile and say, “Because sometimes the sea forgets. And sometimes, all it takes is one little light to help it remember.”
