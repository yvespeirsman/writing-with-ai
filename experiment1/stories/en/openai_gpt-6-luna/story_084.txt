### The Last Light in Bellweather

Every evening at six, the lighthouse on Bellweather Point flashed three times.

It had done so for a hundred years, guiding fishing boats past the rocks and home through the fog. Then the harbor emptied. The boats went elsewhere, the old keeper died, and the lighthouse was scheduled to be switched off for good.

Mara came to Bellweather the night before the shutdown. She had grown up there, though she hadn’t visited in twenty years. The town looked smaller than she remembered. The lighthouse looked exactly the same.

At six, its lamp swept over the sea.

Once. Twice. Three times.

Then it went dark.

Mara stood at the base of the tower, listening to the waves. She was about to leave when she saw a tiny light bobbing beyond the breakwater.

A boat.

It was too far out to see clearly, but she could make out its crooked mast and hear someone calling for help. The harbor radio crackled in the keeper’s empty room. No one answered.

Mara ran up the spiral stairs. At the top, she found the lamp cold and the old brass controls stiff with rust. She pulled a lever. Nothing happened.

Then she remembered something her father used to say: *A lighthouse isn’t lit by the lamp. It’s lit by the person who refuses to give up.*

Mara found a box of matches in the desk. The emergency oil lantern was still there, though its glass was dusty. She struck a match. It went out.

She tried again.

This time the flame caught.

The lantern was small, but when she held it to the great lens, light burst across the water—not a proper beam, not even a steady one. Just a trembling gold glow.

Out at sea, the boat turned.

Mara kept the lantern raised until her arms shook. She watched the boat inch toward the harbor, guided by that wavering light. When it finally slipped behind the breakwater, a bell rang from the town below.

By morning, the townspeople had gathered at the lighthouse. The boat belonged to a family returning from the next harbor. They had lost their engine in the fog.

No one mentioned the shutdown.

That evening, Mara climbed the tower again. She set the old lantern beside the dark lamp and watched the sun sink into the water.

At six, the lighthouse flashed three times.

It wasn’t the lamp. It was the whole town, standing on the shore with lanterns, candles, and phone lights raised high.

From the sea, Bellweather Point shone like a small constellation.

And far beyond the breakwater, a boat answered with one bright flash of its own.
