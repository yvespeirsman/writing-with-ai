At 3:17 every morning, the lighthouse blinked twice.

Mara knew because she was always awake to see it.

The first flash swept across the bay, silvering the waves. The second reached the cliffs, where the old railway tunnel disappeared into the rock. Then darkness returned, and Mara would lie in bed listening to the sea.

Her grandfather had built the lighthouse. He’d also told her, when she was little, that it wasn’t meant to guide ships.

“It’s for anyone who’s lost,” he’d said.

Mara thought he meant sailors. Then he’d died, and the lighthouse went dark.

For three years, she tried to fix it. She replaced the bulb, rewired the lamp, and cleaned the salt from every window. Nothing worked. At 3:17, she still woke. At 3:17, the lighthouse still blinked twice.

On the fourth winter, a storm swallowed the coast.

Mara woke to the two familiar flashes and saw something strange: a third light, far out on the water. It moved in sharp, frightened jerks.

A boat.

She ran to the lighthouse and climbed the spiral stairs barefoot. At the top, the lamp was cold. But beneath it, tucked into a drawer she’d searched a hundred times, lay a small brass key.

It fit a lock hidden behind the lamp.

When Mara turned it, the lighthouse shuddered awake.

Light burst across the bay—not white, but gold. It stretched over the waves in a shining road, leading straight to the cliffs. The boat followed it, rocking wildly, until it reached the little harbor below.

By dawn, a boy stood at her door, wrapped in a blanket. His fishing boat had lost its engine. He said he’d seen the lighthouse guide him home.

Mara looked past him toward the tower.

The lamp had gone dark again.

That night, at 3:17, it blinked twice.

Mara smiled. “I know,” she whispered. “You’re still working.”

And somewhere beyond the cliffs, someone who had been lost saw the light.
