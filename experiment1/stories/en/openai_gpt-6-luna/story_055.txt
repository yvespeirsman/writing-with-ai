### The Lighthouse Keeper’s Map

Every evening, Mara climbed the lighthouse stairs with a lantern in one hand and a cup of tea in the other. At the top, she lit the great lamp, watched its beam sweep across the dark water, and waited for the ships to answer with their own small lights.

She had kept the lighthouse for twelve years. In all that time, no ship had ever gone missing near the rocks.

Then, one winter night, the lamp went out.

Mara hurried downstairs. The wind rattled the windows, and rain tapped hard against the glass. She found the oil tank full, the wick dry, the mechanism sound. Nothing seemed broken. But when she climbed back to the lantern room, she saw a small blue light moving across the sea—far too close to the rocks.

She grabbed her coat and ran to the shore.

A boy stood on the beach beside a rowboat, holding a blue lantern. He looked no older than ten.

“Where did you come from?” Mara called.

“Over there,” he said, pointing toward the dark horizon.

There was nothing there but waves.

“You can’t be out here alone.”

“I’m looking for my mother.”

Mara glanced at the boat. It was old, with peeling paint and a name carved into its side: *Eleanor*.

She knew that name. It belonged to a fishing boat lost at sea twelve years earlier, the night Mara’s father had gone out and never returned.

The boy held out his lantern. “She said to follow the light home.”

Mara looked back at the lighthouse. Its lamp still sat dark above them. Then she understood: the boy wasn’t lost because the light had failed.

The light had failed because he had come.

She took his hand. “Come with me.”

Together they climbed the lighthouse stairs. At the top, the boy touched the cold glass of the lantern room, and the blue glow in his lantern brightened. The great lamp shuddered awake. Its beam swung out over the water, shining farther than Mara had ever seen it shine.

For a moment, a boat appeared beyond the rocks—a small, weather-worn vessel with a woman standing at its bow.

The boy ran to the window.

“Mother!”

The woman lifted one hand. Then the boat turned toward the open sea, and the darkness folded around it.

When Mara looked down, the boy was gone. Only the blue lantern remained, resting on the floor.

The next morning, she carried it to the shore and set it beside the old boathouse. She expected the tide to take it.

But every evening after that, when Mara lit the lighthouse lamp, a small blue light appeared far out on the water—answering her beam, guiding someone home.
