### The Last Light in Bellweather

Every evening at six, the lighthouse on Bellweather Point flashed once across the bay.

It had done so for nearly a hundred years. It flashed through storms and fog, through summer festivals and winter power cuts. Even after the harbor closed and the fishing boats stopped coming, the light kept its appointment.

Mara was the lighthouse keeper’s granddaughter. She had grown up on the point, learning which floorboards squeaked and how to tell rain from sea spray on the windows. When her grandfather died, the town offered to take over the lighthouse. Mara said no.

“It’s only a light,” people told her.

“It’s a promise,” she replied.

Then, one November night, the lamp went dark.

Mara climbed the spiral stairs with a toolbox in one hand and a lantern in the other. At the top, she found the bulb intact, the wiring unbroken. Outside, the sea pressed black against the rocks. She checked the generator, the switches, the old brass timer her grandfather had insisted on keeping.

Everything worked.

Except the light.

Mara leaned her forehead against the cold glass. Out in the bay, a tiny yellow glow bobbed on the water. A boat, too far from shore and heading toward the rocks.

She grabbed her lantern and ran down the stairs. The rain slapped her face as she reached the cliff path. She waved the lantern wildly, but the boat kept drifting.

Then the lighthouse roared behind her.

Its great lamp blazed to life, bright enough to turn the rain silver. The boat swung away from the rocks.

Mara stared up at the tower.

The timer’s bell rang once, though it was nowhere near six.

Years later, Mara would tell people the wiring must have corrected itself. She would say the generator had caught, or the bulb had finally warmed. But on the night the lighthouse woke, she had felt something pass through the stone beneath her hand—steady and warm, like a heartbeat.

And every evening at six, the light kept its promise.
