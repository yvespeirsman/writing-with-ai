### The Lighthouse Keeper’s Map

Every night, Mara lit the lighthouse at the edge of the island. Every morning, she climbed the spiral stairs and found the beam had drawn a new line across the sea.

The lines weren’t visible from shore. They appeared only on the old brass map in the lantern room, thin and bright as scratches of moonlight. Some curved around reefs. Others ended in the open water.

Mara’s father had kept the lighthouse before her. He’d told her the map showed where ships had been.

“Not where they’re going?” she’d asked.

He’d only smiled. “The sea doesn’t like to be told what it’s going to do.”

Then, one winter, his boat failed to return.

Mara took over the lighthouse and stopped looking at the map.

Years passed. The island’s harbor grew quiet. The fishing boats went farther south, chasing warmer waters. Then, one stormy night, the lamp flickered—and went dark.

Mara ran up the stairs with a matchbox in one hand. The wind battered the glass. Below, beyond the reefs, she heard a bell ringing.

A ship was out there.

She struck a match. It flared, then died. She struck another. Her hands shook so badly that the match snapped.

In the lantern’s dim glow, the brass map shimmered. A new line had appeared, running from the lighthouse straight into the storm. At its end was a small mark: a star.

Mara stared. She knew that mark. Her father used to scratch it beside the places he loved most—his favorite fishing cove, the cliff where he’d taught her to climb, the little beach where they’d watched the first meteor shower of summer.

The ship’s bell rang again.

Mara grabbed the oil can and poured the last of it into the lamp. She turned the wheel. The wick caught with a roar, and light swept across the water.

For a moment, the storm opened. In the beam, she saw a small boat pitching between the waves. At its stern stood an old man, one hand raised against the rain.

Her father.

Mara leaned against the glass. She wanted to shout, but the wind stole her voice. So she turned the lamp again and again, sending its bright answer out over the sea.

By dawn, the boat was gone.

On the brass map, the line had faded. The star remained. And beside it, in her father’s neat handwriting, were three words:

**You found your way.**

Mara touched the mark. Outside, the sea rolled on, keeping its secrets. But that night, when she lit the lighthouse, she watched the map closely.

A new line appeared.

This one began at the island and reached toward the shore.
