### The Lighthouse at the End of the World

Every night, Mara climbed the lighthouse stairs and lit the lamp.

She had done it for eleven years, ever since the sea took her father’s boat and left her the lighthouse, the cottage, and a brass key he’d worn around his neck. The key didn’t fit any lock she could find.

“Some things,” her father used to say, “are waiting for the right door.”

Mara thought that was just one of his sayings—like *never trust a gull with your lunch*—until the night the lighthouse went dark.

The lamp sputtered once, then vanished. Outside, fog rolled over the water, thick and white. From somewhere in it came the sound of a bell.

Mara grabbed her coat and hurried down to the shore. A ship was drifting toward the rocks, its sails hanging limp. She ran back to the lighthouse, but no matter how she turned the gears, the lamp stayed black.

Then she remembered the key.

At the base of the lighthouse, beneath a rug she’d never bothered to move, was a narrow iron door. The key fit.

Behind it lay a small room, and in the room sat a lantern no bigger than an apple. Its flame was blue and bright as a star.

Mara lifted it. The bell sounded again—closer now.

She carried the lantern to the top of the lighthouse. Its little flame grew brighter with every step. When she set it in the great lamp’s cradle, the whole tower blazed with blue light.

The ship turned away from the rocks.

As it passed the shore, a figure stood at the bow. Mara knew the shape of his shoulders, the tilt of his head.

Her father raised one hand.

Then the fog folded over the ship, and it was gone.

In the morning, Mara found the brass key warm in her palm. For the first time in eleven years, she understood what her father had meant.

Some doors didn’t lead somewhere.

Some doors led someone home.
