### The Lantern Keeper

Every evening, when the sun slipped behind the mountains, Mara lit the lanterns along the old harbor.

There were twelve of them, each mounted on a crooked post. The first stood beside the fish market, where gulls quarreled over scraps. The last leaned over the water at the end of the pier, its glass clouded with salt.

Mara lit them all, even though no ships had come to the harbor in years.

“Waste of oil,” the baker said.

“Waste of time,” said the mayor.

Mara only smiled and trimmed the wicks.

Her father had been the harbor’s last keeper. When she was small, he told her the lights weren’t for ships that knew where they were going.

“They’re for the ones that don’t,” he’d said.

Then, one winter night, his boat failed to return. After that, the harbor grew quiet, and people stopped watching the sea.

Mara kept lighting the lanterns.

One evening, a storm rolled over the mountains. Wind slammed shutters. Rain flooded the streets. The sea rose black and furious against the breakwater.

Mara hurried from lantern to lantern, one hand shielding her flame. By the time she reached the pier, her coat was soaked through and the wind had nearly knocked her off her feet.

She was reaching for the final wick when she saw a light far out on the water.

It blinked once. Then vanished.

Mara leaned over the railing. “Hello?” she shouted, though the storm swallowed her voice.

The light appeared again, closer this time.

A boat emerged from the rain, pitching hard between the waves. Its sail hung in tatters. At the bow stood a girl, gripping the rail with both hands.

Mara ran to the end of the pier and waved her lantern high.

“Here!” she cried. “Follow the light!”

The boat turned toward the harbor.

A wave lifted it—and dropped it beyond the breakwater. For one terrible moment, the girl disappeared behind the surge. Then the boat burst into the calmer water inside the harbor, battered but afloat.

By the time it reached the pier, the storm had begun to ease. The girl climbed onto the dock and collapsed at Mara’s feet.

When she could speak, she said, “I’ve been looking for this harbor.”

Mara helped her stand. “Why?”

The girl reached into her coat and drew out a brass compass, green with age. On its back was engraved a name.

Mara knew the name. It was her father’s.

“He gave this to my grandmother,” the girl said. “She told me that if I ever lost my way, I should look for the harbor where every lantern was lit.”

Mara stared at the compass. Her hands trembled.

“Did you know him?” she asked.

The girl nodded. “He brought my grandmother home, years ago. She said he was the kindest man she’d ever met.”

Behind them, the harbor lanterns shone one by one through the rain.

The next morning, the mayor declared that the harbor lights should stay lit every night. The baker offered to pay for the oil. The fishermen came down to repair the crooked posts.

And Mara, for the first time in many years, didn’t have to light the lanterns alone.

Somewhere beyond the mountains, the sea was still wide and dark. But now, whenever someone lost their way, there was a harbor waiting for them.
