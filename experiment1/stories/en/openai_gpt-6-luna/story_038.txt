### The Last Train to Bellwether

Every night at 11:17, a train passed through Bellwether.

It never stopped.

Mara knew because she had watched for it every night since she was seven, when her father first told her the story.

“Somewhere on that train,” he’d said, “is a place for everything you’ve lost.”

Back then, Mara had imagined a carriage full of missing mittens and broken toys. As she grew older, she imagined other things: her mother’s laugh, the dog that ran away, the version of herself who didn’t worry so much.

Then her father disappeared, and she began to wonder if the train had taken him.

On the night the old station clock stopped, Mara heard the whistle.

She ran downstairs, pulled on her coat, and reached the platform just as a silver train emerged from the fog. Its windows shone warm and gold. For the first time, it slowed.

The doors opened.

Inside stood her father, exactly as she remembered him: one hand in his pocket, a smile trying to look casual.

“You took your time,” Mara said.

“I know,” he replied. “I’m sorry.”

She wanted to ask where he’d been. She wanted to ask why he’d left. Instead, she climbed aboard.

The carriage was crowded with things she recognized: a red balloon, a chipped blue cup, a little girl’s missing mitten. At the far end, a window looked out over the town—only the town below was younger, and there was her mother, standing on the platform with a baby in her arms.

Mara’s breath caught.

“Is that me?” she whispered.

Her father nodded. “The first thing you ever lost was the sound of your own beginning.”

The train began to move.

Mara looked at him. “Can I stay?”

He took her hand. It felt warm. It felt real.

“You can,” he said. “But if you do, you’ll miss everything you haven’t lost yet.”

For a long moment, she stood between the open door and the golden carriage.

Then she stepped back onto the platform.

Her father squeezed her hand once before letting go.

The train slipped into the fog, carrying every unsaid thing with it. Mara watched until the last light vanished. Then, from somewhere far away, came one final whistle.

In the morning, the station clock started again. At 11:17 that night, Mara listened from her bedroom window.

No train came.

But for the first time, she could remember her mother’s laugh.
