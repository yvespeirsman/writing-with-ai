### The Last Light in Bellweather

Every evening at exactly seven, the lighthouse on Bellweather Point flashed once.

It had been doing so for nearly a hundred years, though no ships came close enough to need it anymore. The harbor had silted up, the fishing boats had moved south, and the town’s children believed the lighthouse was kept on for the ghosts.

Mara knew better. She was the keeper.

At seven each night, she climbed the spiral stairs and turned the brass switch. The lamp swept its bright eye across the dark water, over the empty harbor, and across the windows of the town.

Then, one November evening, it didn’t.

Mara climbed the stairs as usual, carrying her mug of tea. But at the top, she found the lamp already glowing—a warm, steady light, though the switch was off.

And beside it stood a boy.

He looked about ten, with a coat too thin for the cold and seawater dripping from his sleeves.

“You’re late,” he said.

Mara set down her tea. “Who are you?”

“Eli.”

“That doesn’t answer my question.”

He pointed out the window. Far beyond the harbor, a tiny boat rocked in the waves. A single lantern shone at its bow.

“I’ve been waiting for you,” Eli said.

Mara looked again at the boat. “Are you on it?”

“I was.”

The boy’s reflection appeared in the glass, though his face did not. Mara felt the chill then—not from the open door, but from the years between them.

She had heard of Eli. Everyone in Bellweather had. He had vanished at sea in 1932, during a storm that took three boats and left the lighthouse dark for a whole night. His mother had waited at the harbor until spring.

Mara turned to him. “You can’t stay here.”

“I know.” He nodded toward the boat. “But I couldn’t find the way back.”

The lamp pulsed. Out at sea, the lantern flickered.

Mara reached for the switch. “Then let’s show you.”

Together they watched the beam sweep across the water. Once. Twice. On the third pass, the distant lantern steadied.

Eli smiled. For a moment he looked less like a ghost and more like a boy who had finally spotted his house from the end of the street.

“Tell my mother I’m sorry I kept her waiting,” he said.

“She’s gone,” Mara replied gently.

“I know.” He looked out at the dark. “But maybe she’s there.”

The lighthouse flashed again.

When the beam returned, the boat was gone.

So was Eli.

The lamp clicked off by itself. Mara stood in the quiet, listening to the sea breathe against the rocks below.

The next morning, she found a small brass button on the floor. She tucked it into her pocket and went down the stairs.

That night, at seven, she climbed back up.

The lamp was dark. The switch waited beneath her hand.

For the first time in years, Mara wasn’t certain anyone needed the lighthouse.

Then, far out beyond the harbor, a single light appeared on the water.

She turned the switch.
