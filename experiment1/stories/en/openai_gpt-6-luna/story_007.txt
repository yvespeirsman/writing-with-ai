### The Lighthouse That Kept the Sea

Every night, Mara climbed the lighthouse stairs with a lantern in one hand and a cup of tea in the other.

The lantern was unnecessary. The lighthouse had been dark for twenty years, ever since the last shipwreck. But Mara liked the ritual: up the spiral steps, past the windows rattling in the wind, to the room at the top where the sea filled every pane.

She’d inherited the lighthouse from her grandfather, along with his pocket watch and a note that said:

*If you hear the bell, don’t answer it.*

There was no bell in the lighthouse.

On the first night of winter, Mara heard one.

A single, deep *clang* rolled over the water.

She set down her tea. Far below, the tide had drawn back farther than she’d ever seen, leaving the harbor boats tilted in the mud. Beyond them, the sea stood like a wall, black and glistening beneath the moon.

Then came the bell again.

Mara remembered her grandfather’s warning. She also remembered the fishermen who’d gone out in the storm that took the light—her grandfather among them. People said the sea had swallowed their boat whole.

The bell rang a third time.

Mara opened the lantern.

It was old and tarnished, but the wick caught at once. She turned the brass wheel, and a beam of gold swept across the water.

In the beam stood a small boat.

A boy sat at its bow, soaked to the skin. He raised one hand.

“Help!” he called.

Mara leaned toward the window. “Who are you?”

The boy looked past her, toward the dark shore. “Someone who hasn’t made it home yet.”

She understood then. The sea had not swallowed her grandfather’s boat. It had kept it—along with every lost thing, every unfinished goodbye—beneath its skin.

Mara turned the light toward the harbor.

The sea wall trembled. Water rushed forward, pouring back into the bay. The little boat rocked, then glided toward shore. As it passed the lighthouse, the boy lifted his hand again.

Behind him sat an old man with a silver watch chain.

Mara pressed her palm to the glass.

Her grandfather smiled.

By morning, the harbor was calm. The fishermen found an empty boat resting on the sand, dry as if it had never touched the sea.

Mara kept the lantern lit every night after that.

And though the bell never rang again, she always left two cups of tea by the window.
