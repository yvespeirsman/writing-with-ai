### The Last Light

Every night at nine, Mara climbed the lighthouse stairs and lit the lamp.

She had done it for forty years, ever since her father taught her how to trim the wick and polish the glass. The village had grown quiet around her. The fishing boats had become smaller, the harbor had become shallower, and most people had stopped looking up at the lighthouse at all.

Still, Mara lit the lamp.

One stormy evening, she found a paper boat on the doorstep. It was folded from a page torn out of a book, and inside, in careful handwriting, were the words:

**I’m lost. Is anyone there?**

Mara looked out at the black sea. No ships were visible. No lights answered from the horizon.

She took a pencil and wrote on the back:

**Yes. Keep looking for the light.**

Then she folded the page into a boat and set it in a rain barrel beneath the lighthouse window.

In the morning, it was gone.

The next night, another paper boat appeared.

**I can see your light. I’m coming home.**

Mara read it twice. Then she lit the lamp.

For seven nights, the boats came. Each carried a few more words.

**The waves are enormous.**

**I’m frightened.**

**I remember the way now.**

And every night, Mara answered.

On the eighth night, there was no boat. The storm had cleared, and the sea lay calm beneath a pale moon. Mara climbed to the lamp room and began her usual work.

Then she noticed a light moving far out on the water.

A small boat turned toward the harbor.

Mara leaned over the railing, one hand shading her eyes. As the boat drew nearer, she could make out a figure at the tiller, waving a lantern.

When it reached the dock, a young woman stepped onto the stones. She was soaked, shivering, and smiling.

“I thought the lighthouse had been abandoned,” she called up.

“It nearly was,” Mara said.

The young woman held up a paper boat. “I made these when I was a child. My grandfather used to answer them. I thought—well, I hoped someone might.”

Mara felt the wind tug at her coat.

“Your grandfather?” she asked.

The woman nodded. “He kept the lighthouse before you.”

For a moment, neither spoke. Then Mara looked up at the great lamp, turning slowly above them.

“I suppose,” she said, “some lights are never really put out.”

And that night, for the first time in years, the village windows began to glow.
