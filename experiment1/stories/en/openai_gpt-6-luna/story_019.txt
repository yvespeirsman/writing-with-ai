### The Lighthouse That Remembered

Every evening, Mara climbed the lighthouse stairs and lit the lamp.

She had done it for forty years, since the winter her father vanished at sea. The village said he’d been caught in a storm. Mara said nothing. She had been eleven then, old enough to remember the last thing he told her:

“If you ever get lost, look for the light.”

The lighthouse had been dark for three days when the storm came.

Wind battered the cliffs. Waves climbed the rocks. Mara reached the top with wet hair plastered to her cheeks and a matchbox in her pocket. She struck one match. It went out. She struck another.

A knock sounded beneath her feet.

Mara froze.

The knock came again—three slow taps from the door at the base of the lighthouse.

No one came to the lighthouse in a storm. No one came at all, usually. She descended carefully, one hand on the stone wall, and opened the door.

A boy stood outside, soaked through and shivering. He couldn’t have been more than twelve.

“I’m lost,” he said.

Mara stared at him. “Where’s your boat?”

“I don’t know.”

She stepped aside. “Come in.”

As she led him upstairs, the boy looked at the walls, where old brass plaques marked the names of ships lost at sea.

“Do you think they can see the light?” he asked.

“Who?”

“The people who are lost.”

Mara had no answer. At the top, she struck a final match. This one caught. The lamp bloomed gold, and its beam swept across the black water.

The boy watched the light pass over the waves.

“There,” he whispered.

Far below, a bell rang.

Mara ran to the window. A small fishing boat was turning toward shore, its lantern bobbing in the dark. On the deck, a figure lifted one hand.

The boy smiled.

Then the lighthouse door clicked shut below them.

Mara turned back. The room was empty except for the boy’s wet footprints, which led to the window and stopped.

She stood for a long time, watching the boat come home.

In the morning, she found something on the stairs: a brass button, green with age. She recognized it from the coat her father had worn the night he disappeared.

Mara closed her hand around it.

That evening, she lit the lamp again.

And for the first time in forty years, she believed the light could find its way both out to sea and back.
