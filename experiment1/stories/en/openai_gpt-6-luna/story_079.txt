### The Last Light in Bellwether

Every evening, just before sunset, Mara climbed the hill to light the old lighthouse.

The sea had swallowed the harbor years ago. It had taken the boats first, then the pier, then the crooked little shops where fishermen used to sell silver-scaled cod. Now waves rolled over the town square, and only the lighthouse remained above the water.

“Waste of oil,” people said in the inland village.

Mara kept lighting it.

One stormy night, as she reached the top of the hill, she saw a glow offshore: a tiny orange flicker, appearing and disappearing between the waves.

A boat.

She rushed up the lighthouse stairs, struck a match, and turned the great lamp toward the sea. Its beam swept over the water.

The flicker vanished.

Mara waited. Nothing.

Then, far out in the dark, a bell rang.

Once.

She turned the lamp again. The beam found a small boat, its mast broken, its bow pitching wildly. Someone stood at the stern, waving a lantern.

Mara ran outside and began ringing the lighthouse bell. The sound shuddered across the water.

The boat turned.

All night, she kept the lamp burning. By dawn, the vessel had reached the rocky shore below the hill. A woman and a small boy climbed out, soaked and trembling.

“We saw your light,” the woman said. “We thought everyone had forgotten this coast.”

Mara looked toward the flooded town, where rooftops gleamed in the morning sun.

“Not everyone,” she said.

Years later, when the sea had climbed higher and the village had moved inland, a new lighthouse was built on the next hill. Mara was too old to climb its stairs, so the boy she had helped rescue took over the lamp.

On his first evening, he asked her why she had kept the old one burning when no ships came.

Mara smiled. “You never know who’s looking for home.”
