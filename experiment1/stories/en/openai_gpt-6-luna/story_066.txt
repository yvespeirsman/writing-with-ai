### The Lighthouse Keeper’s Map

Every evening, Mara climbed the lighthouse stairs with a lantern in one hand and a cup of tea in the other. At the top, she lit the great lamp and watched its beam sweep across the sea.

She had done this for forty years.

“Ships don’t need maps anymore,” her brother used to say. “They have instruments.”

“Then why do they still look for the light?” Mara would ask.

Her brother never had an answer.

One winter night, a storm rolled in so quickly that the gulls barely had time to leave. Rain struck the lighthouse windows. Waves climbed the rocks below, swallowing the beach.

Mara lit the lamp.

At midnight, she saw something between the waves: a small boat, pitching wildly, its mast broken. She reached for the emergency radio, but the storm had drowned the signal in static.

Then she remembered the old map.

It was folded inside the lighthouse desk, drawn by her grandfather before the harbor was marked on any official chart. It showed a narrow passage through the rocks—a passage that appeared only at low tide.

But the tide was rising.

Mara grabbed a flare and ran down the stairs. At the bottom, she rang the great brass bell, once, twice, three times. The sound rolled out over the water.

Then she climbed back up and turned the lighthouse beam toward the rocks, holding it steady on the hidden passage.

For a long moment, nothing happened.

Then the boat’s lantern swung in the dark. It had seen her.

The little boat slipped between the rocks just as the waves rose behind it. Minutes later, it reached the harbor, where fishermen hauled its shivering crew onto shore.

At dawn, the storm passed. The sailors came to thank Mara. One of them, a young girl with salt tangled in her hair, looked up at the lighthouse.

“I thought nobody would see us,” she said.

Mara glanced at the lamp, still glowing in the pale morning.

“Someone always looks,” she said.

That evening, when she climbed the stairs, Mara found a new map on the desk. It was drawn in a child’s hand, showing the lighthouse, the harbor, and a little boat safely inside the rocks.

At the bottom, the girl had written:

**For the person who kept the light on.**
