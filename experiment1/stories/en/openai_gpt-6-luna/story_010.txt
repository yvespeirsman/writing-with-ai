### The Map of Small Things

Every evening, just before sunset, Mara drew a map of the town.

She mapped the bakery’s warm windows, the crooked fence behind the school, the bench where Mr. Vale fed crumbs to sparrows. She marked the alley where rainwater gathered and the house with the blue door that was always open.

Her maps were beautiful, but nobody bought them. People wanted maps that showed roads, train stations, and the quickest way to somewhere else.

“You should draw the highway,” her brother said. “That’s where everyone’s going.”

Mara kept drawing the town.

Then, one winter morning, a letter arrived from the city. The bakery would close. The school would merge with one across the river. Mr. Vale was moving in with his daughter. One by one, the places on Mara’s maps began to disappear.

That evening she spread her maps across the kitchen table. There were dozens of them, each one a record of a town that no longer existed exactly as it had.

She decided to make one last map—not of streets, but of memories.

At the bakery, she asked the baker what she’d miss most. “The way the whole street smelled like cinnamon at six in the morning,” he said.

At the school, the children told her about the oak tree they climbed at recess. Mr. Vale said he would miss the sparrows, though he suspected they’d manage without him.

Mara wrote it all down. She drew the smell of cinnamon as curling lines above the bakery roof. She dotted the oak with tiny children. She gave the sparrows a place on the map, each one no bigger than a comma.

When the map was finished, she pinned it in the empty bakery window.

People stopped to look. Then they began adding their own memories on little scraps of paper: a first kiss beside the river, a lost dog found in the square, the night the power went out and the whole town sang by candlelight.

Soon the window was covered.

Months later, Mara’s brother returned from the city. He stood before the crowded window, reading every note.

“You mapped all this?” he asked.

“Not all of it,” Mara said. “Just enough to help people find their way back.”

Her brother looked at the old streets, the vanished bakery, the tiny drawn sparrows.

Then he took out a pen and added something to the map: the road home.
