### The Last Light on Bellweather Island

Every night for forty years, Mara climbed the lighthouse stairs and lit the lamp.

She did it through storms that shook salt from the walls, through summers when the sea lay flat and silver, and through winters when the steps groaned beneath her boots. Ships still passed Bellweather Island, though fewer each year. The harbor had new lights now. The lighthouse, people said, was no longer necessary.

Mara lit it anyway.

On the last evening of October, a boy appeared at her door carrying a paper boat.

“Are you the lighthouse keeper?” he asked.

“I am.”

“My mother says the lighthouse is closing tomorrow.”

Mara glanced past him toward the harbor. One warm square of light glowed in the nearest town. “Your mother is probably right.”

“Then who’ll help the ships find the island?”

“They have maps.”

The boy frowned at the paper boat in his hands. “Maps don’t help when you’re lost.”

Mara had no answer for that. She let him follow her up the stairs.

At the top, the lamp waited behind its glass. The boy pressed his face to the window and watched the sea darken.

“Do you ever get lonely?” he asked.

“Sometimes.”

“What do you do?”

“I light the lamp.”

“That doesn’t make you less lonely.”

“No,” Mara said. “But it may help someone else.”

She turned the great lens. Its golden beam swept over the waves, across the rocks, and out into the gathering night. The boy watched it pass.

Then, far beyond the reef, a small light flashed once.

Mara went still.

It flashed again—weak, then gone.

“Is that a ship?” the boy whispered.

Mara seized the speaking trumpet and called down to the keeper’s cottage, where the radio sat beside the cold stove. The boy ran ahead. Together they sent the coast guard the coordinates, then returned to the window.

For an hour, the beam swept the water.

At last, a ship emerged from the dark, battered but upright, following the lighthouse’s light toward safe harbor. As it passed the island, someone on deck raised a lantern.

The boy lifted his paper boat. “They saw us.”

“Yes,” Mara said.

The next morning, a crew arrived to switch off the lamp. They found Mara packing her books and the boy sitting on the doorstep, folding another boat.

The crew took the lamp apart. They carried the lens down the stairs, carefully, one piece at a time.

But that evening, from the town across the water, a single bright point shone toward Bellweather Island.

Then another answered from the island’s dark tower.

And far out at sea, a ship turned home.
