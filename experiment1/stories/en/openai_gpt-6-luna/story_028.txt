### The Lighthouse That Remembered

Every night, Mara climbed the hill to light the old lighthouse.

The sea had pulled away years ago, leaving the tower stranded among fields of grass. Still, the town kept it standing. “A lighthouse is useful,” people said, though nobody could explain what it was useful for.

Mara knew. The light helped the lost find their way home.

Not ships. People.

One autumn evening, as she polished the great glass lens, she heard a knock from inside the tower.

Mara froze. The door was locked, and she was alone.

Another knock came—three soft taps from beneath the floor.

She lifted the rug and found a small brass hatch she had never noticed before. It opened onto a narrow staircase spiraling down into darkness.

At the bottom sat a boy with wet hair and a red scarf. He looked about twelve.

“Are you lost?” Mara asked.

“I think so,” he said. “I was looking for my sister.”

“What’s your name?”

He frowned. “I can’t remember.”

Mara led him upstairs. When the lighthouse lamp turned, its beam swept across the fields—and the boy suddenly gasped.

“I know that road,” he said, pointing toward the dark. “That’s where she went.”

He ran out before Mara could stop him.

The next morning, the town’s oldest resident came to the lighthouse. She stared at the red scarf draped over Mara’s chair and began to cry.

“My brother wore one just like that,” she whispered. “He disappeared in a storm when we were children.”

Mara looked toward the fields. Far away, two figures walked together through the mist: a young boy and an old woman, both laughing.

That night, Mara descended beneath the lighthouse. At the bottom of the stairs, she found a row of little brass hooks. One held a scrap of blue ribbon. Another, a wooden button. Each belonged to someone who had once been lost.

Mara understood then. The lighthouse did not guide people across the sea.

It guided memories home.

So every night, she polished the lens, turned the lamp, and watched its bright beam reach across the fields. And sometimes, when the wind came softly from the west, she heard footsteps on the stairs—and a knock from someone who had finally remembered the way.
