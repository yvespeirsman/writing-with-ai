### The Mapmaker’s Window

Every morning, Mara opened the little blue door of her shop and placed a bowl of water on the windowsill.

“Why water?” customers asked.

“For the maps,” she said.

They never understood, but they bought their maps and left.

Mara made maps of places that no longer existed: the old market where the fountain sang, the footpath through the orchard, the lane where the baker used to leave warm rolls on the windowsill. She drew each one carefully, in ink and watercolor, though no traveler could follow them anymore.

One rainy afternoon, a boy came in carrying a map so old it had split down the middle.

“Can you tell me where this goes?” he asked.

Mara studied the faded lines. They wound through hills, crossed a river, and ended at a tiny blue circle.

“I think it leads to the sea,” she said.

“My grandfather drew it,” the boy replied. “He said there was a lighthouse at the end. But the road’s gone.”

Mara looked out at the rain. Beyond the window, the town’s newer streets shone with puddles. The boy’s grandfather had been right: the road was gone. The hill it crossed had been cut away years ago to make room for a railway.

“Maps can’t bring roads back,” Mara said.

“No,” said the boy. “But maybe they can show where to start.”

So they set out together.

They crossed the railway on a footbridge, climbed a slope behind the station, and pushed through a tangle of blackberry vines. At the top, the land opened toward the sea. The lighthouse stood on a distant point, white against the darkening sky.

The boy smiled. “There it is.”

Mara reached into her coat for her pencil. “We should mark the way.”

But the boy shook his head. “You don’t have to. I’ll remember.”

On the walk back, rain tapped the leaves overhead. Mara thought of all the places she had drawn because she feared forgetting them. She thought of the bowl of water on her windowsill, which she replaced every morning.

When they returned to the shop, the boy ran home with his grandfather’s map tucked beneath his jacket.

Mara took down a fresh sheet of paper. For the first time in years, she drew a place that still existed: the station footbridge, the blackberry slope, the path to the lighthouse.

Then she opened the window.

The bowl of water caught a piece of the sky. In it, the clouds moved like ships.
