### The Last Train to Bellwether

Every night at 11:17, a train passed through Bellwether without stopping.

It had done so for as long as anyone could remember. The people in town set their clocks by its distant whistle, then went back to bed.

Except for Mara.

Mara worked the night shift at the station, though no passenger train had stopped there in twelve years. Her father had been the stationmaster before her, and his father before him. The job mostly involved sweeping the platform and keeping the station clock wound.

“Why do you bother?” people asked.

“Someone should be here,” Mara said.

One rainy Thursday, the train slowed.

Mara looked up from her book. Its windows glowed gold against the dark, and in each one she saw a different room: a kitchen crowded with laughing people, a nursery with a mobile turning slowly, a garden beneath summer sunlight.

The train stopped.

Its doors opened.

A boy stepped down onto the platform. He was about ten, with a red scarf tied around his neck. Mara recognized the scarf. She had knitted it herself, years ago, for her little brother, Eli.

Eli had disappeared when he was ten. He’d wandered down the tracks one winter afternoon and never come home. Their father had searched until dawn. Mara had searched every day since.

The boy looked exactly as Eli had looked that last morning.

“Mara?” he asked.

She couldn’t speak.

“I’ve been waiting,” he said. “You’re late.”

Behind him, the train’s golden windows shone. In one, Mara saw her mother, young again, standing at the kitchen sink. In another, her father sat at the station desk, smiling with the tired kindness she remembered.

“Where does it go?” Mara whispered.

“Somewhere you don’t have to miss anybody,” Eli said. “Come on.”

Mara glanced at the station clock. Its hands showed 11:17. They hadn’t moved in years.

She stepped toward him.

Then she noticed his shoes. They were dry, though rain ran in silver streams across the platform. And the red scarf—her scarf—was tied in the same crooked knot she’d made that morning, the knot Eli had complained about because it scratched his chin.

Mara stopped.

“You’ve been waiting all this time?” she asked.

“Yes.”

“But I was there, too.”

The boy’s face changed—not into something frightening, but into something very tired.

“I know,” he said.

Mara understood then. Not all at once, but like a room becoming visible when someone opens the curtains. The train had not brought Eli back. It had brought her the thing she’d wanted most: one more chance to say goodbye.

She looked at the golden windows. Her mother. Her father. Eli.

“I’m sorry,” she said. “I can’t go.”

Eli nodded. For a moment, he was ten again.

“I know.”

He climbed aboard. Before the doors closed, he held up the red scarf and grinned. Then the train pulled away, carrying its bright little rooms into the rain.

Mara stood alone on the platform.

At midnight, the station clock ticked forward.

The next morning, she took down the old timetable and called the railway office. There were still people in Bellwether who needed to travel, she told them. There were still places worth stopping for.

A month later, the first passenger train rolled into town.

Mara was there to meet it. And when the doors opened, she welcomed every stranger as if they had been expected for years.
