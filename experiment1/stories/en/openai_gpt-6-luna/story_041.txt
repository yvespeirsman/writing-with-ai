### The Last Light in Bellweather

Every evening, when the sun slipped behind the hills, the lighthouse at Bellweather blinked once.

It was not supposed to. The lighthouse had been dark for forty years, ever since the harbor filled with sand and the fishing boats stopped coming. But each night, just as the sky turned violet, a small golden light appeared in its highest window.

Mara saw it first from her bedroom.

“Someone’s up there,” she told her grandfather.

He looked toward the hill and went quiet.

The next evening, Mara climbed the winding path to the lighthouse. She brought a lantern, a sandwich, and the old brass key her grandfather kept in the kitchen drawer.

The key turned.

Inside, the lighthouse smelled of dust and salt. A narrow staircase curled upward into darkness. Mara climbed until she reached the lantern room—and found a little girl sitting beside the window.

She wore a yellow coat and held a candle.

“Who are you?” Mara asked.

The girl glanced up. “I’m waiting for my father.”

Mara looked out across the empty harbor. “Is he a sailor?”

“He was.” The girl touched the glass. “He promised he’d come home when he saw the light.”

Mara understood then. The girl was a ghost—or a memory, or perhaps something the sea had kept too long.

“What’s your name?” she asked.

“Elsie.”

Mara sat beside her. “How long have you been waiting?”

Elsie shrugged. “I don’t know. The nights all look the same.”

The next evening, Mara returned with a new candle. Then another. Soon she came every night, and Elsie told her stories about the old harbor: the fish that jumped into buckets, the gull that stole the mayor’s hat, the ships with bright red sails.

One night, Mara brought her grandfather.

He stood in the doorway, trembling. In his hands was a small wooden boat.

“Elsie,” he whispered.

The little girl turned. For the first time, she smiled.

Grandfather set the boat on the windowsill. It had been made by Elsie’s father, he explained—a gift from the last day they spent together.

“I’m sorry I couldn’t bring you home sooner,” he said.

Elsie touched his hand. “You came.”

Outside, beyond the harbor, the sea brightened with moonlight. A ship appeared on the horizon, its sails red as autumn leaves.

Elsie stood.

The candle flame leaned toward the open water.

She took one step, then another, and faded into the golden glow.

The ship vanished with her.

After that night, the lighthouse never blinked again. But Mara kept the lantern polished, and her grandfather placed the little wooden boat in the window.

Years later, when children asked why Bellweather’s lighthouse was dark, Mara told them, “It did its job.”

And if they stayed until sunset, they sometimes saw a red sail moving far out at sea.
