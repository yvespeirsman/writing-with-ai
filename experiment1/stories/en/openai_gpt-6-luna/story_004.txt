At 2:13 every morning, the lighthouse turned toward the town.

Mara knew because she lived in the keeper’s cottage, and because the beam swept through her bedroom window like a slow, searching finger. The lighthouse had been abandoned for forty years. Its lamp had no electricity. Still, each night, it turned.

On the first morning of winter, Mara climbed the spiral stairs with a toolbox and a flashlight. At the top, she found the old lamp glowing softly, though its glass was cold.

A little brass key lay beneath it.

She recognized the key at once. It had hung around her mother’s neck for as long as Mara could remember. After her mother died, it had vanished.

The key fit a small door in the lighthouse floor. Below was a narrow room containing a desk, a chair, and a stack of letters tied with blue ribbon. Every envelope was addressed to Mara.

She opened the first.

*My dearest girl,* her mother had written, *if the light has found you, then you’re old enough to know the truth. The lighthouse doesn’t guide ships to shore. It guides lost things home.*

Mara looked up. Through the round window, the beam swept across the dark sea.

Far out on the water, a tiny light blinked back.

Then came a knock from below.

Mara hurried down the stairs and opened the lighthouse door. On the wet stones stood a boy, shivering in a red coat. He looked about eight.

“Are you lost?” she asked.

He shook his head. “Not anymore,” he said, and held out a photograph of a woman Mara had never seen.

On the back, in familiar handwriting, were the words:

*For Mara, when she’s ready to find her sister.*

The lighthouse turned toward the town again.

And somewhere, in the dark, someone was coming home.
