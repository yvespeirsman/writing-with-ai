Every Thursday, the sea returned one thing it had stolen.

A button. A boot. A wedding ring green with salt. The objects appeared at dawn on the beach below Mara’s house, arranged in a neat line above the tide.

The villagers called it luck. Mara’s father called it rubbish and threw everything away.

Then, one Thursday, the sea returned a letter.

It lay inside a glass bottle, though the bottle was unbroken. Mara carried it home and held it up to the window. The paper inside was dry. Her name was written across the envelope in her mother’s slanted handwriting.

Mara had been six when her mother disappeared during a storm. Her father said the sea had taken her. He never said anything else.

That night, Mara waited until the house was quiet. She opened the letter.

*My darling girl,* it began. *If this has reached you, then the sea has finally learned how to let go.*

The letter told of a small island beyond the horizon, where Mara’s mother had washed ashore after the storm. She had lived there for years, watching ships pass too far away to see her signals. At the end, she had written: *I am not coming home. I only want you to know I thought of you every day.*

Mara read the letter twice. Then she went to the shore.

The tide was coming in. She stood where the water reached her shoes and spoke her mother’s name—not loudly, but clearly.

For a moment, nothing happened.

Then the sea drew back.

It revealed a narrow path of wet sand, stretching into the darkness. At its far end, a small figure waited, holding a lantern.

Mara took one step toward it.

Behind her, the cottage door opened. Her father stood on the porch, pale and shaking. He had followed her, carrying the old red scarf her mother had worn the day she vanished.

Mara looked at him. “You knew?”

“I hoped,” he said. “And I was afraid you’d go looking.”

The figure’s lantern bobbed beyond the waves. The path began to disappear.

Mara took her father’s hand.

Together, they watched the water rise and cover the road.

In the morning, the sea returned the red scarf. It was dry, folded neatly, and warm from someone else’s hands.
