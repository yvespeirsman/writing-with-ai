### The Lighthouse That Remembered

Every night, the lighthouse at the edge of Bellweather Bay swept its golden eye across the water.

It had done so for a hundred years, through storms and calm, through summers full of fishing boats and winters when the sea turned black. The lighthouse remembered every ship it had guided home.

Well, almost every ship.

There was one it could not forget.

On a foggy night long ago, a little boat had vanished beyond the rocks. The lighthouse had shone until dawn, but the boat never returned. Since then, whenever fog rolled in, the lighthouse turned its beam toward the empty horizon and searched.

Years passed. Its keeper grew old, and then he was gone. The town built a newer lighthouse farther up the coast. Bellweather’s old tower was left alone.

One autumn evening, a girl named Mara climbed its spiral stairs. She had run away from home—not forever, she told herself. Just until everyone stopped asking what she wanted to be when she grew up.

At the top, she found the lamp dark.

“You’re still looking,” she whispered, noticing the beam’s great glass lens pointed out to sea.

The lighthouse creaked in the wind.

Mara found a box of matches and a bottle of oil. She had never lit a lamp that large, but she had watched her grandfather do it a hundred times. Soon, a small flame flickered. The old machinery groaned awake, and the light began to turn.

Then the fog came.

Mara watched the beam sweep over the water. Once. Twice. On the third turn, something answered: a faint golden blink far beyond the rocks.

She ran down the stairs and out onto the shore, waving both arms.

A boat emerged from the fog, its sail torn and its bow battered. At the wheel stood an old woman, smiling as if she had been expected.

“I’ve been trying to get back here for years,” the woman called. “That light always knew the way.”

Mara helped pull the boat onto the sand. When she looked back, the lighthouse beam rested on the harbor, steady and bright.

In the morning, she went home.

She still didn’t know what she wanted to be. But she knew where to start: by asking the lighthouse keeper’s granddaughter how the lamp worked—and whether the old tower needed someone to look after it.

For the first time in a long while, the future didn’t feel like a question she had to answer all at once.

It felt like a light she could follow, one turn at a time.
